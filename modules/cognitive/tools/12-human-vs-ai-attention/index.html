Human Attention versus AI Attention

The word "attention" appears in two literatures that have almost nothing in common. In one it names a set of experimental findings about selection, capacity and awareness. In the other it names an arithmetic operation: a normalised similarity between one position in a sequence and every other position. This page has you do both, on the same sentence, so the difference is something you have produced rather than been told.

The numbers here are chosen by hand to be legible — no model was run, and nothing was learned

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to say precisely what a transformer attention weight is, and why a weight map is not an explanation of a model's output.

About 30 minutes. Nothing is timed. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

Before you start — the language problem

A weight is a number, not a noticing. Throughout this page, the computational side is described in arithmetic terms only. A high weight on a position does not mean the system "focused on" or "cared about" or "was drawn to" that word; it means a dot product came out large and the softmax normalised it upwards. The habit of saying otherwise is the single commonest error in writing about these models, and it is easier to avoid here than anywhere else because you can see the arithmetic.

Nothing on the machine side is aware of anything. There is no experience, no report, no effort and no cost of the kind the human literature measures. That is not a limitation of this demonstration — it is the finding.

    What this demonstrates

    What a transformer attention weight is, exactly

    For a chosen position, take its vector. Take the dot product of that vector with the vector of every position in the sequence, including itself. Divide each result by the square root of the vector length, divide again by a temperature, and push the whole list through a softmax so the numbers are non-negative and sum to one. Use those numbers as coefficients in a weighted sum of the value vectors. That is the entire operation. It is a normalised similarity between positions, computed for every pair at once, and nothing in it corresponds to noticing, ignoring, focusing or caring.

    Where the two genuinely rhyme

    Two respects, and they are worth stating precisely because overstating them is how the confusion starts.

    Both are forms of selection under a constraint. A person cannot fully process everything arriving at once, and their behaviour reflects a weighting of some inputs over others. A transformer layer cannot pass every position's representation forward unchanged either: it must produce one output vector per position, and it does so by weighting. Both are, formally, allocation problems. The constraints themselves are not the same kind of thing — one is a limit on a biological system operating in real time, the other is the shape of a matrix — but "everything cannot count equally" is true of both.

    Both are shaped by what came before. Human selection is influenced by expectation, by recent experience and by the task in hand. A transformer's weights depend on the preceding tokens, and under a causal mask depend on nothing else. In both cases the current selection is a function of context rather than of the current input alone.

    Where they diverge, and it is not close

    There is no experience on the machine side. The human literature is substantially about awareness: what gets reported, what does not, and the fact that those two come apart. Nothing in a softmax has a corresponding property, and there is no question the model could be asked whose answer would be a report of an experience.

    There is no bottleneck of the same kind. A transformer computes every pairwise score in parallel and then normalises. Nothing waits for anything. The human findings about serial stages, refractory periods and the cost of dividing attention describe a system with a temporal structure that this operation does not have.

    There is no cost of dividing it. Spreading a transformer's weights evenly across ten positions costs exactly what concentrating them on one costs: the same multiplications. Dividing human attention costs accuracy, speed, or both, and the size of that cost is one of the central measurements in the field.

    The weights are fully inspectable, and human attention is not. You have just read every weight to two decimal places. Nobody can read a person's selection off directly; it is inferred from responses, from errors, from eye position, from electrophysiology, and every one of those inferences is contested. This is the one respect in which the machine case is easier, and it produces its own trap, which is the next paragraph.

    Why a weight map is not an explanation

    It is tempting to treat a picture of the weights as a picture of the reason. It is not. The weights are one factor in one operation in one layer, of which there are many, each with many heads; the value vectors they multiply matter as much as the weights themselves; the residual stream carries information past the operation entirely; and the output depends on everything downstream. Empirically, patterns of weights can be altered substantially without altering the model's output, and identical outputs can be produced by very different weight patterns. Reading a heatmap as "why the model said that" is the computational equivalent of reading a single eye-movement record as the reason for a decision — and it is made worse by the fact that the heatmap is so much easier to look at than the alternative.

    Why the shared word survives anyway

    Not because anyone was confused. The operation was named by analogy to a family of models in which a system weights parts of an input differentially, and the name stuck because it is short. Technical vocabulary borrowed from psychology has form here — "memory", "learning" and "representation" all carry the same hazard. The remedy is not to police the word but to be able to say exactly what is being claimed whenever it appears.

    What this does not demonstrate

    • No model was run. The ten key vectors and the three heads were written by hand for this page and chosen so that the three presets would produce three legible pictures. In a real model these numbers are learned, nobody chooses what they mean, and most heads produce patterns that resist description altogether.
    • It is not a simulation of a transformer. It is one attention operation, in one head, over three dimensions and ten tokens, with no value vectors, no residual stream, no feed-forward layers and no second layer. Real models have dozens of layers and many heads each.
    • It says nothing about how people resolve pronouns. Stage 1 is a demonstration that you can do it and report it, not a measurement of how. That literature involves reading times, eye movements and manipulations this page does not contain.
    • Agreement between the two stages would mean very little. Two processes can produce the same output for unrelated reasons. Disagreement would mean little too.
    • Nothing here licenses claims about machine understanding, awareness or intent. Those are separate questions with their own literatures, and a weight vector is not evidence in any of them.
    • The word "attention" is doing no work in the comparison. If the operation had been called "weighted pooling", which is what it is, this page would be much shorter.
