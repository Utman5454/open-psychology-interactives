# Teaching notes — Dichotic Listening and Selection Theories

`modules/cognitive/tools/07-dichotic-listening-selection/`

"Did you notice anything?" and "what was it?" are two different questions. The
twenty-year argument about early and late selection is what happens when you
keep them apart.

---

## Before you run this

- **This is an analogue, not dichotic listening.** Two written columns are not
  two spoken messages at two ears. Reading is voluntary in a way listening is
  not, and eyes move. Say this out loud before anybody starts; the page says it
  three times, and students still need to hear it.
- **Do not describe the design before the first trial.** The tool deliberately
  does not say what is planted in the ignored stream, and the practice trial
  contains nothing and asks no questions. The first trial is the only naive
  observation anyone will get.
- **The tones are optional and synthesised.** No audio files, no speech, no
  network. They give real left–right separation with headphones and they carry
  the physical-salience manipulation, but they carry no words. They start
  switched off; pressing **Test the sound** switches them on for the trial as
  well as playing the three calibration tones, so a headphone check cannot
  leave you with a silent task. The menu above the button still turns them off
  again.
- **Every trial is followed by a full transcript** of both streams, which is
  the text alternative for both the visual display and the tones.

## Intended level

Second-year undergraduate, or a keen first-year cohort. It assumes the students
will meet Broadbent, Treisman and Deutsch and Deutsch in a lecture; the tool's
job is to give the argument something to be about.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. separate a physical judgement about an ignored channel from identification
   of its content;
2. say what a load manipulation does to breakthrough, and why;
3. state which findings each of the three accounts was built to handle;
4. explain why "they could not report it" is weak evidence for "it was not
   processed";
5. see why attenuation's flexibility is a problem as well as a strength.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 8 minutes — one naive trial run by the
  room, then the worked example.
- **Students running practice plus one set:** 12 minutes.
- **With a second set at the harder rule, the challenge and the debrief:**
  30 minutes.

## Preparation

Run one set yourself so you know how the task feels — in particular, how
strongly the marked item pulls the eye. Decide in advance whether you will let
the room run their naive trial simultaneously (best) or watch one volunteer.

## The prediction question

> What do you expect to be able to report about the ignored stream?

Four options. The intended answer is *whether it looked different at some
point, but not what it said*. The three others all get substantive feedback:

- *most of it* — people shadowing a message can report remarkably little about
  the other one, often not even what language it was in;
- *nothing at all* — too strong, and the exceptions are the interesting part;
- *only words that matter to me* — this is the attenuation prediction, and the
  feedback names it as such, then points out that a meaningless physical change
  gets through too.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

This is the five minutes that makes the lecture land.

1. Have everybody run **one trial** — no explanation of the design first.
2. Ask for two shows of hands: *who reported that something looked different?*
   and then *who got the word right?*
3. The gap between those two counts is the demonstration. Write both numbers on
   the board.
4. Now load the **worked example** and show the same gap across all four probe
   types and both rules.
5. Ask: *which of those two questions would an early filter be able to answer
   yes to?*

## Activity sequence

1. **Predict** what will be reportable from the ignored stream.
2. **One practice trial** — task only, no probe, no questions.
3. **A run of four trials** at the one-rule setting.
4. **Read the results**, starting with the naive-trial panel.
5. **A second run** at the two-rule setting; compare the rows.
6. **Open the attended-accuracy panel** and check the load manipulation
   actually loaded them.
7. **The challenge** — five findings, three accounts.
8. **Debrief.**

## Debrief questions

1. What exactly does "looked different" tell you that "which word was it" does
   not, and vice versa?
2. On a trial where you noticed a change but could not name the word — what had
   been processed, and what had not?
3. Why does making the attended task harder reduce breakthrough? What does that
   imply about the shape of the original question?
4. Someone says: "they couldn't report it, so it wasn't processed." What is
   wrong with that step?
5. Attenuation accounts for every finding in the challenge. Is that a point in
   its favour?
6. What would you have to change about this page to make it a real test of
   anything?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "Broadbent was proved wrong." | Superseded, and for good reasons, but the filter account explained the striking ignorance of the unattended message better than anything before it. Theories are replaced when something explains more, not when they are shown to be silly. |
| "Treisman's theory is the right one." | It is the most flexible one, which is not the same thing. A model that no result can embarrass is not being tested by any of them. The challenge feedback says this. |
| "So selection is late." | Sometimes. Under high attended load, breakthrough of meaning largely disappears. The productive move was to stop asking early-or-late and start asking under-what-conditions. |
| "I didn't notice anything, so nothing got in." | Failure to report is the weakest evidence there is. Priming studies find effects of unattended words people cannot report. |
| "The marked word proves meaning got through." | It proves a physical property got through. Whether its meaning did is the recognition question, which is a separate column for exactly this reason. |
| "This is dichotic listening." | It is a visual analogue with an optional tone layer. Written columns are not two ears. |

## The design, exactly as built

| Setting | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Trial length | 16 item pairs |
| Item duration | 800 ms (about 1.25 screen changes per second) |
| Probe position | pair 9, 10 or 11, chosen at random |
| Probe factors | marked or plain × relevant or unrelated |
| "Relevant" | an animal — the category being monitored on the attended side |
| "Marked" | larger, bolder, underlined; with audio, a different tone |
| A run | 4 trials, one per probe type, shuffled |
| Attended rule (one) | press for every animal |
| Attended rule (two) | press for every animal except birds |
| Targets per trial | 4 animals, plus 2 birds under the two-rule setting |
| Practice | 10 items, no probe, no questions |
| Recognition options | probe + 5 foils; always 2 animals and 4 non-animals |
| Tones | 300 Hz attended, 420 Hz ignored, 640 Hz square for a marked item |

The attended item at the probe position is always a non-target, so the attended
load at the moment the probe appears is the same on every trial. Recognition
foils come from pools disjoint from both streams, so a foil has never been on
screen.

## The worked example — reference values

A simulated class of **40 fictional participants**, one trial per cell, seed
**20260955**. Verified against the running tool:

| Planted word | Rule | Trials | Looked different | Word recognised |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Marked and relevant | One rule | 40 | 83% | 68% |
| Marked and relevant | Two rules | 40 | 80% | 40% |
| Marked, unrelated | One rule | 40 | 90% | 53% |
| Marked, unrelated | Two rules | 40 | 78% | 25% |
| Plain but relevant | One rule | 40 | 20% | 33% |
| Plain but relevant | Two rules | 40 | 8% | 15% |
| Plain and unrelated | One rule | 40 | 18% | 15% |
| Plain and unrelated | Two rules | 40 | 3% | 3% |

Generator probabilities: *looked different* .88 / .72 for marked items under
one and two rules, .17 / .10 for plain items; *word recognised* .62/.38,
.55/.30, .34/.14, .13/.06 for the four probe types.

**A deliberate simplification to point out to students:** the two questions are
drawn independently in the generator, so the plain-relevant cell shows
recognition (33%) above noticing (20%). In real data the two would be
correlated. That artefact is worth using — ask the class what it would take to
model them properly, and what the answer implies about the relationship between
noticing and identifying.

The whole pattern is **built in**. It illustrates the shape of the classic
findings; it is not evidence for them, not a norm, and not anybody's data.

## What the tool refuses to do

**It will not adjudicate between the theories.** All three survive everything
on this page, and the challenge is about which findings trouble which account,
not about which account wins.

**It will not treat trials after the first as naive.** They are reported in the
same table, but the naive panel is separate and says why.

**It will not claim to be dichotic listening.** Said in the hero, in the limits
panel, in the code comments and here.

## The challenge

| Finding | Answer | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Almost nothing reportable from the shadowed-away message | Late selection | If everything is identified, why is so little available? |
| Own name gets through | Early selection | A name is only a name once identified |
| A change of voice is noticed at once | Neither | Physical; all three accounts handle it |
| Unattended words prime related words | Early selection | Meaning must have been extracted |
| High attended load abolishes breakthrough | Late selection | Difficulty should not matter if everything is identified |

Attenuation deliberately does not appear as an option. The feedback explains
why: it absorbs all five, which is its strength and its problem.

## Limitations and cautions

- **Not dichotic listening**, and not a measure of anybody.
- **Only the first trial is naive.**
- **One observation per cell** from any individual — the class dataset exists
  because of this.
- **Not noticing is not not processing**, here as throughout this literature.
- **The load manipulation may not have loaded a given person**, which is why
  the attended-accuracy panel is there. Check it before believing a difference.
- **The tones carry no words** and are not a substitute for spoken messages.
- **The worked example is simulated**, with the pattern and one modelling
  artefact both built in.

## Accessibility considerations

- Items change about 1.25 times a second; only the two words change; nothing
  animates and no CSS transition is used.
- The attended side is marked by border weight, a filled label and a caret
  glyph — three carriers, none a hue. The marked probe is larger, bolder and
  underlined.
- Audio is off by default, opt-in, low-gain, with a test button; a full
  transcript of every trial follows it.
- The live stream area is `aria-hidden` — reading both sides aloud would
  destroy the selection the task is about.
- Responses by pointer or space bar; targets at least 3.25rem; no trial is
  timed out at the response end.
- The chart is paired with a five-column table; its series differ by fill, by
  hatch and by printed value.
- Every control has an accessible name; headings do not skip; focus moves to
  the results heading.
- Usable at 320px and at projector widths.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Design the load manipulation that is not confounded.** The two rules here
   also change what counts as a target. What would a cleaner one look like?
2. **Predict the priming result.** If you gave a word-recognition test twenty
   minutes later, which probe types would show an advantage, on each account?
3. **Pool the room.** Collect everyone's naive trial by probe type. How many
   people would you need before the marked/plain difference was worth
   believing?
4. **Argue against the analogue.** List three ways the visual version could
   produce the classic pattern for reasons that have nothing to do with
   auditory selection.
5. **Write the exam answer.** In 200 words, explain why "early or late?" turned
   out to be the wrong question.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Cherry (1953)** for the original shadowing work and the cocktail-party
  problem.
- **Broadbent (1958)** for the filter theory.
- **Moray (1959)** for the own-name effect in the unattended channel.
- **Treisman (1964)** for attenuation.
- **Deutsch and Deutsch (1963)**, and **Norman (1968)**, for late selection.
- **Lavie (1995, 2005)** for perceptual load, and for the reframing that made
  "early or late?" the wrong question.
- **Wood and Cowan (1995)** for a careful replication of the own-name effect,
  including how often it actually happens.
- **Driver (2001)** for a readable history of the whole argument.

Full references are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the tool does not
appear to derive any quantity from them.
