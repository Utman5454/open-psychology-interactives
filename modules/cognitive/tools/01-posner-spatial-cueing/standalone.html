What this browser cannot do

Your eye position is not measured. This page has no access to a camera or an eye tracker, and it cannot tell whether you kept fixation or simply looked at the cue. Asking you to hold fixation is an instruction, not a control. Every result here is compatible with covert orienting and with small eye movements you did not notice making.

Browser timing is approximate. Display refresh, keyboard latency and background processes add tens of milliseconds. Treat the numbers as illustrative.

First, a prediction

In the experiment below, a cue brightens one of two boxes and then a target appears in one of them. On valid trials the target appears in the cued box; on invalid trials it appears in the other one; on neutral trials both boxes are cued, so the cue points nowhere. The cue is correct on three out of every four cued trials, so it is worth using.

The experiment

Look at the central cross and keep looking at it. When the target — a filled disc — appears, say which box it is in: press Left arrow or Right arrow, or use the two buttons. No trial is timed out; the target waits for you.

Key terms
Covert orienting
Shifting attention to a place without moving your eyes. It is why the task asks you to keep looking at the central cross.
Cue-to-target interval
The gap between the cue appearing and the target appearing. Often written SOA, for stimulus onset asynchrony.
Neutral trial
A trial whose cue points nowhere in particular. It is the baseline the valid and invalid conditions are each measured against.
Benefit and cost
The two halves of a cueing effect, each measured from the neutral baseline: what a valid cue was worth, and what an invalid one cost.

What this demonstrates

Attention can move while the eyes do not

The cueing paradigm was designed to show that the place you are attending to and the place you are looking at can come apart. A cue you are told to ignore, at a location your eyes never visit, still changes how quickly you respond to what appears there. That dissociation is the reason the word covert is used at all: overt orienting is the eye movement, covert orienting is the shift of attention that can happen without one.

A neutral baseline is what makes the effect readable

Valid trials minus invalid trials gives one number, and that number hides two different things. Subtract the neutral baseline instead and you get two: a benefit (neutral minus valid) when attention is already in the right place, and a cost (invalid minus neutral) when it has to be disengaged from the wrong one. Studies that report only valid against invalid cannot tell you which of the two produced their effect, and the two have different theoretical consequences.

Cost is usually the larger half

In the published literature the cost of an invalid cue is typically bigger than the benefit of a valid one, which is part of why disengagement became a topic in its own right. Whether your own block shows that depends heavily on how many invalid trials you ran — eight is not many.

Endogenous and exogenous cues are not the same thing

A peripheral flash, like the one used here, pulls attention whether or not it is informative and does so within roughly a tenth of a second. A central symbolic cue — an arrow at fixation that you have to interpret — works more slowly and only when it is worth using. This tool uses a peripheral cue that is also predictive, so it mixes the two, which is a simplification made for the sake of getting a visible effect in a classroom block.

What this does not demonstrate

  • It does not show covert orienting. Nothing here measures eye position. If you moved your eyes to the cue, the result would look identical. Demonstrating covert attention requires an eye tracker or trials discarded on the basis of recorded eye movements — this page has neither.
  • It does not measure your attention. One block in a browser is not an assessment of anything about you: not attentional ability, not concentration, not distractibility, and nothing clinical or diagnostic.
  • The noise statements are approximate. They use the standard error of each difference, which assumes trials are independent. Fatigue and practice make trials within a block correlated, so read them as a rough guide.
  • Browser timing is imprecise. Display refresh, input latency and background load add tens of milliseconds of noise. Reported intervals are approximate targets, not achieved durations.
  • One block cannot settle a mechanism. Facilitation, disengagement cost, and accounts in which the cue changes response criteria rather than perception all predict faster valid trials. Separating them needs converging designs, not a larger version of this one.
  • The worked example is simulated. Its numbers come from a documented generator with a fixed seed. They are not a norm, a published effect size or anybody's data.
  • Cueing effects are context-dependent. Their size and even their direction change with the interval, the predictiveness of the cue, the task and the observer. At long intervals with uninformative peripheral cues, responses to the cued side can become slower — a separate phenomenon this tool does not attempt to produce.
