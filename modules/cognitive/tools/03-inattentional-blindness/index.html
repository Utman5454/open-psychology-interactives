Cognitive Psychology

Inattentional Blindness

A counting task that takes real effort, on a display that contains something you were not asked about. Whether you report it afterwards is the demonstration.

A classroom demonstration — not a test of anybody's attention

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to say why failing to report something is not the same as not seeing it, and what pushes noticing rates up or down.

About 20 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

This works exactly once

Do not read ahead. If you scroll down and read the debrief before doing the task, the demonstration is over for you — permanently, not just today. There is nothing on this page that is dangerous or unpleasant; the only reason for the warning is that a surprise cannot be repeated.

Running this for a group? Use the demonstrator route in the controls. It shows you the whole design without spending a variant, and the tool records which variants have been used in this browser session so you do not re-use one.

  1. Answer the short question below. It is about the counting task, and it unlocks the display.
  2. Read the counting instruction, then start. Count as accurately as you can.
  3. Say when you have finished, enter your count, and answer the question that follows.
  4. The same display is then shown again, in full, with everything labelled.
  5. Only then read the debrief.

What this demonstrates

Attention and awareness are related, not identical

An object can fall on your retina, be fixated, and still fail to be reported. What you are set to look for shapes what gets reported, and a task that demands close attention to one property narrows that set. The demonstration is about the relation between what is attended and what is available for report — not about whether the object reached the eye, which it plainly did.

Not reporting is not the same as not seeing

A person who says "no, there was nothing" may have processed the object without being able to report it, may have seen it and forgotten it by the time the question was asked, or may genuinely have had no experience of it at all. The three are very hard to tell apart, and the disagreement about which one inattentional blindness experiments demonstrate is a live one. Calling the phenomenon "blindness" builds in an answer that the method does not supply.

Noticing rates are a property of the design

Four things move them reliably: how demanding the primary task is; how similar the unexpected object is to what you are attending to; how salient it is; and whether you had any expectation that something might appear. Change any of those and the rate changes, which is why a rate from one demonstration describes that demonstration and nothing else.

Why this cannot measure you

A single trial gives a single binary response. There is no reliability to speak of, no norm, and no evidence that noticing on one occasion predicts noticing on another. Whether individual differences in noticing exist at all is contested, and it is certainly not something one display can settle. If you noticed the object, that is not a fact about your attention; if you missed it, neither is that.

What this does not demonstrate

  • It is not a test of your attention. One trial, one binary answer, no reliability, no norms. Nothing here measures attentional ability, distractibility, or anything clinical.
  • It does not show that the object was invisible. Failure to report is evidence about report, and the step from there to experience is exactly what is argued about.
  • A retrospective question is a memory test too. By the time you were asked, whatever happened had to survive until the question. Some accounts of these findings are about memory rather than perception.
  • The recognition options may prompt you. Being offered "a star" among the choices is a stronger cue than being asked an open question, and it makes a yes answer easier. Real studies ask open questions first for precisely this reason.
  • No noticing rates are reported. The tool has no data, and a rate invented to look plausible would be worse than none. Any figure quoted from the literature belongs to the study that produced it, not to this display.
  • A classroom result is not a finding. Twenty people in a room, self-selected, in a lecture, with whatever expectations the previous slide gave them, is not a sample of anything.
