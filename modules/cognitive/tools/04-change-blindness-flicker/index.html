Cognitive Psychology

Change Blindness

Two versions of the same scene, alternating. With a blank between them, a very large difference can take a surprisingly long time to find. Take the blank away and it leaps out.

Original vector scenes — a demonstration, not a measurement of anybody

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to explain what the blank interval does, say why the same change is trivial to spot without it, describe how change type, position and size affect search, and explain why change blindness does not show that people store nothing about a scene.

About 20 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

Flicker, safety and pacing

The alternation is slow and can be switched off. A full cycle takes about 1.7 seconds, so the screen changes well below the three-per-second threshold associated with photosensitive reactions. There is a Pause button throughout, and a Step manually mode in which nothing changes until you press a key or a button.

If your system asks for reduced motion, the tool starts in manual mode and says so. The phenomenon works perfectly well that way — it is the blank that matters, not the speed.

  1. Answer the prediction below — it unlocks the scenes.
  2. Pick a scene, start it, and search for the one thing that differs between the two versions.
  3. Press I can see it as soon as you have it, then say what changed.
  4. Run the same scene again with the blank switched off, and notice how long that takes.
  5. Each scene works properly only once. Four are provided, and the tool marks the ones you have used.

Notes for whoever is running this

The single most useful move is to run one scene with the blank on, let the room struggle, and then run the same scene with the blank off. The change that took forty seconds is now impossible to miss. Nothing about the picture changed; only the blank did.

Four scenes exist and each works properly once. The tool marks the ones already used, and it keeps that record across a reset.

Challenge — why does the blank matter?

Without the blank, the changing object produces a local flash of movement or brightness at exactly its own position, and that transient captures attention on its own. Inserting a blank replaces it with a transient covering the entire picture.

Which of these is the best account of why the blank makes the change so hard to find?

What this demonstrates

The transient is doing the work, not the picture

When something in view changes, the change itself produces a local signal — a flash, a flicker, a movement — at the place where it happened, and that signal pulls attention there without any searching. A blank screen wipes that advantage out by producing the same signal everywhere at once. The picture is identical either way. What changed is whether the change announces its own location.

Comparison needs attention

With the local transient gone, finding the difference becomes a deliberate search: hold part of the scene in mind, look again, and compare. That is slow, effortful and easy to do in the wrong place. Where the change is, how large it is, and whether it is somewhere you would naturally look all matter — which is why the trials in this tool differ on exactly those properties.

This does not show that nothing is stored

The strongest reading of change blindness — that people retain essentially nothing between glances — does not follow, and the evidence is against it. People remember a good deal about scenes they have looked at, including things they failed to report a change in. What the paradigm shows is that comparing two views requires attention to be in the right place at the right time; not that the views leave no trace.

Failing to report is not failing to see

As with the other awareness paradigms, the measurement is a report. Someone may hold information they cannot bring to a deliberate comparison, or may compare the wrong region confidently. A search that takes forty seconds is not forty seconds of blindness — it is forty seconds of looking in other places.

What this does not demonstrate

  • It does not show that people store no visual information. That inference is popular and wrong. The paradigm bears on comparison across views, not on how much is retained.
  • It measures nothing about you. A handful of self-paced trials in a browser is not an estimate of attention, observational skill or anything clinical.
  • Detection times here are not comparable to published ones. The alternation is slower than the standard paradigm, deliberately, and screen size and viewing distance are uncontrolled.
  • Four scenes is not a design. Change type, position and size are varied one scene at a time and confounded with each other. Nothing here can attribute a difference in time to any one of them.
  • A scene works once. After a reveal, running it again measures memory for where the change was.
  • Simplified scenes are easier than photographs. A vector street with a dozen objects has far less to search than a real image, so times here will be shorter than in the published paradigm.
