# Teaching notes - Attentional Blink

`modules/cognitive/tools/05-attentional-blink/`

**What it teaches.** Reporting a first target in a fast stream costs
accuracy on a second target that follows soon after, but the cost has a
specific shape: spared at the shortest gap, worst two or three items
later, recovered by the time eight items have passed. That shape, not
just the existence of a cost, is the finding, and several competing
accounts all predict it equally well.

**Before students start.** Four trials per lag is deliberately too few
to trust on its own. That is what the worked example is for.

**What students do.**
1. Predict the shape of the cost: steadily worse, only at the shortest
   gap, or a dip that recovers.
2. Run practice at a slowed rate, then a scored block, or load the
   worked example instead.
3. Report the digit, then say whether an X appeared, on every trial.
4. Read the lag-accuracy chart.
5. Sort five claims about what the pattern rules in or out.

**What to look for.** In the worked example, second-target accuracy is
spared at the shortest lag (86%), drops to its lowest two to three items
later (48%, then 41%), and recovers by the longest lag tested (88%).
That dip-and-recover shape, not a flat cost, is what to point at.

**Debrief.** The shortest lag is often as good as the longest. What does
that rule out about an account where the first target simply blocks
processing for a fixed stretch of time? Name two competing accounts of
the blink, and an experiment, not this one, that would separate them.

**Common misconception / caution.** "It shows doing two things at once is
hard" undersells the finding: a generic dual-task account predicts some
cost, but not this particular timing. Four trials per lag means a single
trial moves that point by 25 percentage points, so treat a student's own
block as a feel for the task and the worked example's larger sample as
the number worth trusting.

**Use in class / timing.** A front-of-room demonstration runs 5 minutes.
Practice plus one scored block takes 12 to 15 minutes. With the
challenge and debrief, about 25 minutes.
