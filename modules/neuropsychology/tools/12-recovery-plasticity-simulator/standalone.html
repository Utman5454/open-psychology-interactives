Recovery and Plasticity Simulator

Four different things all look like getting better. Watch five simulated trajectories fan out, then take one trained score apart.

Simulated - an invented model with a visible seed, not data, not a prognosis and not a prediction about anybody

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to name the processes that all raise a score after injury, and say why a trained score cannot separate them.

About 30 minutes, in two experiments. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

Numbers from a model, not from people

Every curve here is produced by an invented equation from a visible seed. None of it is data, none of it is a norm, and none of it forecasts what would happen to any real person. The model exists to make a logical point about what a score can and cannot distinguish.

Recovery is not guaranteed, is not proportional to effort, and does not continue indefinitely. Some of the simulated trajectories plateau and some drift downwards, because real ones do. Nothing here is a promise about outcome or a prognosis for anybody.

  1. Experiment 1. Set an injury severity and a rehabilitation intensity, and look at five people rather than one curve.
  2. Turn the measurement noise up and see how much of a "change" is not one.
  3. Experiment 2. Take one person's trained score apart, and compare it with a task they have never practised.

Key terms
Spontaneous recovery
Improvement in the period after an injury that happens without treatment, as acute effects subside.
Diaschisis
Reduced functioning in undamaged tissue connected to the injury. Its resolution is one of the things that lifts a score.
Restitution and compensation
Getting the original way of doing the task back, and reaching the same outcome by a different route. Both raise a trained score.
Measurement noise
Variation in a score between occasions that is not a change in the person.
Transfer
Whether an improvement also shows up on a task that was never practised.

Challenge - what would settle it

A fictional rehabilitation service reports that scores on its training task rise by an average of 22 points over six months, and concludes that the programme restores function.

What evidence would distinguish genuine functional recovery from an improved strategy for that task?

What this demonstrates

Four processes, one rising line

Restitution is the early, largely spontaneous return of function as oedema resolves, as remote depression of undamaged tissue lifts, and as tissue that was quiet but not destroyed comes back. Relearning is a slower change in the underlying ability, driven by practice. Compensation is doing the task another way - a different strategy, a different route, a diary, a colleague. Practice effects are simply the score rising because the same test has been given eleven times. All four raise the number, and only one of them is the thing most people mean by recovery.

The trained score cannot separate them

Load the two routes in experiment 2 and the trained scores land within a few points of each other while the untrained scores are far apart. That is the whole problem with reporting improvement on the task that was trained: the measurement is downstream of all four processes and cannot say which of them moved.

An untrained task is the cheapest discriminator

Compensation attaches to the task and the setting it was developed in; practice attaches to the specific test. Neither reaches a task the person has never done. Restitution and relearning change the underlying capacity, so they do. The gap between the trained and untrained curves is exactly compensation plus practice in this model, and asking for an untrained measure is far cheaper than most of the alternatives.

Five people, not one curve

Textbook recovery curves are smooth and rising. The five simulated people are not: some plateau, some drift down, and the noise is large enough that a difference between two adjacent months usually means nothing. Reporting a group mean as though it described an individual, or two time points as though they described a trend, are both mistakes this fan makes visible.

Plasticity is not unlimited

Move injury severity to its highest setting with everything else at maximum. The trained task still does not reach the starting level, and the untrained task falls much further short. The ceilings in this model shrink as severity rises, because that is the pattern the evidence shows. Compensation is often the most useful thing available, and saying so is not a lesser conclusion - it is a different one, with different implications for what to train and for what to expect.

Compensation is not failure

A person who has learned a reliable way of managing a task has gained something real, and the setting that supports them is part of the outcome. The problem is only ever the description: calling it restored function misstates what has changed, what will transfer, and what happens when the setting changes.

What this does not demonstrate

  • No published values. No trajectory, effect size or recovery rate from the literature is reproduced here.
  • Four processes is a simplification. Diaschisis resolving, oedema, reperfusion, denervation supersensitivity, axonal sprouting, map reorganisation, strategy substitution and behavioural compensation are not four things, and they overlap in time.
  • Smooth curves are wrong. Real trajectories include illness, medication changes, depression, seizures, surgery, a change of job and a change of family circumstances, none of which is in the model.
  • Rehabilitation is not a single number. Intensity, timing, content, specificity and how much the person wants to do it all matter and are collapsed into one slider here.
  • No anatomy. The tool names no region and no mechanism at the level of tissue. "Reorganisation" on a scan is not the same as recovered function and is entirely compatible with compensation.
