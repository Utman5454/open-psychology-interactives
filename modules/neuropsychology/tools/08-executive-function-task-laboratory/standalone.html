Executive Function Task Laboratory

Four executive tasks and six underlying capacities. Predict what each task needs, then take a capacity away and see which tasks notice.

Simulated — original task descriptions and a transparent model, not real testing

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to say why no executive task is pure, and why the lowest score does not name the weakest process.

About 30 minutes, in two experiments. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

A transparent model, not a measurement

Nobody performs these tasks. Each task is a set of weights on six capacities, and every score you see is a weighted average computed from sliders you set. The weights are an argument about what tasks require, written for teaching. No standardised test, subtest or item is reproduced, and no score means anything about the person reading it.

  1. Experiment 1. For each task, tick the processes you think it needs, then reveal the model's weights.
  2. Experiment 2. Reduce one capacity at a time and watch all four task scores.
  3. Then find two different reductions that make the same task the lowest.

What is the single most important thing wrong with that sentence?

What this demonstrates

There is no pure executive task

Every row of the weight matrix has at least five non-zero entries. That is not a limitation of this model; it is the central finding of the executive-function literature. Tasks named after a process load on that process and on several others, and the shared variance between executive tasks is routinely larger than the variance unique to any one of them.

One low score identifies nothing

Reduced inhibition makes the colour-word task the lowest of the four. So does reduced processing speed. Reduced updating makes the running-total task the lowest, and so does reduced sustained effort. The mapping from a low score to a reduced capacity is many-to-one, which means the inference from the first to the second needs something the score cannot supply.

Speed and effort are not extras

Both appear in all four tasks, so a reduction in either lowers everything. That is why a profile of executive scores so often looks uniformly low, and why processing speed has to be measured and partialled out before an executive interpretation is worth anything. Sustained effort is worse still: it varies within a session, so the same person tested at the start and the end of a two-hour assessment produces two different profiles.

A summary score hides the strategy

Move the strategy slider and the summary score for the timed tasks does not change at all, while accuracy and speed move in opposite directions. Two people with identical scores can be doing entirely different things - one slow and careful, one fast and error-prone - and the difference between them is often the more informative finding. Any assessment that reports one number per task has thrown it away.

"Executive function" is a description, not a mechanism

The evidence supports several partly separable capacities that also share a great deal, rather than one faculty or a set of independent modules. Whether the shared part is a single common factor, general processing speed, working-memory capacity or the overlap between tasks is argued about, and no arrangement of these four tasks would settle it.

What this does not demonstrate

  • The weights are an argument. They were chosen to make particular points about task demands. They are not measured loadings, they are not published values, and a reasonable person would argue with several of them.
  • Six capacities is a simplification. The literature separates several kinds of inhibition, distinguishes switching costs of different sorts, and disagrees about whether planning belongs on the list at all.
  • A weighted average is not how performance works. Real tasks have thresholds, floor and ceiling effects, strategy changes and interactions between capacities that a linear model cannot represent.
  • No anatomy. The tool names no brain region anywhere, and nothing in it supports a claim about one.
  • No assessment of anybody. The fictional person in the opening scenario has no diagnosis and no prognosis, and neither does anyone else on this page.
