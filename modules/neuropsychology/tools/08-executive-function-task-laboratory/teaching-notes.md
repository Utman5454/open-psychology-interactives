# Teaching notes — Executive Function Task Laboratory

`modules/neuropsychology/tools/08-executive-function-task-laboratory/`

A low score on an executive task is a fact about the task. Turning it into a
fact about a person's executive function needs evidence the score cannot
supply.

---

## Intended level

Second-year undergraduate neuropsychology or cognitive assessment. It follows
well from the Aphasia Profile Comparator, which makes the same argument about
language measures, and from the Lesion-Symptom Inference Trap, whose point
about over-reading a single finding this tool restates in a purely cognitive
setting. No clinical experience is assumed and no clinical materials are used.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. explain why every executive task requires several processes at once, and
   why that is a finding rather than a nuisance;
2. show that the mapping from "which task came out lowest" to "which capacity
   is reduced" is many-to-one;
3. say why processing speed and sustained effort have to be accounted for
   before any executive interpretation is worth anything;
4. describe what a single summary score hides about a speed–accuracy
   trade-off;
5. state why "executive function" names a set of task demands rather than a
   mechanism.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 10 minutes — the colour-word task in
  experiment 1, then the two presets in experiment 2.
- **Students in pairs:** 30 minutes.
- **With the challenge and debrief:** 45 minutes.

## Preparation

None. Read the "a transparent model, not a measurement" panel aloud before
starting. Nobody performs these tasks: every number is a weighted average of
slider positions, and the weights are an argument about what tasks require.
Students who arrive expecting a test will otherwise treat the output as data.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

**Experiment 1, the colour-word conflict task.** Ask the room which of the six
processes it needs, and take a show of hands for each. Almost everyone ticks
inhibition; a few add processing speed; hardly anyone adds sustained effort.
Then reveal: the model loads it on five of the six — inhibition 0.90,
processing speed 0.70, sustained effort 0.50, updating 0.15, switching 0.10,
planning not at all. Open **the whole weight matrix** and ask how many of the
four rows contain a single non-zero entry. None do, and none contains fewer
than five.

**Experiment 2, the two presets.** Load **reduced inhibition only**: the four
scores are 75, 88, 92, 88 and the colour-word task is the lowest. Then load
**reduced processing speed only**: 81, 93, 86, 96 — and the colour-word task
is the lowest again. The interpretation panel says so in words, because the
tool recomputes which single reductions share the current lowest task rather
than asserting it. Two different reductions, the same clinical-looking
finding.

Do the same pair with **reduced updating only** (96, 91, 76, 86) and **reduced
sustained effort only** (86, 88, 84, 91). Both make the running-total task
the lowest.

**The strategy slider.** With reduced inhibition loaded, drag the strategy
slider to *speed first*. The colour-word summary score stays at 75 while
accuracy falls to 63 and speed rises to 87. Drag it to *accuracy first* and
the two swap: 87 and 63, with the summary still at 75. Ask what a report
containing only the summary score would have said about those two people. The
same thing, twice.

**Everything mildly reduced** is worth thirty seconds at the end: every
capacity at 75 per cent gives 75 on all four tasks. A flat profile is very
common and identifies nothing at all.

## Prediction question

> A fictional person scores well below their control group on a colour-word
> conflict task, and within range on three other executive tasks. What does
> that identify?

Intended: *nothing on its own — several different reductions produce that
pattern*. The tool gives explanatory feedback for all four options, including
the natural "a difficulty holding back an automatic response", which is
treated as the reasonable reading that experiment 2 then undercuts.

## Activity sequence

1. **Commit to the opening judgement.** The laboratory stays hidden until an
   answer is recorded; there is a skip button for demonstrators.
2. **Experiment 1, all four tasks.** Tick before revealing each one. The
   button through to experiment 2 stays disabled until all four are revealed,
   which is deliberate: the point of experiment 2 depends on having seen the
   matrix.
3. **Experiment 2, one preset at a time.** Ask each pair to write down, before
   loading the next preset, which task they expect to be lowest.
4. **The many-to-one exercise.** Find two different single reductions that
   make the same task the lowest. There are two such pairs.
5. **The one exception.** Reduce planning alone. Only the disc puzzle moves
   much, and no other single reduction makes it the lowest. Ask why this one
   task is different — planning has a zero weight on two of the four tasks,
   which is as close to a pure measure as the matrix gets, and it is still
   not pure.
6. **The strategy slider**, as above.
7. **The challenge**: the fictional report sentence to correct.

## Debrief questions

1. Every row of the matrix has at least five non-zero weights. Is that a
   limitation of this model or a claim about executive tasks?
2. Reduced inhibition and reduced processing speed both make the colour-word
   task the lowest. What extra measurement would tell them apart, and what
   would it have to look like?
3. Processing speed and sustained effort appear in all four tasks. What does
   that do to the shape of a whole profile, and how would you notice?
4. Sustained effort changes within a session. What does that imply about
   testing someone at the start and at the end of a two-hour assessment?
5. Two people score 75 on the colour-word task, one with accuracy 87 and speed
   63, the other the reverse. Which difference would you rather know about?
6. If no task is pure, what is the point of naming tasks after processes?
7. The tool never mentions the frontal lobes. Why might that be deliberate?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "The colour-word task measures inhibition." | It loads on inhibition at 0.90 and on processing speed at 0.70 and sustained effort at 0.50. It is named after one process and requires four. |
| "A low score on a switching task means damage to switching." | Load the two presets that share a lowest task. The mapping is many-to-one, so the inference needs something the score cannot supply. |
| "Executive function lives in the frontal lobes." | The tool mentions no anatomy at all. Executive tasks are impaired by damage in many places, and frontal damage does not reliably impair them. |
| "Processing speed is a nuisance variable." | It is a weight in every row here. If it is not measured independently, an executive interpretation is one of at least two readings. |
| "A flat profile means a general problem." | It is consistent with that, and also with fatigue, effort, mood, medication, education or a testing session that ran too long. Load *everything mildly reduced* and ask what could have produced it. |
| "The summary score tells you how well they did." | Move the strategy slider. The summary score does not move at all while accuracy and speed reverse completely. |
| "The weights are the real loadings." | They are an argument, chosen for teaching. Students who disagree with one should say what the disagreement predicts. |
| "Then executive assessment is useless." | No — it is useful as a description of what a person finds hard on which tasks. What it does not support is a jump to one damaged faculty. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **Nobody performs the tasks.** Every score is a weighted average of slider
  positions. The tasks are described, not administered.
- **No proprietary material.** No standardised test, subtest, item or stimulus
  is reproduced. The four task descriptions are original prose, and the
  colour-word, rule-change, running-total and disc-moving procedures are
  described generically because the general procedures are common property.
- **The weights are invented.** They are not measured loadings and not
  published values. A reasonable person would argue with several of them.
- **Six capacities is a simplification.** The literature separates several
  kinds of inhibition, distinguishes switching costs of different sorts, and
  disagrees about whether planning belongs on the list at all.
- **A weighted average is not how performance works.** There are no
  thresholds, no floor or ceiling effects, no strategy changes and no
  interactions between capacities.
- **No anatomy, no diagnosis, no prognosis.** The fictional person in the
  opening scenario is a scenario, and nothing here is an assessment of the
  person using the tool.

## Accessibility considerations

- Native radio, checkbox, range and button controls throughout; the reveal and
  the challenge are form submissions.
- Both charts are `aria-hidden` and each carries a visible text equivalent
  inside its own figure, plus a full table further down the stage. The weight
  chart's line names every weight and every capacity the learner ticked; the
  score chart's line names every score and the lowest task.
- The learner's prediction is drawn as a **tick shape**, not a colour, and is
  repeated as "yes" or "no" in the table. The lowest task is named in words in
  the interpretation as well as filled differently.
- Each experiment has its own pinned primary and the inactive one is hidden,
  so only one `.stage__primary` is ever visible. Measured heights at 1366×768
  are 318px for experiment 1 and 249px for experiment 2, well inside the
  budget; with the last control in experiment 2 scrolled to the bottom of the
  window, the score chart is still at the top of the stage.
- The capacity sliders announce values in words ("Inhibition at 35 per cent of
  the typical level") and the strategy slider announces "leaning towards
  speed, 60 per cent".
- Every reveal, preset and stage change announces through the shell's polite
  live region. Focus moves to the laboratory heading when the prediction
  unlocks it.
- Wide tables scroll inside their own containers; heading levels do not skip;
  no horizontal page scroll at 320px, 375px, 1366px or 1440px.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Design the disambiguating test.** You have a person whose colour-word
   score is low. Specify one further task that would tell reduced inhibition
   from reduced processing speed, and say what its weights would have to be.
2. **Argue with a weight.** Pick one entry you think is wrong. What would the
   four scores look like under your version, and which preset would change?
3. **Add a fifth task.** Invent one, give it weights, and say which contrast
   it would sharpen. Then check whether you managed to give it fewer than
   five non-zero entries.
4. **The fatigue experiment.** Reduce sustained effort a little at a time and
   watch the whole profile fall together. Write the sentence a report should
   contain when a session has run long.
5. **Read the unity-and-diversity argument** and decide what the shared part of
   these four tasks would have to be for the model here to be a fair caricature
   of it.

## The model

Each task is a set of weights *w* on six capacities. Each capacity has a level
*c* between 0 and 1, where 1 is the typical level. The simulated score is

    score = 100 × Σ(w × c) / Σ(w)

so with every capacity at 1 every task scores 100 and any drop is attributable
to the sliders alone. Presets set one capacity to 0.35 and leave the rest at 1;
the sliders themselves run from 20 to 100 per cent in steps of 5.

### The weight matrix

| Task | Inhibition | Switching | Updating | Planning | Processing speed | Sustained effort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Colour-word conflict task | 0.90 | 0.10 | 0.15 | — | 0.70 | 0.50 |
| Rule-change sorting task | 0.50 | 0.90 | 0.40 | 0.20 | 0.30 | 0.50 |
| Running-total task | 0.30 | 0.10 | 0.90 | — | 0.50 | 0.60 |
| Disc-moving puzzle | 0.50 | 0.20 | 0.60 | 0.90 | 0.15 | 0.40 |

No row has fewer than five non-zero entries. Processing speed and sustained
effort appear in all four.

### Reference values a lecturer can check

Every value below is what the built tool prints, rounded as it rounds. One
capacity at 0.35, the other five at 1:

| Reduced capacity | Colour-word | Rule change | Running total | Disc puzzle | Lowest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Inhibition | 75 | 88 | 92 | 88 | Colour-word |
| Switching | 97 | 79 | 97 | 95 | Rule change |
| Updating | 96 | 91 | 76 | 86 | Running total |
| Planning | 100 | 95 | 100 | 79 | Disc puzzle |
| Processing speed | 81 | 93 | 86 | 96 | Colour-word |
| Sustained effort | 86 | 88 | 84 | 91 | Running total |

Two pairs of reductions share a lowest task: inhibition with processing speed
(colour-word), and updating with sustained effort (running total). The tool
computes this by re-running the model for each single reduction, so if you edit
the weights the interpretation follows.

*Everything mildly reduced* sets all six to 0.75 and gives 75 on all four
tasks.

### The strategy slider

For the two timed tasks the summary score is split into

    accuracy = score − 12s        speed = score + 12s

where *s* runs from −1 (accuracy first) to +1 (speed first). The summary score
is their mean and therefore never moves. With reduced inhibition loaded and the
slider fully to *speed first*, the colour-word row reads 75, 63, 87 and the
rule-change row reads 88, 76, 100; fully to *accuracy first* they read 75, 87,
63 and 88, 100, 76. On this scale 100 is the typical level rather than a
ceiling, so a person working fast and carelessly can pass 100 on the speed
component while falling below it on accuracy; the tool says so in a note under
the table.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Miyake, Friedman, Emerson, Witzki and Howerter (2000)**, and
  **Friedman and Miyake (2017)** for unity and diversity, and for the
  observation that executive tasks correlate substantially while remaining
  partly separable.
- **Burgess (1997)** and **Phillips (1997)** on task impurity: the difficulty
  of measuring one executive process with any task that a person can actually
  perform.
- **Salthouse (1996, 2005)** on processing speed as a common cause, and on how
  much of an "executive" difference survives partialling it out.
- **Stuss and Alexander (2007)** on fractionating frontal contributions rather
  than treating the frontal lobes as the seat of a single supervisor.
- **Alvarez and Emory (2006)** for a review of how weakly executive test
  performance localises to frontal damage.
- **Snyder, Miyake and Hankin (2015)** on the measurement problems that follow
  from interpreting single executive task scores.
- **Heitz (2014)** on the speed–accuracy trade-off, and why a single accuracy
  or latency figure is not a measure of ability.

Full references are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the tool does not
appear to derive its invented weights from any of them.
