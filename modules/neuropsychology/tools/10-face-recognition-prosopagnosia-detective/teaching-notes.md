# Teaching notes — Face Recognition Detective

`modules/neuropsychology/tools/10-face-recognition-prosopagnosia-detective/`

The second route is what makes the argument. Without the voice tasks, a
difficulty confined to faces and a difficulty with people in general produce
exactly the same face-task profile.

---

## Running it from the front

In experiment 1, the pair worth putting side by side is face familiarity and person identity knowledge. Both leave face perception intact. One spares the voice route completely and one does not, and that single difference is the whole argument for a face-specific stage.

Then general visual processing against face perception. The only task that separates them is the one about everyday objects. Students routinely skip it as irrelevant; it is the control task that makes the rest interpretable.

In experiment 2, profile E fits no single component and profile F fits two. Say nothing about either in advance. The habit worth breaking is the assumption that a profile always has an answer.

## Intended level

Second-year undergraduate neuropsychology or cognitive psychology. It assumes
nothing about anatomy and mentions none. It follows well from the Memory
Systems Detective, whose "this profile cannot decide it" move it reuses, and
from the Aphasia Profile Comparator.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. say what each of six components contributes to recognising a person;
2. read a profile of task outcomes backwards to the component that would
   explain it;
3. use the voice route to decide whether a difficulty is confined to faces;
4. recognise when a profile fits more than one component, or none;
5. list the explanations that come before any face-system account.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 10 minutes - face familiarity against
  person identity knowledge, then general vision against face perception.
- **Students in pairs:** 30 minutes for all six profiles.
- **With the challenge and debrief:** 45 minutes.

## Preparation

None. Read the "this is not a test, and not a diagnosis" panel aloud. There is
no self-test on the page and no score for the reader, and it is worth saying
so before anyone starts wondering about themselves. Say also that the faces
are drawn from seven numbers and resemble nobody; open the faces panel and
press *Draw four more* if that needs demonstrating.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

**Face familiarity, then person identity knowledge.** Both leave face
perception intact, so on the face tasks alone they look similar - identity from
the face and the name from the face fail in both. The difference is entirely
in the voice route: familiarity spares it, identity knowledge does not. Ask the
room what a report containing only face tasks could have said. Nothing that
distinguishes them.

**General vision, then face perception.** Now the profiles are identical on
every single face task, and on the voice tasks too. The only cell that differs
is the object-discrimination task. Ask which task students would have dropped
if the session had run short. It is always that one.

**Then profile F in experiment 2**, which is that session. The object task was
not administered, and two components fit exactly. The tool says so and names
both.

**Finish with profile E.** Every task below range, including the voice route.
No component fits. Let the room try three or four before telling them. The
reflex to find an answer is the thing worth catching.

## Prediction question

> A fictional person no longer recognises colleagues by sight. They can tell
> any two unfamiliar faces apart without difficulty, and they know exactly who
> a colleague is the moment that colleague speaks. Where is the difficulty
> most likely to be?

Intended: *after face perception and before knowledge of the person*. Each of
the three wrong answers is ruled out by a specific finding in the scenario, and
the feedback names which.

## Activity sequence

1. **Commit to the opening judgement.** The model stays hidden until an answer
   is recorded; there is a skip button for demonstrators.
2. **Experiment 1, all six components.** The goal has three checks: a component
   that ruins the face route and spares the voice route, one that ruins both,
   and one that affects non-face objects too. The button through to experiment
   2 stays disabled until all three are met, which takes three well-chosen
   visits.
3. **Ask each pair to state the rule** before moving on: a task is impaired
   exactly when the affected component is one of the components it needs.
4. **Experiment 2, profiles A to D**, which each have one answer.
5. **Profile E**, which has none, and **profile F**, which has two.
6. **The alternatives.** After every check the tool lists what has to be ruled
   out before the component account is worth anything. Ask each pair to pick
   the one they would test first and say how.
7. **The challenge** on the matched-difficulty problem.

## Debrief questions

1. Which single task does the most work in this whole model, and why?
2. Face familiarity and person identity knowledge produce the same face-task
   profile. What does that say about assessments built only from face tasks?
3. Why is an object-matching task not automatically a fair control for a
   face-matching task?
4. Profile E is below range on everything. Name three explanations that come
   before any component, and say which is easiest to check.
5. Profile D is by far the commonest experience on the list. Why is it also the
   least informative?
6. The tool never names a brain region. What would naming one add, and what
   would it risk?
7. Two fictional people could receive the same label and have profiles B and C.
   What has the label discarded?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "Faces impaired, objects intact - so it is face-specific." | Only if the two tasks were equally hard. Faces are far more similar to one another than teapots are, so a general perceptual difficulty shows on the face task first. |
| "If they cannot name the face, face recognition is impaired." | Profile D names nobody and recognises everybody. Naming is the last step and the least specific. |
| "A voice task is a nice extra." | It is the task that decides whether the difficulty is about faces at all. Compare profiles B and C. |
| "Everything is a bit low, so it must be mild." | Profile E fits no component. Effort, fatigue, low-level vision, mood and general slowing all come first. |
| "Prosopagnosia is one condition." | Four of the six profiles here would attract the word and they are four different patterns. Real difficulties differ in component, severity, and whether they were acquired or lifelong. |
| "They recognise their family, so nothing is wrong." | Recognition is supported by hair, gait, voice, context and clothing. Performing well in life and performing well on a controlled task are different measurements. |
| "The missing task does not matter, the rest is clear." | It is the only task that separates two accounts. Profile F exists to make that concrete. |
| "This tells me whether I have it." | It cannot, and it does not try. Identifying a real difficulty needs formal assessment by a qualified clinician. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **No assessment of anybody.** No self-test, no score for the reader, no
  diagnosis. This is worth restating at the end as well as the beginning,
  because students frequently recognise themselves in profile D.
- **Six components is a simplification.** The literature separates more
  stages, argues about whether familiarity is a stage or a signal, and
  disagrees about whether the face system is truly face-specific or the
  extreme end of expertise with within-category discrimination.
- **All-or-nothing components are unrealistic.** Real difficulties are graded,
  vary from day to day, and improve with familiarity, context and time.
- **The profiles are invented.** No published case is reproduced and no
  clinical materials are used.
- **No anatomy.** These components do not map neatly onto single areas, and
  the same profile arises from damage in several places and from developmental
  differences with no lesion at all.
- **Describe tasks, not people.** The tool never says a person is a condition.

## Accessibility considerations

- Native radio, select and button controls; every control has an accessible
  name (verified programmatically).
- The flow diagram is `aria-hidden` and carries a visible sentence inside its
  figure naming the affected component and what it does to each route. The
  affected box is crossed through **and** labelled "affected" as well as being
  filled differently.
- The seven outcomes print their own words - "within range", "impaired", "not
  administered" - so the left border is never the only carrier. A full table
  gives each task, what it asks for, the components it needs and its outcome.
- Each experiment has its own pinned primary and the inactive one is hidden.
  Measured at 1366×768 the pins are 517px and 246px; with the last control of
  either experiment scrolled to the bottom of the window the primary is still
  on screen.
- The schematic faces carry no information the model uses, so they are
  `aria-hidden` with their explanation in adjacent text.
- The goal checklist writes "(met)" or "(not yet)" into the text.
- Every change announces through the polite live region; focus moves to the
  model heading when the prediction unlocks it.
- No horizontal page scroll at 320px, 375px, 1366px, 1440px or 1920px.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Add a task.** Where would "decide whether two photographs of the same
   person taken years apart show the same person" sit, and which profiles
   would it change?
2. **Add a component.** Some accounts separate a stored face description from
   the sense of familiarity. Add it, decide which tasks need it, and see
   whether any of the six profiles becomes ambiguous.
3. **Rescue profile F.** Write the one extra task you would run, and say what
   each possible outcome would license.
4. **Design the matched control.** Specify a non-face task as hard as the face
   task, and say how you would establish that it is.
5. **Write the report.** In 150 words, describe profile B without naming a
   syndrome and without naming a brain region.

## The model

Two routes converge:

    a face  -> general vision -> face perception -> face familiarity --+
                                                                       |
    a voice -> voice recognition ------------------------------------->+
                                                                       |
                                        person identity knowledge <----+
                                                  |
                                          name retrieval

| Task | Components it needs |
| --- | --- |
| Two everyday objects, same or different? | general vision |
| Two faces, same or different? | general vision, face perception |
| Is this face familiar? | general vision, face perception, face familiarity |
| From the face, who is this? | + person identity |
| From the face, what is their name? | + name retrieval |
| From the voice, who is this? | voice recognition, person identity |
| From the voice, what is their name? | + name retrieval |

A task is impaired exactly when the affected component is one it needs.

### Reference profiles a lecturer can check

Reading order: objects, faces, familiar, identity-from-face, name-from-face,
identity-from-voice, name-from-voice. **I** is impaired, **–** is within range.

| Affected component | Profile |
| --- | --- |
| General visual processing | I I I I I – – |
| Face perception | – I I I I – – |
| Face familiarity | – – I I I – – |
| Voice recognition | – – – – – I I |
| Person identity knowledge | – – – I I I I |
| Name retrieval | – – – – I – I |
| Nothing selectively affected | – – – – – – – |

### The six cases in experiment 2

| Profile | Observed | Fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | – I I I I – – | face perception, uniquely |
| B | – – I I I – – | face familiarity, uniquely |
| C | – – – I I I I | person identity knowledge, uniquely |
| D | – – – – I – I | name retrieval, uniquely |
| E | I I I I I I I | nothing - every component leaves something intact |
| F | (not administered) I I I I – – | general vision **and** face perception |

The tool re-runs all six locations against the observed profile rather than
asserting any of this, so editing the task requirements changes the feedback
automatically.

### The faces

Each schematic face is seven numbers - head width, chin width, eye spacing,
eye height, eye size, nose length, mouth width - drawn by mulberry32 from a
visible seed. The seed starts at 20260807 and advances by 1013 on each
*Draw four more*, and it is printed beside the faces so a demonstrator can
reproduce a set. Nothing in the model depends on what the faces look like;
they exist to make the stimulus question concrete and to show that no
photograph is being used.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Bruce and Young (1986)** for the functional model this simplifies, and for
  the ordering of familiarity, identity and name.
- **Burton, Bruce and Johnston (1990)** for the interactive-activation version
  and for why names are hardest.
- **De Renzi et al. (1991)** and **Damasio, Damasio and Van Hoesen (1982)** on
  the heterogeneity of acquired face-recognition difficulties.
- **Duchaine and Nakayama (2006)** on developmental cases, and on why a control
  task matched for difficulty is indispensable.
- **Behrmann and Avidan (2005)** for the developmental literature and its
  variability.
- **Gauthier and Tarr (1997)** and **McKone, Kanwisher and Duchaine (2007)**
  for the two sides of the face-specificity argument, which this tool
  deliberately does not settle.
- **Ellis, Young, Quayle and De Pauw (1997)** on covert recognition, which is
  why "absence of a reported feeling of familiarity" is not "absence of
  processing".
- **Hanley and Cohen (2008)** on proper-name retrieval and why difficulty with
  it is expected rather than diagnostic.

Full references are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the tool does not
appear to derive its invented profiles from any of them.
