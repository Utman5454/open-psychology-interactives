Assessment Battery Builder

A referral question, ninety minutes and seventeen task types. Choose what to administer, then find out what your battery could and could not conclude.

Simulated - generic task descriptions, fictional referrals, no real tests and no scoring of anybody

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to select tasks from a referral question, and say which measures make the rest interpretable.

About 30 minutes across three referrals. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

Not a battery, and not a clinical tool

Every task here is described generically - "learning and recalling a word list", not any published test. No real test, subtest, item, stimulus or norm is reproduced, no score is produced for anybody, and the durations are round teaching numbers rather than published administration times.

The three referrals are invented. Real assessment involves a history, an interview, informant report, observation across the session and a formulation, none of which a task list can carry. This activity is about the reasoning behind a selection, not about how assessment is actually done.

  1. Read the referral question. It is the only thing that makes a task relevant or irrelevant.
  2. Tick tasks. The panel on the right updates as you go: time used, what the battery can support, and what it cannot.
  3. Submit the battery for a verdict, then try the other two referrals - the right answer changes completely.

    Challenge - a conclusion to weigh

    A fictional report reads: "Delayed recall fell at the 3rd percentile, indicating a significant memory impairment." The assessment consisted of that one task.

    What is the most important thing missing?

    What this demonstrates

    The referral question decides what is relevant

    The same task is essential in one battery and a waste of fifteen minutes in another. Nothing on the menu is good or bad on its own. A battery assembled from a list of respected tasks, without a question in front of it, produces a great many numbers and no argument.

    Two measures of the same thing are not a waste

    A single score carries measurement error, task-specific demands and whatever happened in the ten minutes it was administered. Two tasks that make the same demand in different ways either agree, which is evidence, or disagree, which is information about the tasks. One task can do neither.

    You need a domain you expect to be intact

    Without one, every finding is "this person scored low on the things we tested", which is compatible with a selective difficulty and equally compatible with a general one. The comparison domain is what turns a list of low scores into a pattern, and it is usually the first thing dropped when time runs short.

    The measures that answer nothing are the ones that matter

    Mood, fatigue, effort, sensory function and premorbid level answer no referral question. They decide whether the answers to the referral question mean anything. A low score has several explanations that are cheaper to check and more common than the one in the referral letter, and a battery that has not checked them has not ruled them out.

    Longer is not better

    Session time is finite and so is the person's attention. Past about seventy minutes the tasks administered last are measuring fatigue as much as anything else, which is a problem precisely because the interesting tasks tend to be scheduled first and the context measures last. A battery is a set of decisions about what to leave out.

    What this does not demonstrate

    • Nothing here is a real battery. Task descriptions are generic, no published test, subtest, item, stimulus or norm is reproduced, and the durations are round teaching numbers.
    • Nobody is assessed and nothing is scored. The tool evaluates the design of a battery, not a person. No fictional person in it receives a result.
    • Four checks is a caricature. Real selection also weighs the person's language, literacy, education, cultural background, sensory and motor limitations, the availability of norms that fit them, how the results will be used and what the person actually wants from the assessment.
    • Assessment is not a task list. History, interview, informant report, observation across the session and a formulation carry at least as much weight as the scores, and none of them appears here.
    • The domains are conveniences. Every task loads on several of them, which is the point made at length by the Executive Function Task Laboratory in this module.
    • No diagnosis, no anatomy, no prognosis. The tool names no brain region and reaches no conclusion about any fictional person's condition.
