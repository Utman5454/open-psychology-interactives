# Teaching notes — Assessment Battery Builder

`modules/neuropsychology/tools/11-neuropsych-assessment-battery-builder/`

The measures that answer no referral question are the ones that decide whether
the answers to the referral question mean anything. They are also the first
things dropped when the session runs short.

---

## Running it from the front

Give the room the first referral and no advice, and most batteries come back full of the interesting tasks and short of the boring ones. The four checks are what to talk about, and the one that fails most often is the context one.

The second referral is the important one. Eight months after a mild head injury, the mood and effort measures are not an afterthought - they are the referral question. A battery without them cannot distinguish the readings that matter, no matter how many cognitive tests it contains.

Worth doing explicitly: tick everything. All three content checks pass and the battery runs to 167 minutes, which means it cannot be delivered and therefore says nothing. Ask what would have to come out, and watch how quickly the room offers the context measures.

## Intended level

Second- or third-year undergraduate neuropsychology, or an introductory
assessment strand. No clinical experience is assumed and no clinical materials
are used. It works best after the Executive Function Task Laboratory, which
establishes that no task measures one thing, and after the Face Recognition
Detective, which establishes what a control task is for.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. select tasks from a referral question rather than from a list of respected
   tests;
2. explain why two measures of the same demand say more than one;
3. say why a battery needs a domain the referral does not ask about;
4. name the measures that answer no referral question and without which
   nothing else can be interpreted;
5. treat session time as a design constraint that forces real trade-offs.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 10 minutes - referral 2, built badly and
  then repaired.
- **Students in pairs:** 25 minutes for all three referrals.
- **With the challenge and debrief:** 40 minutes.

## Preparation

None. Read the "not a battery, and not a clinical tool" panel aloud. Every
task is described generically, no published test or norm is reproduced, and
the durations are round teaching numbers. Students with placement experience
will otherwise spend the session mapping the descriptions onto real tests,
which is not the exercise.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

**Referral 2** - the mild head injury eight months on. Ask the room to call
out tasks and tick what they say. Almost always you end up with a memory task,
an attention task, a switching task and a couple of others, and no mood
questionnaire and no validity check. Submit it. Two checks fail, and the
feedback prints the reason: at eight months after a mild injury, low mood,
poor sleep, pain and the effort a person brings to a test they have a stake in
all lower scores, and the battery cannot choose between the readings.

Now press **Load a defensible battery** and compare. It is five tasks and 58
minutes. The two measures that were missing cost fifteen minutes between them.

**Then tick everything.** All three content checks pass and the battery runs
to 167 minutes, so it cannot be delivered and therefore says nothing. Ask what
would come out. The room will offer the context measures within about ten
seconds, which is the habit the whole activity is about.

**Finally, switch referral without changing the selection.** The same battery
that met all four checks for referral 1 fails for referral 2. Nothing on the
menu is good or bad on its own.

## Prediction question

> You have ninety minutes and a referral asking whether the memory problems a
> fictional person reports show up on testing. You have already chosen one good
> memory test. Which single addition would do most for the confidence of your
> conclusions?

Intended: *a measure of mood and fatigue, and a check on effort*. The feedback
treats "a second memory test" and "a comparison domain" as genuinely valuable
for different problems, and explains what each does and does not buy. "A
longer version of the same test" is the only one treated as a mistake.

## Activity sequence

1. **Commit to the opening judgement.** The builder stays hidden until an
   answer is recorded; there is a skip button for demonstrators.
2. **Referral 1, in pairs, without advice.** Submit, read the verdict, repair,
   submit again.
3. **Compare with the worked battery** and ask what each pair had that it did
   not need.
4. **Referral 2.** This is the one where the context measures are most of the
   answer.
5. **Referral 3**, which needs two target domains covered rather than one -
   the tool requires at least one task in each.
6. **The goal counter** tracks how many of the three referrals have been
   answered with all four checks met.
7. **The challenge** on the single-score report sentence.

## Debrief questions

1. Which task on the menu is essential in one referral and a waste of time in
   another? Name two.
2. Why is "learning and recalling a word list" the most expensive item here,
   and what does that cost you elsewhere?
3. What exactly does the comparison domain buy you that a second target-domain
   task does not?
4. Mood, effort, premorbid level and sensory function answer no referral
   question. Why is that a reason to include them rather than to leave them
   out?
5. The tool flags two tasks measuring the same thing outside the target
   domains as redundant, but two tasks measuring the same thing inside them as
   converging evidence. Why the difference?
6. What is wrong with the sentence "delayed recall was at the 3rd percentile,
   indicating a memory impairment"? Rewrite it defensibly.
7. This activity is a task list. Name three things a real assessment contains
   that no task list can carry.

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "More tests means more confidence." | Tick everything: 167 minutes, undeliverable, and therefore worth nothing. |
| "Mood questionnaires are for clinical psychologists." | Low mood is among the commonest reasons a cognitive score falls. Not measuring it does not remove it from the explanation. |
| "An effort measure implies the person is faking." | It measures whether the scores can be taken at face value. Effort varies with pain, fatigue, sleep and mood in people with no interest at all in appearing impaired. |
| "The comparison domain is a luxury." | Without it every finding is "low on the things we tested", which cannot distinguish a selective difficulty from a general one. |
| "Two tests of the same thing is redundant." | Inside the referral's domains it is converging evidence. Outside them it is administration time. The tool distinguishes the two. |
| "A percentile is objective." | A percentile is a comparison with a norm group. Whether this person belongs in that norm group is what the premorbid estimate is for. |
| "You can always add the missing test next time." | Sometimes, and the session you have is the session you have. Design is what you do with the constraint rather than around it. |
| "The best battery is the one with the best tests." | Nothing on the menu is good or bad on its own. Switch referral without changing the selection and watch the checks change. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **Nothing here is a real battery.** Task descriptions are generic and no
  published test, subtest, item, stimulus, norm or administration time is
  reproduced.
- **Nobody is assessed and nothing is scored.** The tool evaluates the design
  of a battery, and no fictional person in it receives a result.
- **Four checks is a caricature.** Real selection also weighs the person's
  language, literacy, education, cultural background, sensory and motor
  limitations, whether norms exist that fit them, how the results will be used,
  and what the person actually wants from the assessment.
- **Assessment is not a task list.** History, interview, informant report,
  observation across the session and formulation carry at least as much weight
  and appear nowhere here.
- **The domains are a convenience.** Every task loads on several, which is the
  argument made at length by the Executive Function Task Laboratory in this
  module.
- **No anatomy, no diagnosis, no prognosis.**

## Accessibility considerations

- Native checkbox, select and button controls; every control has an accessible
  name (verified programmatically). Each task label carries its own duration,
  so the time cost is readable without the chart.
- The session-time bar is `aria-hidden` and paired inside its figure with a
  sentence giving the number of tasks, the minutes used, the budget and
  whether the battery is deliverable. The end of the session is a dashed line
  **and** is named in the axis text.
- The four checks write "(met)" or "(not yet)" into their own text.
- The control column is seventeen checkboxes, which is why the primary is
  pinned. Measured at 1366×768 the pinned block is 272 to 293px depending on
  how much is selected, and with the submit button at the bottom of the window
  the checks are still at the top of the stage.
- Every selection, preset and submission announces through the polite live
  region; focus moves to the builder heading when the prediction unlocks it.
- No horizontal page scroll at 320px, 375px, 1366px, 1440px or 1920px.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Write the referral.** Invent a fourth referral, name its target domain,
   its comparison domain and the two context measures it most needs, and say
   why.
2. **Halve the session.** Rebuild your best battery for referral 3 in
   45 minutes and defend what you dropped.
3. **The other constraints.** Add three checks the tool does not have -
   language of testing, availability of appropriate norms, motor requirements -
   and say which tasks each would rule out.
4. **Report the limits.** Write the paragraph of a report that states honestly
   what the battery could not establish.
5. **Order the session.** Given a battery, decide the order of administration
   and say what your ordering costs.

## The model

Session budget **90 minutes**; a burden warning above **70**.

Four checks:

1. **Converging evidence** - at least two tasks in the referral's target
   domains, and at least one in each of them.
2. **A comparison domain** - at least one task in a domain the referral does
   not ask about.
3. **Context measures** - both of the two named by that referral.
4. **Deliverable** - total time within the budget.

Plus a redundancy note when two selected tasks share a narrow measure outside
the target domains, and a dependency: recognition of a word list cannot be
administered without the learning task, and a task with an unmet dependency
counts towards nothing.

| Task | Domain | Min | Measures |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| Recognising those words among distractors | Memory | 8 | verbal recognition |

| Naming line drawings | Language | 10 | word retrieval |
| Generating words to a category | Language | 6 | word retrieval |
| Following spoken instructions | Language | 9 | sentence comprehension |
| Sorting to a rule that changes | Executive | 15 | set switching |
| Naming the ink colour of a colour word | Executive | 8 | response inhibition |
| Copying a complex figure | Visuospatial | 12 | constructional |
| Crossing out targets across a page | Visuospatial | 8 | spatial attention |
| Mood, sleep and fatigue questionnaire | Context | 5 | mood and fatigue |
| A performance-validity check | Context | 10 | effort |
| Estimating premorbid level from word reading | Context | 8 | premorbid level |

Everything ticked comes to **167 minutes**.

### The three referrals, and their worked batteries

| Referral | Target | Comparison | Context needed | Worked battery | Minutes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Word-finding after a left-hemisphere stroke | language | visuospatial, memory, executive | premorbid level, sensory screen | naming, fluency, following instructions, figure copy, premorbid, senses | 50 |
| 2. Concentration complaints after a mild head injury | memory, attention | language, visuospatial | mood and fatigue, effort | digit sequences, word list, naming, mood, effort | 58 |
| 3. Getting lost on familiar routes | visuospatial, memory | language, attention | sensory screen, mood and fatigue | figure copy, cancellation, word list, naming, senses, mood | 65 |

Every worked battery meets all four checks. The verdict is then composed from
what passed and what failed, in two halves - what the battery could say and
what it could not - so the output is a statement about the design rather than
a score.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Lezak, Howieson, Bigler and Tranel**, *Neuropsychological Assessment*, for
  the hypothesis-driven approach to selection. No materials from it are used.
- **Bauer (2000)** and **Kaplan's** process approach, for the argument that
  what a battery is for determines what belongs in it.
- **Larrabee (2012)** and **Bush et al. (2005)** on performance validity, and
  on why validity measures are part of standard practice rather than an
  accusation.
- **Crawford, Garthwaite and Gault (2007)** on the base rate of low scores: in
  any long battery, several low scores are expected in people with no
  difficulty at all, which is a further argument against interpreting isolated
  scores.
- **Binder, Iverson and Brooks (2009)** for how common low scores are in
  healthy adults.
- **Franzen and Iverson**, and the wider premorbid-estimation literature, on
  why "low" needs a comparison with this person rather than only with a norm
  group.
- **Rohling et al. (2015)** and the mild-traumatic-brain-injury outcome
  literature, for why referral 2 turns so heavily on mood, effort and
  expectation.

Full references are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the tool does not
appear to derive its invented task menu from any of them.
