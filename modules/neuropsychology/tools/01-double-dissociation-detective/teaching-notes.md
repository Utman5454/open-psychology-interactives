# Teaching notes - Double Dissociation Detective

`modules/neuropsychology/tools/01-double-dissociation-detective/`

**What it teaches.** A double dissociation defeats one specific rival:
that the two tasks differ only in difficulty, both drawing on the same
shared graded resource. It does not prove the tasks are handled by
separate modules, and several single-system accounts survive it
perfectly well. A single dissociation, on its own, cannot even rule out
the difficulty explanation.

**Before students start.** Read the "these are not patients" panel
aloud. It sets the frame and saves a detour later.

**What students do.**
1. Commit to a verdict on the opening judgement.
2. Work through six case files, reading each score against its own
   control group before opening the task demands and returning a
   verdict.
3. On case 5, move the test-length slider before committing.
4. Take the closing challenge on what a clean crossover actually
   supports.

**What to look for.** Case 4 catches most people out. The raw scores are
88% and 30%, but referenced to their own control groups the gap almost
disappears, down to 0.18 control SDs against the 2.16 that forty items
can resolve. On case 5, watch the verdict move through three stages,
insufficient evidence, a single dissociation, then a double
dissociation, as the slider goes from 40 to 55 items, while none of the
actual scores change.

**Debrief.** Why is comparing 88% with 30% not really a comparison at
all? Case 6 is a genuine crossover with a confound sitting in the
task-demands table. What does that case say about how real single-case
reports should be read?

**Common misconception / caution.** If a student says a double
dissociation proves two separate brain regions, point out that nothing
on the page mentions anatomy at all. A crossover is evidence about
tasks, not tissue. And two people are not a syndrome: the statistics
here use a simplified binomial standard error rather than the modified
t-test a real single-case comparison needs.

**Use in class / timing.** Cases 4 and 5 alone carry the argument in
about 10 minutes from the front. All six cases with the challenge and
debrief run to about 45 minutes.
