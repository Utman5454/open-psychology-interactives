Neuropsychology

Split-Brain Laboratory

Set up a lateralised trial, predict whether the answer can get out, and then find the conditions the classic result quietly depends on.

Simulated - a deliberately simplified model, original items, no published stimuli and no real participants

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to work out what a split-brain trial shows about routing, rather than about what each hemisphere is for.

About 30 minutes, in two experiments. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

Read this before you start

Nothing on this page is flashed to one visual field. A web page cannot control where your eyes are, and lateralised presentation needs fixation control and timing that a browser cannot provide. You build a trial and the tool reports what a simplified model predicts. The model is an argument about routing, not a measurement.

The findings this is based on come from a very small number of people who had surgery for severe epilepsy, whose brains differed from typical brains in other ways too. Results varied between them and between tasks. Nothing here supports the idea of left-brained or right-brained people.

  1. Experiment 1. Choose where the item appears, how you ask for a response, and whether the corpus callosum is intact. Predict the outcome, then run the trial.
  2. Keep going until you have found a trial where cutting the callosum changes the outcome and one where it changes nothing.
  3. Experiment 2. Take the one trial that classically fails and switch off, one at a time, the conditions it depends on.

First, a judgement

In a fictional study, a word is presented in the left visual field of a person whose corpus callosum has been completely divided. The person says they saw nothing at all. Moments later, their left hand picks the matching object out of a bag they cannot see into.

    With the callosum intact every cell succeeds, because both hemispheres receive the item within a few tens of milliseconds. After a complete section, a response succeeds only when the hemisphere that received the item also controls the channel you asked with. That is the whole model.

    Challenge - two claims to weigh

    1. A magazine article says some people are "right-brained" - creative, intuitive, visual - and others are "left-brained". What is the strongest objection from this material?
    2. A fictional participant with a complete section names a word shown in the left visual field on 8 trials out of 10. Which conclusion is safest?

    What this demonstrates

    The finding is about routing, not about jobs

    Each half of the visual world reaches the opposite hemisphere. Each hand is controlled mainly by the opposite hemisphere. Speech is produced by one hemisphere - usually the left. None of that is a claim about what a hemisphere is for. It is a claim about which structure receives a signal and which can act on it. The dramatic split-brain results follow from putting a receiving structure and an acting structure on opposite sides of a cut.

    The same item, two answers

    A word in the left visual field cannot be spoken but can be picked out by the left hand. Nothing about the item changed, and nothing about the person's knowledge of spoons changed. Only the channel you asked with changed. This is why "what can this person do?" is an incomplete question: the answer depends on how you ask.

    The demonstration is fragile on purpose

    Presentation has to be brief enough that the eyes cannot move, fixation has to be monitored, the section has to be complete, and routes such as the left hand tracing a shape or the person making a small noise have to be controlled. Switch any of these off and the person reports the word. A result that appears when the safeguards fail looks exactly like a result that overturns the theory, and the only way to tell them apart is the method section.

    Lateralisation is a tendency, not a switch

    Language is left-lateralised in most right-handed people and in most left-handed people, but not in all, and in a substantial minority it is bilateral. Many disconnected right hemispheres can read simple words and understand speech even though they cannot speak. Lateralisation is a matter of degree, it varies between people, it varies between tasks, and it changes what a lateralised trial can tell you.

    Why "left-brained people" does not follow

    These effects appear because the hemispheres have been surgically separated. In a connected brain the corpus callosum carries the information across in a few tens of milliseconds, so both hemispheres have almost everything almost always. No study of connected brains has found people who use one hemisphere and not the other, and creativity, intuition and logic are not the sort of thing that a hemisphere holds.

    What this does not demonstrate

    • No lateralised presentation happens here. A web page cannot control your fixation or the duration of a flash, so nothing on this page is presented to one hemisphere. You set up a trial and read the model's prediction.
    • The model is a caricature. Real split-brain performance varies with the material, the task, the interval, the amount of practice and the individual, and the same person can succeed on Monday and fail on Friday.
    • Speech is not always left. The model treats it as left in every case because that is the textbook simplifying assumption. In real people it is not universal, and the tool lets you switch that assumption off in experiment 2.
    • Very few people, and not typical brains. The original participants had epilepsy severe enough to justify surgery, along with medication, prior seizures and, in some cases, atypical organisation from early life.
    • No published stimuli. The four items here are ordinary words invented for this page. No study's materials, images or procedures are reproduced.
    • No diagnosis, no personality claim. Nothing here says anything about the person using the tool, and nothing here supports left-brained or right-brained descriptions of anybody.
