# Teaching notes — Split-Brain Laboratory

`modules/neuropsychology/tools/09-hemispheric-lateralisation-split-brain/`

The split-brain findings are not a discovery about what each hemisphere is
for. They are a discovery about what happens when you put the structure that
receives a signal and the structure that can act on it on opposite sides of a
cut.

---

## Intended level

First- or second-year undergraduate neuropsychology, or a research-methods
strand that wants a worked example of how a famous result depends on its
safeguards. No clinical experience is assumed. It sits well beside the Network
Disconnection Mapper, which makes the same disconnection argument for a
network rather than for two hemispheres.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. work out, for any combination of visual field and response channel, whether
   a disconnected hemisphere can answer;
2. explain why that is a claim about routing rather than about hemispheric
   jobs;
3. name at least three methodological conditions the classic demonstration
   depends on, and say what happens to the result when each fails;
4. distinguish a result that challenges an account from a result produced by a
   procedural failure, given that the two look identical;
5. explain why none of this supports left-brained or right-brained
   descriptions of people.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 10 minutes - the classic trial, then the
  same trial with the other hand, then the callosum restored.
- **Students in pairs:** 25 minutes.
- **With the challenge and debrief:** 40 minutes.

## Preparation

None, but read the "read this before you start" panel aloud. Students expect
a tachistoscope demonstration and there is not one: a web page cannot control
fixation or presentation duration, so nothing here is presented to one visual
field. The tool reports what a simplified model gives. Saying this once at the
start prevents the whole activity being misread as data.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

**The classic trial.** Press *Set up the classic trial*: the word SPOON in the
left visual field, a spoken report, a complete section. Take a vote on the
prediction before running it. The model says the person says they saw nothing
at all.

**Change one thing.** Keep everything and switch the response channel to the
left hand. The same word, the same hemisphere, the opposite outcome. Ask what
changed about the person. Nothing did. What changed is the channel you asked
through.

**Restore the callosum.** Set it back to intact and run both again. Both
succeed. Contralateral projection has not changed at all; what has changed is
whether the two hemispheres can pass the information between them. This is the
step that kills the left-brained/right-brained idea, because it shows the
effects existing only when the cut exists.

**Open "every combination at once"** and read down the two right-hand columns.
Twelve succeed with the callosum intact; three of six succeed after a section.

**Then experiment 2.** With the trial fixed at the one that fails, untick
*presentation was brief*. The word is now reported - because the eyes moved,
not because anything about the brain is different. Tick it back and untick
*cross-cueing routes were controlled* instead. The word is reported again, for
an entirely different reason. Ask the room: if you read "the participant named
the word on 8 trials out of 10", which of these are you looking at? You cannot
tell from the result. You can only tell from the method.

Finish with *fixation was monitored and held*. This one does not turn the
failure into a success - it makes the trial uninterpretable. Nothing about the
data looks different, which is why it is the dangerous one.

## Prediction question

> A word is presented in the left visual field of a person whose corpus
> callosum has been completely divided. The person says they saw nothing at
> all. Moments later their left hand picks the matching object out of a bag
> they cannot see into. What is the best account of that?

Intended: *the hemisphere that saw it is not the hemisphere that speaks, and
the two can no longer pass the information between them*. The "not conscious
of it" option is treated as a redescription rather than an explanation, and the
feedback says why.

## Activity sequence

1. **Commit to the opening judgement.** The laboratory stays hidden until an
   answer is recorded; there is a skip button for demonstrators.
2. **Experiment 1, with a prediction on every trial.** The Run button refuses
   to fire without one, and says why.
3. **Meet the goal.** Three checks: a sectioned trial where the section
   changes the outcome, one where it changes nothing, and one visual field
   asked with each hand in turn. The button through to experiment 2 stays
   disabled until all three are met.
4. **Ask each pair to state the rule** in one sentence before they move on.
   The rule is: after a complete section a response succeeds only when the
   hemisphere that received the item also controls the channel you asked with.
5. **Experiment 2, one condition at a time**, then the presets.
6. **The challenge**, two questions - one about the neuromyth, one about
   inference from an anomalous result.

## Debrief questions

1. The same word produces a correct left hand and a silent mouth. What does
   that do to the question "what does this person know?"
2. Which is the more fundamental fact here, contralateral projection or the
   cut? What happens to the phenomenon if you remove each one?
3. Four of the five conditions in experiment 2 let the word be reported. Why
   does the fifth behave differently?
4. You read a paper reporting that a participant named left-field words on
   most trials. What do you need from the method section before you can
   interpret it?
5. Language is left-lateralised in most people but not all. What does that do
   to a single-case split-brain result?
6. Why can a finding that depends on a surgical disconnection not describe
   people who do not have one?
7. What would you have to observe in a connected brain for "right-brained
   person" to mean anything?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "The right hemisphere is the visual one." | Both hemispheres process vision. Which one received *this* item is a fact about where it appeared, not about a division of labour. |
| "The left hemisphere is dominant, so it overrides the right." | It has nothing to override - it does not have the information, which is why it says nothing rather than something wrong. |
| "The person is unconscious of the word." | That restates the failure to report it. Ask why the left hand is unaffected. |
| "So we really are left-brained and right-brained." | Restore the callosum in experiment 1. Every effect disappears. A phenomenon that requires a cut cannot describe people without one. |
| "The right hemisphere cannot understand language." | Many disconnected right hemispheres read simple words and follow spoken instructions. Producing speech and understanding language are different things, and the tool separates them in condition five. |
| "If the participant named the word, the theory is wrong." | Experiment 2 gives four ways to get that observation without touching the theory. |
| "Split-brain surgery cuts the brain in half." | It divides the corpus callosum, in several different extents. The splenium in particular can be spared, which is condition three. |
| "These are healthy volunteers." | They had epilepsy severe enough to justify surgery, with medication, prior seizures and, in some cases, atypical organisation from early life. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **Nothing is presented to a visual field.** A browser cannot control
  fixation or duration. The tool computes; it does not test.
- **The model is a caricature.** Real split-brain performance varies with
  material, task, response interval, practice and individual, and the same
  person can succeed one week and fail the next.
- **Speech is treated as left in every case**, which is the textbook
  simplification rather than the truth. Experiment 2 exists partly to make
  that assumption visible and switchable.
- **No published stimuli or procedures.** The four items are ordinary words
  invented for this page. No study's materials or images are reproduced and
  no real participant appears.
- **No diagnosis and no personality claim.** Nothing here says anything about
  the person using the tool.
- **The routing rules are textbook-level.** Real hand control is not purely
  contralateral, subcortical routes exist, and some cross-hemisphere transfer
  survives a callosotomy by other commissures.

## Accessibility considerations

- Native select, radio, checkbox and button controls; every control has an
  accessible name (verified programmatically).
- The routing diagram is `aria-hidden` and carries a visible sentence inside
  its own figure naming the item, the receiving hemisphere, the channel asked
  with, the state of the callosum and the outcome. A second table gives all
  twelve combinations.
- Nothing in the drawing depends on colour: the active route is a thick solid
  line against thin dashed ones, active boxes carry a heavier border **and**
  their own label text ("SPOON shown here", "asked with this", "answers",
  "no answer"), and the section is drawn as a cross over a gap and named
  "callosum cut".
- Each experiment has its own pinned primary and the inactive one is hidden.
  Measured at 1366×768 the pins are 419px and 229px; with the last control of
  experiment 1 scrolled to the bottom of the window the diagram is still at the
  top of the stage.
- The condition explanations in experiment 2 sit outside the pinned block, so
  the pin cannot grow past its budget as conditions are switched off.
- The goal checklist writes "(met)" or "(not yet)" into the text rather than
  relying on the tick marker.
- Every trial, preset and condition change announces through the polite live
  region; focus moves to the laboratory heading when the prediction unlocks it.
- No horizontal page scroll at 320px, 375px, 1366px, 1440px or 1920px.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Design the crucial trial.** You want to know whether a particular
   person's right hemisphere can read. Specify the visual field, the response
   channel and the safeguards, and say what each possible outcome would mean.
2. **Write the method paragraph.** In 120 words, describe a lateralised
   presentation procedure that closes all four methodological conditions.
3. **Find the neuromyth.** Bring in a magazine or training-course claim about
   left-brained and right-brained people and identify the exact step where the
   inference fails.
4. **Add a channel.** Where would drawing with the left hand sit in the
   diagram, and which trials would it change?
5. **Look up the individual variation.** Find an estimate of how often language
   is not left-lateralised and work out what that does to a single-case
   conclusion.

## The model

| | Left visual field | Right visual field |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reaches | right hemisphere | left hemisphere |

| Response channel | Controlled by |
| --- | --- |
| Speech | left hemisphere |
| Left hand | right hemisphere |
| Right hand | left hemisphere |

With the callosum **intact**, whatever one hemisphere receives is available to
the other, so all twelve combinations produce a correct response. After a
**complete section**, a response succeeds only when the hemisphere that
received the item also controls the channel asked with.

### Reference values a lecturer can check

| Item appears in | Asked with | Intact | Sectioned |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Left field | Speech | correct response | no response |
| Left field | Left hand | correct response | correct response |
| Left field | Right hand | correct response | no response |
| Right field | Speech | correct response | correct response |
| Right field | Left hand | correct response | no response |
| Right field | Right hand | correct response | correct response |

The goal checklist is met by, for example: left field with speech (the section
changes the outcome), left field with the left hand (it does not), and left
field with each hand in turn. Three trials are enough.

### Experiment 2

The trial is fixed at **left visual field, spoken report, complete section**.
Five conditions, all holding by default:

| Condition | What happens if it fails |
| --- | --- |
| Presentation was brief | The eyes move, the word also lands in the right visual field, and the left hemisphere reads it there |
| Fixation was monitored and held | Which hemisphere received the word is unknown; the trial is uninterpretable in either direction |
| The section was complete, including the splenium | Visual information still crosses |
| Cross-cueing routes were controlled | The person cues their own left hemisphere; the answer is right by an excluded route |
| Speech is produced by the left hemisphere only | The right hemisphere contributes to speech and the word is reported without anything crossing |

Losing fixation monitoring dominates: whatever else is switched off, the
verdict becomes *uninterpretable*. Breaking any of the other four gives
*the word is reported* with one explanation listed per broken condition. With
all five holding, the verdict is *nothing is reported - the classic result*.

The three presets are **all five hold**, **a session that ran too fast to
control** (brief presentation and fixation monitoring both lost) and
**everything that could go wrong** (all five lost, five reasons listed).

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Sperry (1968, 1982)** and **Gazzaniga (1967, 2005)** for the original
  disconnection work and its interpretation. None of their materials are
  reproduced here.
- **Bogen and Vogel** for the surgical context, and for why the extent of the
  section varied between patients.
- **Corballis (1995)** and **Gazzaniga (2000)** on cross-cueing and on how
  carefully the response channel has to be controlled.
- **Pinto et al. (2017)** for a case where response channel and visual field
  dissociated less cleanly than the classical account predicts, and the debate
  that followed - a good illustration of the second challenge question.
- **Corballis and Häberling (2017)** and **Nielsen et al. (2013)** on why
  lateralisation does not support left-brained and right-brained personality
  types.
- **Knecht et al. (2000)** for estimates of how often language lateralisation
  departs from the left-hemisphere default.
- **Mazoyer et al. (2014)** on atypical and bilateral language organisation.

Full references are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the tool does not
appear to derive its simplified model from any particular study.
