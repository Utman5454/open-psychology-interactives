Crowd Behaviour Laboratory: Deindividuation and ESIM

One fictional afternoon on Marlow Bridge, set up five different ways. A generic deindividuation account asks how much a crowd does. The elaborated social identity model asks what it does, to whom, and who joined in - and answers differently depending on how the crowd was policed.

Fictional event, model-implied numbers - Marlow Bridge exists only here, and no real incident, policing operation or dataset is described

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to say how deindividuation and the elaborated social identity model differ about the patterning of crowd action, and where each locates the cause.

About 30 minutes. Nothing is timed and nothing you choose is saved or sent anywhere.

What the numbers here are

Everything on this page is produced by a small rule-based model whose arithmetic is printed in the teaching notes. It is a device for making two theories argue with each other in a way you can point at, not a simulation of any real crowd and not evidence for either account. Neither theory is refuted here. Deindividuation research is a live tradition with a serious modern successor, and the elaborated social identity model has its own critics; the section below the laboratory sets out what each is criticised for.

How to use this

  1. Read the two accounts and commit to an answer to the question below.
  2. Set up the afternoon: how the police responded, how the crowd read that response, whether a common category was available, what the norm was, and whether people were identifiable.
  3. Watch the two things the result reports - how many sections of the crowd took part, and how concentrated their action was.
  4. Use the four preset set-ups to reach the contrasts quickly.
  5. Then judge five observations from a different event.

The two accounts

Both are trying to explain the same thing: why people in crowds sometimes do what they would not do alone. They locate the explanation in different places, and that is what makes them testable against each other.

A generic deindividuation account

Being one of many reduces attention to the self and to personal standards. Anonymity, arousal and the sheer size of the crowd loosen the ordinary restraints on behaviour, so what emerges is behaviour that is less regulated than usual.

What follows: action should rise with anonymity, and because the restraint that has been lost was general, the resulting behaviour should be relatively undirected - spread across whatever targets happen to be to hand. The cause sits inside the crowd member.

The elaborated social identity model

People in a crowd do not lose an identity, they act in terms of one they share. A shared identity brings its own norms about what is and is not acceptable, and those norms come with limits as well as licence.

What follows: action should be patterned - aimed at some targets and withheld from others, sometimes actively policed by the crowd itself. And because a shared identity can be created by how a crowd is treated, indiscriminate policing can produce the very unity it is trying to prevent. The cause sits in the relationship between the crowd and the people policing it.

The laboratory

Marlow Bridge, a Saturday afternoon. Around two thousand people have come to a demonstration about the closure of the river crossing. Six recognisable sections of the crowd are present, including a handful who arrived expecting a confrontation. Set the afternoon up and see what the model produces.

Key terms
Deindividuation
An account in which being submerged in a crowd loosens self-regulation, so that behaviour becomes less constrained.
Elaborated social identity model
An account in which crowd action follows from a shared social identity, and is patterned by what that identity makes legitimate.
Patterning
Whether action concentrates on particular targets or spreads across all of them. It is where the two accounts predict differently.
Legitimacy
How the crowd reads the treatment it is receiving. Here it is a setting you choose, not a verdict the activity reaches.

What each account is criticised for

Neither of these is a settled matter, and a tool that let you believe otherwise would be doing you a disservice.

Against the deindividuation account

  • The evidence is thinner than the textbook suggests. Reviews of the experimental literature have repeatedly found the predicted effects of anonymity and group immersion to be small and inconsistent.
  • It predicts the wrong shape. Crowd action is usually limited: particular targets are attacked and others nearby are conspicuously left alone, sometimes protected by the crowd. A loss of restraint does not obviously explain restraint.
  • Anonymity does not simply disinhibit. A substantial body of work finds that anonymity increases conformity to whatever norm is salient - which can be helpfulness, order or silence as easily as aggression.
  • It has a politics. Locating the cause inside the crowd member makes the crowd the thing to be explained and the policing merely the response to it, which is a convenient division of labour for whoever did the policing.

Against the elaborated social identity model

  • Much of the evidence is retrospective. Detailed accounts of particular events, reconstructed afterwards from interviews and records, are hard to falsify and hard to aggregate.
  • "The norm" can be read off the outcome. If whatever the crowd did is explained as the norm it shared, the account risks describing rather than predicting - unless the norm is specified in advance, which is difficult.
  • Not all crowd action is patterned. Some events really do involve widespread, unfocused destruction, and an account built on selectivity has to work harder there.
  • It can under-describe what a crowd contains. Treating the crowd as one identity risks losing the sections within it that disagreed, left, or tried to stop what was happening.
  • It also has a politics. Locating the cause in the relationship makes policing a candidate cause, which is a substantive claim and not a neutral one.

Challenge - a different event

A fictional summer night in a different fictional town: a music event on Calder Green overran, the power was cut at midnight and several thousand people were moved off the field. Five things were observed. For each, say which account handles it better - and notice that the honest answer is sometimes "both" and sometimes "neither".

What this demonstrates

The disagreement is about pattern, not about amount

Both accounts agree that people in crowds sometimes do things they would not do alone. What they disagree about is the shape of it. A generic deindividuation account expects restraint to be lost, and lost restraint has no particular direction. An identity account expects action to be governed by a norm, and a norm is a set of limits as much as a licence. That is why the useful question to ask of any crowd event is not "how bad did it get" but what did they do, to whom, and what did they leave alone.

Indiscriminate treatment can manufacture the category

The most consequential move in the model is the one that runs between presets 1 and 2. Nothing about the people changes. What changes is whether they are dealt with as individuals or handled as a single mass - and being handled as a single mass is itself an argument that they are one. Once "people being pushed back" becomes a description that everyone present recognises, sections of the crowd who wanted nothing to do with a confrontation are inside a category that now has a reason to have one. This is the model's sharpest practical claim, and it is why the model has had consequences for public order training.

Anonymity amplifies a norm rather than removing one

In the model, being anonymous in the mass does not add indiscriminate action; it makes whatever norm is in play more dominant. Set the norm to "confrontation of any kind is out of bounds", make the crowd anonymous, and the crowd becomes more restrained, not less. That is the direction the social identity model of deindividuation effects predicts, and it is the point at which the classic account and its successor part company.

And a case where the other account fits better

Preset 3 removes the common category and the shared norm, and the result is action scattered almost evenly across every available target - a selectivity index around 0.08. That is not a defect in the model. It is the case that a deindividuation account describes well and an identity account has to work hardest to explain, and the fact that it exists is why this remains a live argument rather than a settled one.

Where the problem is located, and who benefits

These are not just two hypotheses. An account that places the cause inside crowd members makes the crowd the problem and the policing the response, and recommends containment. An account that places the cause in the relationship makes the policing a candidate cause and recommends differentiation, communication and facilitation. Both are substantive positions with consequences for what gets funded, trained and reviewed after an event - and noticing that is part of reading the literature, not a substitute for it.

What this does not demonstrate

  • Nothing here is evidence for either account. The model was written to make two theories argue in a way you can point at. It produces the patterns it was built to produce, and the evidence about real crowds is in the literature, not on this page.
  • Marlow Bridge and Calder Green are inventions. No real event, policing operation, organisation or person is described, and no number here corresponds to anything reported anywhere.
  • Deindividuation is not refuted. It has a serious modern successor in the social identity model of deindividuation effects, which keeps the interest in anonymity and changes what anonymity is taken to do. Treating the classic account as simply wrong is a caricature of an active research tradition.
  • The elaborated model does not predict peaceful crowds. It predicts norm-governed crowds. Whether that is peaceful depends entirely on the norm, and preset 2 and preset 4 differ in nothing except that.
  • A model cannot settle a causal question. That indiscriminate policing produces unity in this model is true by construction. Whether it does so in the world is an empirical question with a real and contested literature behind it.
  • "The norm" is doing a lot of work. Here you set it with a control. In a real event nobody can see it directly, and the risk of reading it backwards off what the crowd did is the standard criticism of this kind of account.
  • Six sections is a simplification. Real crowds contain far more, overlapping, shifting, and disagreeing with each other throughout.
