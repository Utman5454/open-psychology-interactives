# Teaching notes - Crowd Behaviour Laboratory: Deindividuation and ESIM

`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/09-crowd-deindividuation-vs-esim/`

**What it teaches.** Deindividuation and the elaborated social
identity model make genuinely different predictions about the same
crowd. The manipulation that tells them apart is whether anonymity
sharpens participation onto one target or spreads it evenly:
indiscriminate policing that creates a shared category changes the
pattern of who takes part far more than anonymity alone does, and no
single run refutes either account.

**Before students start.** Read aloud that Marlow Bridge is entirely
fictional; no real incident, policing operation or dataset is
described.

**What students do.**
1. Predict what happens when a crowd is pushed back as a single mass,
   regardless of what any individual was doing.
2. Set five situational controls, or load one of four presets, and
   read how many of six sections took part and how concentrated the
   action was on one target.
3. Read both accounts' verdicts side by side and where they diverge,
   then judge five observations from a different fictional event
   against both.

**What to look for.** Running the indiscriminate-policing preset with
and without a shared category leaves the deindividuation account's own
index nearly unchanged while the pattern of who took part and what
they targeted changes completely. Anonymity alone does not do what
indiscriminate, category-forming policing does.

**Debrief.** Which of the five available manipulations would let you
tell the two accounts apart, and why?

**Common misconception / caution.** The elaborated model does not
predict crowds are peaceful, and deindividuation research finding
small or inconsistent effects is not the same as the account being
disproved. One run built into this activity is deliberately a case
the classic account describes better than the usual identity
prediction does.

**Use in class / timing.** Comparing one pair of presets makes the
point in about 10 minutes. The full sequence with the closing
challenge runs to about 35.
