# Teaching notes — Crowd Behaviour Laboratory: Deindividuation and ESIM

`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/09-crowd-deindividuation-vs-esim/`

Presets 1 and 2 are the same crowd. Nothing about the people changes between
them. What changes is how they were treated.

---

## Running it from the front

Run presets 1 and 2 back to back and say nothing except: the crowd is the same crowd. Nothing about the people changed between those two runs. What changed was how they were treated, and six sections took part instead of one.

Then run preset 4, where a crowd unites completely and does almost nothing. Students routinely arrive believing that the identity account predicts crowds will be violent. It predicts that crowds will act in line with a shared norm, and a shared norm often forbids exactly the behaviour people expect.

Finish on preset 3, which is the honest one. With no common category and no shared norm, action scatters across every target and the selectivity index falls to about 0.08. That is the pattern a deindividuation account describes best, and saying so out loud is what stops this becoming a tool that argues for one side.

## Intended level

Second-year undergraduate on a social psychology strand, or any group meeting
crowd psychology beyond the textbook paragraph on deindividuation. No statistics
needed. It follows tool 07 (a norm forming under ambiguity) and tool 08 (mere
categorisation) well, because it is the point at which categorisation and norms
stop being laboratory curiosities and start doing explanatory work on a public
event.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. state what each account predicts about crowd action;
2. explain that the disagreement is about the *patterning* of action, not its
   existence;
3. describe how indiscriminate treatment can create the category it responds to;
4. distinguish anonymity amplifying a salient norm from anonymity removing
   restraint;
5. name a case the deindividuation account describes better, and say why that
   keeps the argument live.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 8 minutes using presets 1, 2, 4 and 3 in that
  order.
- **Students exploring the settings themselves:** 20 minutes.
- **With the five-observation challenge and the debrief:** 40–45 minutes.

## Preparation

Read the two account panels at the top of the page before the session; they are
short and they are what the whole activity assumes. Also read the two criticism
panels below the laboratory, because the most common failure of this topic in
teaching is presenting the elaborated model as the correct answer and
deindividuation as a discredited relic. It is not a relic: it has a serious
modern successor in the social identity model of deindividuation effects, and
that successor keeps the interest in anonymity while changing what anonymity is
taken to do.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

Run the presets in the order **1, 2, 4, 3**.

1. **Preset 1 — differentiated policing.** 1 of 6 sections took part, selectivity
   0.84. The handful who came for a confrontation stay a handful.
2. **Preset 2 — indiscriminate policing.** 6 of 6 sections took part, selectivity
   still 0.84. Say nothing except: *this is the same crowd.* Participation
   multiplied by six; what they did stayed aimed at the same target.
3. **Preset 4 — united, and the norm forbids it.** 1 of 6, and the crowd is more
   unified than in preset 2. This is the answer to "so the identity account says
   crowds turn violent". It says crowds act in line with a norm, and this norm
   forbids exactly the behaviour students expect.
4. **Preset 3 — no common category, anonymous.** 2 of 6, selectivity 0.08, action
   spread almost evenly across everything. Say out loud that this is the shape a
   deindividuation account describes best. That sentence is what stops the tool
   arguing for one side.

Then put presets 2 and 3 on screen together and read the two numbers:
disinhibition **0.70** against **0.67** — near enough identical — with selectivity
**0.84** against **0.08**. The quantity one account tracks cannot tell those two
afternoons apart.

## Prediction question

*A crowd is pushed back as a single mass, regardless of what any individual was
doing. What would you expect to happen next?*

"More of the crowd takes part, and what they do stays aimed at the police" is the
elaborated model's two-part prediction. "Restraint collapses and people attack
whatever is nearest" is the deindividuation prediction and is treated as a real
answer, not a wrong one — the laboratory can produce that shape, and preset 3
does.

## Activity sequence

1. **Read the two account panels**, then commit to the prediction. It gates the
   laboratory.
2. **Run presets 1 and 2** and compare the two numbers in the headline.
3. **Change one control at a time** from preset 2 — set the response to
   *reasonable*, or the category to *not available* — and watch participation
   collapse. Legitimacy and category availability are doing as much work as the
   policing.
4. **Run preset 4** and then **preset 3**.
5. **Open "Who took part, section by section"** and check the arithmetic against
   the model below.
6. **Read the two criticism panels.**
7. **Judge the five observations** from Calder Green.

## Debrief questions

1. Between presets 1 and 2, what changed about the people in the crowd?
2. Preset 2 produced six sections taking part and 88 per cent of the action aimed
   at one target. Which account predicts each of those two facts?
3. In preset 4 the crowd is completely unified and almost nothing happens. What
   does that tell you about the claim that the identity account predicts
   disorder?
4. Preset 3 has a selectivity of 0.08. What would an identity theorist have to
   say about it, and how much extra work is that?
5. In the model, anonymity sharpens whatever norm is in play. What would you have
   to observe to tell that apart from anonymity removing restraint?
6. One account locates the cause inside crowd members; the other locates it in
   the relationship between crowd and police. What does each recommend be
   changed, and who is asked to change it?
7. You set "the norm in play" with a control. In a real event, how would anyone
   know what it was — and what is the risk in reading it off what the crowd did?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "Deindividuation has been disproved." | The criticism panel. Reviews find the classic effects small and inconsistent, which is not the same as disproof, and the SIDE model is its living successor. |
| "ESIM says crowds are peaceful." | Preset 4 against preset 2. Identical unity, opposite outcome, because the norm differs. The model predicts norm-governed action, not peaceful action. |
| "Anonymity makes people worse." | Preset 4 is anonymous and restrained. In the model anonymity sharpens whatever norm is salient, which is the SIDE prediction. |
| "So it's the police's fault." | The model makes policing a *candidate* cause. That is a substantive claim with evidence behind it, not a verdict, and treating a model output as blame is exactly the error the page warns about. |
| "The simulation shows ESIM is right." | Nothing here is evidence for either account. The model produces what it was built to produce, and the page says so three times. |
| "Crowd action is always targeted." | Preset 3, and observation 5 in the challenge. Some events really are diffuse, and an account built on selectivity has to work harder there. |
| "The two accounts are just different words for the same thing." | Presets 2 and 3 have almost the same disinhibition index and completely different patterns. One number cannot tell them apart; that is what makes them different theories. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **Nothing here is evidence.** The model is a device for making two theories
  argue in a way a room can point at.
- **Marlow Bridge and Calder Green are inventions.** No real event, operation,
  organisation or person is described, and no number corresponds to anything
  reported anywhere.
- **A model cannot settle a causal question.** That indiscriminate policing
  produces unity *in this model* is true by construction.
- **"The norm" is doing a lot of work**, and here you set it with a control. The
  risk of reading it backwards off what the crowd did is the standard criticism
  of this kind of account, and it is on the page.
- **Six sections is a simplification.** Real crowds contain far more, overlapping
  and disagreeing throughout.
- **Neither account is presented as settled.** Both criticism panels are part of
  the material, not an appendix.

## Accessibility considerations

- Nothing is timed and nothing is scored.
- All controls are native radios in labelled fieldsets and labelled selects.
- The target chart is hidden from assistive technology and paired with a sentence
  giving all four shares, the even-spread reference and the selectivity value.
  Every share is also printed beside its own bar.
- The even-spread reference is a dashed rule *and* a printed label — never colour
  alone. The police-line bar is drawn differently *and* named in words.
- The section table marks participation with a Yes/No column; the heavier left
  rule is a redundant second cue only.
- Every change is announced through the shell's polite live region, including
  which preset was loaded and what it produced.
- Focus moves to the laboratory heading when the prediction is recorded. Targets
  are at least 2.75rem. Forced-colours rules provided. No horizontal page scroll
  at 320px, 375px, 1366px, 1440px or 1920px.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Find the zero.** There is a setting in which no section takes part at all.
   Find it, then write 150 words on why a literature built only on the events
   that did not end that way is a biased sample.
2. **Design the falsifying observation.** Write down one thing you could observe
   at a real event that the elaborated model predicts and the deindividuation
   account does not, and one that goes the other way. Then say how you would
   record it without already knowing the answer.
3. **Specify the norm in advance.** The hardest criticism of the identity account
   is that the norm is read off the outcome. Propose a method for establishing
   what a crowd's norm is *before* anything happens.
4. **Follow the money.** Each account implies a different thing to fund and
   train. Write 300 words on what a public-order review would recommend under
   each, and who is asked to change their behaviour.

## The model

Documented in full at the top of `tool.js`. Nothing is random; every number below
is reproducible from a fresh load.

Baselines: residents 0.20, campaign regulars 0.40, stallholders 0.12, students
0.28, families 0.04, the handful 0.88. A section takes part when

    baseline + unity x legitimacy + norm shift  >=  0.60

| Factor | Setting | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unity | differentiated policing | 0.10 |
| | indiscriminate, no common category | 0.55 |
| | indiscriminate, common category | 1.00 |
| Legitimacy | read as reasonable | 0.12 |
| | read as unfair | 0.50 |
| Norm shift | police line only | +0.18 |
| | no shared norm | 0.00 |
| | confrontation out of bounds | −0.45 |

The norm shift applies only to sections inside the category, which means unity of
at least 0.55 — and the handful who came for a confrontation are absorbed only at
unity 1.00.

Target weights by norm, before identifiability: police-line-only
0.88 / 0.05 / 0.02 / 0.05; no shared norm 0.33 / 0.27 / 0.18 / 0.22; out of bounds
0.45 / 0.22 / 0.13 / 0.20 (police line, shop fronts, community centre, other
crowd members). Anonymity raises each weight to the power 1.35 and renormalises
where a norm is in play, and otherwise moves the weights 40 per cent towards 0.25.

Selectivity is the summed absolute deviation from 0.25 divided by 1.5. The
disinhibition index is 0.2 (identifiable) or 0.5 (anonymous) plus the
participation count divided by 12. Both are teaching devices invented for this
page.

**Reference values a lecturer can check.**

| Preset | Took part | Selectivity | Disinhibition | Target shares |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Differentiated policing | 1 of 6 | 0.84 | 0.28 | 88 / 5 / 2 / 5 |
| 2. Indiscriminate, common category | 6 of 6 | 0.84 | 0.70 | 88 / 5 / 2 / 5 |
| 3. No common category, anonymous | 2 of 6 | 0.08 | 0.67 | 30 / 26 / 21 / 23 |
| 4. United, norm forbids it | 1 of 6 | 0.37 | 0.58 | 53 / 20 / 10 / 18 |

Rows 2 and 3 are the pair to put on screen: near-identical disinhibition,
completely different patterns.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Reicher (1984, 1996)** for the St Pauls analysis and the social identity
  account of crowd action.
- **Drury and Reicher (2000)** and **Stott and Reicher (1998)** for the elaborated
  model and the argument that indiscriminate treatment unifies a crowd.
- **Stott, Adang, Livingstone and Schreiber (2007, 2008)** for the applied
  public-order work, which is where the model has had practical consequences.
- **Zimbardo (1969)** and **Diener (1979)** for the classic deindividuation
  formulations.
- **Postmes and Spears (1998)** for the meta-analysis that found the classic
  effects small and inconsistent, and **Reicher, Spears and Postmes (1995)** for
  the SIDE model that succeeded them.
- **Le Bon (1895)** as the historical ancestor of the crowd-as-problem framing,
  worth reading for its politics as much as its psychology.
- **Schweingruber and Wohlstein (2005)** on the durability of crowd myths in
  textbooks.

Full references are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the invented
numbers are not mistaken for anything derived from them.
