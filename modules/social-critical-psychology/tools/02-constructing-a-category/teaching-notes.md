# Teaching notes — Constructing a Category

`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/02-constructing-a-category/`

Nothing is discovered between stage 1 and stage 5. By the end there is a name, a
line, an instrument, a figure and a set of careers. That is the lesson.

---

## Intended level

First- or second-year undergraduate on a social, critical or conceptual-issues
strand. No statistics are needed: the simulated population does the arithmetic
and the learner only moves a threshold. It pairs naturally with the Epistemology
Lens Switch (tool 01), which handles the prior question of what the object of
study is.

It is also an unusually good session for students who have decided that "socially
constructed" is either an insult or a magic word.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. name the steps at which a description hardens into a thing;
2. explain why a prevalence figure follows from a threshold;
3. say what a category name imports before any evidence exists;
4. recognise criterion contamination in an instrument;
5. state clearly why constructed does not mean unreal, and what critical realism
   adds to the point.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 8 minutes — the threshold slider in stage 3,
  and nothing else.
- **Students in pairs, all five stages:** 35 minutes.
- **With the challenge and the debrief:** 50 minutes.

## Preparation

None. Have someone read two or three of the nine accounts aloud before anyone
touches the tool, so the material lands as description rather than as data.

If you have fifteen minutes, run stages 1 and 3 only.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

Press **Worked example**, then go back to **stage 3**. Read the prevalence aloud
at each threshold, slowly, and say each time: *not one of the four hundred
answers has changed.*

With the worked example's feature set (feeling behind, dread before messages,
starting before finishing, staying up late, unrefreshing tiredness) and the
default duration and interference rules, the figures are:

| Features required | Prevalence |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | 41.3% (165 of 400) |
| 2 | 37.8% (151) |
| 3 | 28.0% (112) |
| 4 | 17.3% (69) |
| 5 | 7.0% (28) |
| 6 | 0.0% (0 — more features required than were selected) |

Then ask which of those numbers a newspaper would print, and which the training
company would prefer.

The second demonstration, if there is time, is **stage 1**: add *long or
unpredictable working hours* to the feature set and watch the verdict change from
"every feature you have chosen is a property of a person" to "you have included a
feature of an arrangement". Point out that nothing forced the first choice.

## Prediction question

*The team will turn these accounts into a category and then estimate how many
people have it. What will most determine that figure?*

Cohorts usually split between "how common the experience actually is" and "how
good the questionnaire is". The tool's answer — where the boundary is drawn — is
demonstrated rather than asserted in stage 3.

## Activity sequence

1. **Read the nine accounts.** They are the whole of what exists.
2. **Commit to the prediction.** It gates the laboratory.
3. **Stage 1, find the pattern.** The two count columns are the point: the most
   common features are the least distinguishing.
4. **Stage 2, name it.** Six options, including refusing to name it. Read the
   "what recedes" line for each.
5. **Stage 3, draw the boundary.** Slider, then the sensitivity table.
6. **Stage 4, build the instrument.** Five items from ten. Note who is missed.
7. **Stage 5, let it loose.** Five institutional decisions, each with an
   uncritical route, an accountable route and a refusal.
8. **Go back and change one thing.** Stage 1 is the most instructive return.
9. **The five claims.** Two of them are marked against the learner's own stage 1
   choices, so different tables get different verdicts.

## Debrief questions

1. At which stage did the accounts stop being accounts?
2. "Tiredness that sleep does not fix" appears in all nine, and in five of the
   nine comparison accounts. Why is it nevertheless the feature everyone selects?
3. What changed about the nine people when you moved the threshold?
4. Account 7 is about a rota. Which of the six names has anywhere to put it?
5. The worked example takes the accountable route at every institutional step and
   still reaches "a thing people are said to have". What would have had to be
   different?
6. Somebody in the class says the category is "just a social construct". What is
   wrong with the word "just"?
7. If people learn the category and start describing themselves in its terms,
   later accounts will fit it better. Is that evidence for the category?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "So the category is fake." | The caution panel, and the debrief section "Constructed, and real". The nine people describe something. The question is whether this is a good carving of it. |
| "The prevalence is what it is; we just measured it." | Stage 3. Same 400 people, six different answers. |
| "A questionnaire makes it objective." | Stage 4. Five items, two of the original nine no longer detected, and five of five items restating the criteria. |
| "The name is just a label." | Stage 2, the "what intervention follows" line for each of six names, all before any evidence. |
| "Reification is something careless people do." | The worked example takes the careful option five times and still lands at 68 of 100. |
| "This only applies to contested categories." | Ask which uncontested category was never constructed. Then ask what makes the difference between a good construction and a bad one. |
| "Including 'working hours' is cheating — that's not a symptom." | Exactly the question. Who decided that the category may only contain properties of persons, and when? |

## Limitations and cautions

- **This is not an argument against classification.** Naming something people
  could not previously name is a real gain, and every science begins by sorting.
  The activity is about hardening, not about whether to classify.
- **No real diagnosis or construct is described.** The category, its name, its
  criteria, its items and its prevalence are invented. Nothing here should be
  read as a claim about any published category or the people who use it.
- **The simulated population is a model.** 400 respondents from a documented
  latent-trait model with a fixed seed. The prevalence figures show how a
  threshold behaves; they estimate nothing about anybody.
- **Nobody is assessed.** The questionnaire is never administered to the person
  using the tool, and no score is produced for anyone.
- **The object-status index is a teaching device.** It is not a measurement, and
  the numbers attached to each institutional decision are chosen for teaching.
- **The five institutional events are stylised.** Real uptake is slower, more
  contested, and often resisted from inside the institution.
- **Handle the room.** Some students will recognise themselves in the accounts.
  Nothing in the session asks anyone to disclose anything, and it is worth saying
  so before you begin.

## Accessibility considerations

- Native checkboxes, radios, one range and two selects. No dragging, no timing,
  no hover-only content.
- Each stage exposes only its own controls and its own result, which keeps the
  controls column short enough for the pinned primary result to remain visible at
  1366×768. The pinned block is 265px in the worked example.
- Both charts are hidden from assistive technology. The boundary chart prints
  each band's count beside its bar and is paired with a table of counts and
  percentages; the object-status bar is paired with a sentence naming the band.
  No band is identified by fill alone.
- The two counts for every feature are printed on the checkbox label, so the
  judgement in stage 1 never requires reading across to the table.
- Focus moves to the new stage heading when a stage button is pressed.
- Announcements go through the shell's polite live region, including the
  consequence text when an institutional decision is made.
- Reduced-motion and forced-colours rules are provided. No horizontal scroll at
  320px, 375px, 1366px, 1440px or 1920px.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **The other instrument.** Build a five-item questionnaire that finds accounts
   7 and 9. What has it become a questionnaire about?
2. **Two press releases.** Write the "one in five" version and the honest
   version. Which one gets printed, and what would have to change for the other
   one to?
3. **Reverse it.** Take a category you consider obviously real and work backwards
   through the five stages. Where were its thresholds set, by whom, and when were
   they last revised?
4. **The looping question.** Read Hacking on interactive kinds and write 300
   words on whether the ninth account would have been written differently in a
   world where the category was already famous.

## The model

Documented at the top of `tool.js`.

**Stage 1** is authored. Each account carries a hand-written feature list, and
each feature carries an authored count for a comparison set of nine accounts from
people who said nothing was hard to name. A feature "distinguishes" when it
appears at least three more times in the nine than in the nine comparisons.

**Stage 3** simulates 400 respondents with `mulberry32(20260807)` and Box–Muller
normal draws:

```
latent ~ N(0, 1)
feature f present  when  u < logistic(intercept_f + slope_f * latent)
months             = max(0, round(3 + 3.2*latent + 1.5*N(0,1)))
interference       = 2 + 1.25*latent + N(0,1)
meets criteria     = (#selected features present >= threshold)
                     AND months >= duration rule
                     AND interference >= interference rule
```

Duration rules are 0, 1 and 6 months; interference rules are none, 2.5 and 4.0.
"Just outside" means failing by exactly one of the three conditions.

**Stage 4** detects an account when it would endorse three or more of the five
chosen items. Items map one-to-one onto features. Accounts 7 and 9 are written so
that the five most obvious items (dread, behind, switching, staying up late,
tiredness) miss them.

**Stage 5** starts the object-status index at 12, adds the chosen name's
hardening value (0 for refusing to name, 8 for the purely descriptive name, up to
22 for the medical one) and then the deltas of the five institutional decisions.
One option — writing to the funder to object to the framing — subtracts 4.

### Reference values a lecturer can check

- Worked example: object status **68 of 100**, "a thing people are said to have";
  prevalence **28.0%**; accounts **7 and 9** not detected; **5 of 5** items
  restate a criterion.
- Every institutional decision taken uncritically, with the medical name:
  **100 of 100**, "an administrative fact".
- Every institutional decision refused, with the physiological name: **30 of
  100**, "a working construct inside one research group".
- Challenge answers with the worked-example feature set: 1 decision, 2 finding,
  3 finding, 4 unsupportable, 5 unsupportable. Add *sleeping badly* or *working
  hours* to the stage 1 selection and claims 2 or 3 become "a decision reported
  as a finding".

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Hacking (1995, 1999)** on interactive kinds and looping effects — the reason
  a category's fit with people's accounts is weak evidence once the category is
  well known.
- **Hacking (1999), *The Social Construction of What?*** for the discipline of
  asking *what* is being said to be constructed: the idea, the person, or the
  experience.
- **Bowker and Star (2000), *Sorting Things Out*** for classification as
  infrastructure, and for what happens to the people who fall between categories
  — which is stage 4 in one sentence.
- **Berger and Luckmann (1966)** for the original account of externalisation,
  objectivation and internalisation, which is the shape of the five stages.
- **Rose (1998), *Inventing Our Selves*** on psychology's role in producing the
  objects it then studies.
- **Danziger (1997), *Naming the Mind*** on how psychological categories acquired
  their names and what those names imported.
- **Bhaskar (1979)** and **Sayer (2000)** for the critical realist position used
  in the debrief: our categories are fallible human products, and there is still
  something they are categories of.
- **Kirk and Kutchins (1992)** and **Cooper (2014)** for careful, non-polemical
  treatments of how diagnostic criteria and thresholds get set, if you want a
  real-world companion reading.

Full references are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the invented
category is not mistaken for anything derived from them.
