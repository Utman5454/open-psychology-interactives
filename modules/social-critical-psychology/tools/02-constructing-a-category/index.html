Social and Critical Psychology

Constructing a Category

Nine messy fictional accounts and five decisions. By the end there is a named condition with a boundary, a questionnaire, a prevalence figure, a training course and a place in somebody's spreadsheet - and nothing new has been discovered.

Simulated - fictional accounts, a fictional survey population and an invented category

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to say where a classification acquires the appearance of a natural object, and why “socially constructed” does not mean “made up”.

About 35 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

Construction is not fabrication

The nine people describe something. They are tired, they dread opening their messages, they stay up late for an hour of quiet. Nothing in this activity suggests they are inventing it, and nothing here is a criticism of anyone who finds a category useful for naming their own experience. The question is a different one: what happens to a description once it is given a name, a threshold, an instrument and an institutional user - and what stops being asked as a result.

How to use this

  1. Read the nine accounts.
  2. Commit to an answer to the question below.
  3. Work through the five stages in order. Each one exposes only its own controls and its own result.
  4. At the end, go back and change one decision - the threshold is the most instructive - and watch what moves.

The material

A fictional research team collected open-ended responses to the question "Is there anything about daily life at the moment that you find difficult to put a name to?" Nine of them are below. They have not been coded, grouped or tidied. This is the whole of what exists before the category does.

    All nine are invented for teaching. No real respondent, dataset, survey or published category is reproduced.

    Key terms
    Reification
    Treating the product of a classification as a thing that was there all along waiting to be found.
    Boundary
    The rule deciding who is inside the category: how many features, for how long, with how much interference.
    Prevalence
    How many people the category contains. It depends on where the boundary was put as much as on the people.

        Challenge - five sentences about your category

        Each of these could be written about the category you have just built. Decide what each one is: a finding, a decision reported as a finding, or a claim the work cannot support at all.

        What this demonstrates

        Reification has identifiable steps

        "Reification" is often taught as an attitude - treating an abstraction as a thing - and then left there. This activity breaks it into moves you can point at. Selecting features fixes what will count. Naming imports a frame and an expert. Setting a threshold converts a continuum into two kinds of people. Building an instrument makes the category operationally prior to the experience. Institutional uptake makes it costly to question, because budgets, job titles and training contracts now depend on it existing.

        Prevalence is manufactured, not discovered

        Nothing about the 400 simulated respondents changes when you move the threshold. Their answers are fixed. What changes is where the line is, and the prevalence figure follows from the line. Every real prevalence estimate has this property, which is why the respectable ones report their criteria and their sensitivity analyses, and why a headline figure without them tells you almost nothing.

        The name does work before any evidence arrives

        "Syndrome" recruits clinicians and points at treatment. "Dependence" recruits a moral vocabulary and points at the individual's willpower. "Poverty" recruits a distributional vocabulary and points at how a resource is shared out. These are not neutral labels for the same object; they make different things askable, and they nominate different people as the ones who know.

        Instruments police their own boundary

        Once a five-item questionnaire exists, the people it does not detect are, administratively, people who do not have the thing - however they describe their lives. Two of the nine accounts are written to fall outside a conventional instrument. Institutionally they now have no name for what they said, which is the opposite of what the category was built to provide.

        Constructed, and real

        None of this shows that the nine people are mistaken. Critical realism puts it well: our categories are fallible, historically situated human products, and there is still something they are categories of. A well-made category can carve that something usefully; a badly made one carves it in a way that serves the institution doing the carving. The interesting question is never "is it socially constructed" - everything in this vocabulary is - but "constructed how, by whom, at what cost, and what would a better construction look like".

        Categories loop back

        People learn the category, recognise themselves in it, and start describing their experience in its terms. Later accounts then confirm it. That loop is not a scandal. It is how a shared vocabulary for inner life ever gets built. But it does mean that the fit between a category and the accounts people give is weak evidence for the category once the category is well known.

        What this does not demonstrate

        • It does not show that categories are bogus. Classification is how any science gets started, and a category that names something people could not previously name is a real gain. The activity is about how classification can harden, not about whether to classify.
        • It is not an argument about any real diagnosis or construct. The category built here is invented, its name is invented, and no published criteria, instrument, item or prevalence estimate is reproduced or referred to.
        • The simulated population is a model, not data. The 400 respondents are generated from a documented latent-trait model with a fixed seed. The prevalence figures show how a threshold behaves; they estimate nothing about anybody.
        • Nobody is assessed. The questionnaire is never administered to you. The tool asks nothing about your own experience and produces no score for anyone.
        • Institutional uptake is not automatic. The five events are stylised, and each offers a route that keeps the category accountable. Real uptake is slower, contested, and often resisted from inside the institution.
        • Being hard to measure is not evidence of being unreal. Several things in this vocabulary - grief, loneliness, exhaustion - are difficult to operationalise and obviously real. Difficulty of measurement is a fact about instruments.
