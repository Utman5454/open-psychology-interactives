# Teaching notes — Minimal Group Allocation Laboratory

`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/08-minimal-group-positive-distinctiveness/`

The option to watch for is the one that gives your own category *fewer* points.
If anyone takes it, the thing being pursued is not gain.

---

## Running it from the front

The option to look for is always the one that gives your own category fewer points than the "own group most" option does. If a room chooses it, the thing being pursued is not gain; it is being further ahead. That distinction is the entire empirical contribution of the paradigm, and it is easy to lose if the options are read quickly.

Run the equal condition first and the "already ahead" condition second. The same choice means something different in the two: in the first it creates a difference, in the second it widens an existing one. Ask the room whether their reasoning changed, and whether it should have.

Expect a substantial number of fair allocations. That is not a failed demonstration - fairness is the single most common strategy in the real literature too, and the finding was always about a reliable pull away from it rather than about its absence.

## Intended level

First- or second-year undergraduate meeting the minimal group paradigm and social
identity theory. No statistics needed. It sits well after tool 07 in this module
(a norm forming out of nothing) and before tool 09 (crowds), because all three
turn on the difference between a laboratory demonstration of a mechanism and an
explanation of a social phenomenon.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. distinguish the four allocation strategies and say what each reveals about the
   allocator's goal;
2. explain why maximum difference rather than maximum ingroup gain is the finding
   that matters;
3. describe positive distinctiveness as a comparative claim about identity;
4. state why an allocation pattern between two invented categories is not an
   explanation of prejudice or of structural inequality;
5. judge which conclusions this kind of evidence supports, partly supports and
   does not support.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 6 minutes using the worked example.
- **Students individually, six allocations in two conditions:** 20 minutes.
- **With the five-claim challenge and the debrief:** 35–40 minutes.

## Preparation

Do it yourself first and notice how easy it is to read the options quickly and
miss the arithmetic. That is exactly what happens in a room, and it is why every
option on the page prints its total and its signed difference underneath.

Say before anyone starts that nothing is stored, nothing is scored and no profile
is produced for any person. Some students expect a "how prejudiced are you"
result and need telling clearly that no such result exists here or anywhere
honest.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

Press **Worked example**. It runs one plausible profile — three fair allocations,
one own-group-most and two largest-lead — in the equal condition *and* in the
already-ahead condition, and opens the comparison table.

Then point at three things in order:

1. **The two largest-lead choices.** Go back to allocation 2 and read both rows
   aloud: own-group-most gives the Kestrel 20 points; largest lead gives the
   Kestrel 15. Ask what was bought with the five points that were given up.
2. **The strategy profile.** Fairness is still the most-used strategy. That is
   the honest picture, not a failed demonstration.
3. **The comparison table.** The two rows have identical strategy counts. The gap
   column reads **+0 to +25** in the equal condition and **+300 to +325** in the
   already-ahead condition.

Say while pointing at the last one: *a study that reported only the strategy
profile would report the same result in both worlds.*

## Prediction question

*What do people most often do?*

"Split everything equally" is the most common honest answer and is treated
generously by the feedback: fairness really is the single most-used strategy.
"Give their own category as much as possible" is the most useful wrong one — it
is close enough to feel right and misses the entire point, and the feedback sends
the student back to look for the option that gives their own side less.

## Activity sequence

1. **Commit to the prediction.** It gates the laboratory.
2. **Answer the sorting question.** The basis is a pattern preference and the
   page says immediately that it was decided by nothing.
3. **Make six allocations in the equal condition.** Read the detail line under
   each option before choosing.
4. **Read the outcome verdict**, which names what your own profile does and does
   not separate.
5. **Change the starting position** to *your category ahead* and make the same
   six choices again. The comparison table opens once two conditions are done.
6. **Judge the five claims** in the challenge.
7. **Read the debrief and the limitations panel.**

## Debrief questions

1. In allocation 2, the largest-lead option gave your own category five fewer
   points than the own-group-most option. What was bought with them?
2. Nobody could allocate to themselves and the points buy nothing. Which
   explanations of discrimination does that rule out?
3. Fairness was probably your most-used strategy. Does that weaken the finding?
4. You made the same six choices in two worlds. Did your reasoning change? Should
   it have?
5. The strategy profile is identical in both rows of the comparison table. What
   is it failing to record?
6. Kestrels have no history, no property, no institutions and nothing has ever
   been done to them. Which parts of real intergroup relations does that remove?
7. Is "ingroup favouring" the same thing as "outgroup hostility"? What evidence
   would tell them apart, and could an allocation task provide it?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "This explains racism." | Claim 2 in the challenge. Prejudice has beliefs, feelings and a history; the paradigm removes all three by design. What it does is rule out accounts that *require* prior hostility or competition. |
| "People just want their group to win." | Then they would take own-group-most every time. The largest-lead option costs their own side points. That is the whole contribution. |
| "Most people were fair, so the effect isn't real." | The finding was always a reliable pull away from fairness, not its absence. A study where everyone was unfair would be less interesting and less believable. |
| "Structural inequality is the sum of individual biases." | Claim 3. Pay gaps and housing outcomes are produced by arrangements that keep working whatever any individual allocates. The already-ahead condition is a hint at the direction of explanation. |
| "So I found out I'm biased." | Claim 5. Six choices between invented categories, nothing stored, no score produced. A tool that told you about your character from this would be a tool to distrust. |
| "The already-ahead condition shows privilege." | It stipulates a number. Belonging to an advantaged group involves knowing why, for how long and at whose expense — none of which is on the page, and the page says so. |
| "The paradigm is settled." | It is not. The response options constrain what can be expressed, participants may be reading the task as being about what is wanted of them, and allocating may be the only available way of doing anything at all. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **Not an explanation of prejudice.** A minimum condition is not an explanation
  of what happens under maximum conditions.
- **Not an explanation of structural inequality.** Nothing here bears on how
  institutional arrangements are produced or maintained.
- **Not a measurement of the learner.** No score for any person is produced and
  nothing is stored.
- **The three starting positions are this tool's addition**, not part of the
  classic paradigm, and they are stipulated rather than earned.
- **The matrices are original.** No published allocation matrix, instruction set
  or dataset is reproduced, and no number on the page corresponds to any
  published result.
- **Ingroup favouring is not outgroup hostility**, and an allocation task cannot
  distinguish them.

## Accessibility considerations

- Nothing is timed and nothing is scored.
- Every allocation option is a native radio in a labelled fieldset and prints its
  full arithmetic — points to each side, total, and signed difference — so no
  choice depends on reading the chart or on colour.
- The strategy chart is hidden from assistive technology and paired with a
  sentence naming all four counts and the running totals. The largest-lead bar is
  drawn differently *and* named in words.
- The comparison table is a real table with row headers inside a scroll wrapper.
- The progress checklist marks each item met or not yet in text.
- Category assignment, every recorded allocation and every condition change are
  announced through the shell's polite live region.
- Focus moves to the laboratory heading when the prediction is recorded. Targets
  are at least 2.75rem. Forced-colours rules provided. No horizontal page scroll
  at 320px, 375px, 1366px, 1440px or 1920px.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Design the missing condition.** What would you have to add to the situation
   before an allocation result could bear on real discrimination? Write the
   procedure, then say what new ethical problem you have created.
2. **The other direction.** Run the *behind* condition. Favouring your own
   category there narrows a gap. Write 200 words on whether the same measure
   should be given the same name in both worlds.
3. **Read the critics.** Take one published criticism of the paradigm and say
   precisely which of the five claims in the challenge it would change.
4. **Beyond allocation.** Social identity theory predicts other routes to
   positive distinctiveness — leaving the group, redefining the comparison,
   changing the dimension of comparison. Which of these could an allocation
   matrix ever detect?

## The model

Documented at the top of `tool.js`. Nothing is random and no respondents are
simulated: the profile is only the learner's own six decisions.

The six matrices are original. In each one the largest-lead option gives the
learner's own category **fewer** points than the own-group-most option does:

| Allocation | Fair | Largest total | Own group most | Largest lead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 18 / 18 | 23 / 26 | 25 / 22 | 19 / 7 |
| 2 | 14 / 14 | 17 / 21 | 20 / 18 | 15 / 5 |
| 3 | 21 / 21 | 26 / 30 | 29 / 26 | 22 / 9 |
| 4 | 12 / 12 | 16 / 19 | 18 / 15 | 13 / 3 |
| 5 | 17 / 17 | 22 / 25 | 24 / 21 | 18 / 6 |
| 6 | 15 / 15 | 19 / 23 | 22 / 19 | 16 / 4 |

(Own category first, other category second. The largest-total option always has
the highest sum; the largest-lead option always has the largest signed
difference and always gives the own category less than own-group-most does.)

Starting positions: equal 100 / 100; ahead 400 / 100; behind 100 / 400.

**Reference values a lecturer can check.** The worked example runs the pattern
`fair, largest lead, fair, own group most, largest lead, fair`. In both conditions
the profile is **3 fair, 0 largest total, 1 own group most, 2 largest lead**, and
the allocations add 105 points to the Kestrels and 80 to the Wrens. The comparison
table therefore reads:

| Starting position | Fair | Joint total | Own group most | Largest gap | Gap afterwards |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Equal start | 3 | 0 | 1 | 2 | +0 to +25 |
| Your category ahead | 3 | 0 | 1 | 2 | +300 to +325 |

Identical strategy counts, two quite different acts. That is the row pair to put
on the screen.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Tajfel, Billig, Bundy and Flament (1971)** for the original paradigm and the
  maximum-difference result.
- **Tajfel and Turner (1979)** for social identity theory and positive
  distinctiveness.
- **Brewer (1979, 1999)** for the argument that these effects are ingroup love
  rather than outgroup hate, which is why claim 4 in the challenge is only
  partly supported.
- **Billig (1973)** and **Gerard and Hoyt (1974)** on demand characteristics and
  the constraint imposed by the response options — the standard methodological
  criticisms.
- **Rabbie, Schot and Visser (1989)** for the behavioural interaction
  alternative, on which the effect is read as instrumental rather than
  identity-based. Worth setting against the standard textbook account.
- **Reicher (2004)** for the broader argument that laboratory intergroup
  paradigms strip out exactly the history and power that the phenomena of
  interest depend on.

Full references are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the invented
matrices are not mistaken for anything derived from them.
