# Teaching notes — Conformity Under Context

`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/04-conformity-under-context/`

The private-response condition is the whole session in one manipulation.

---

## Running it from the front

The pair worth running back to back is Classic public unanimous and One person disagrees first. The majority is still four to one; the rate collapses anyway. Unanimity is not about numbers, it is about whether the answer looks like the only answer available.

The discriminating manipulation is private written response. It moves the normative bar and leaves the informational bar almost untouched. Under the ambiguous-task preset, private responding barely helps at all, which is the cleanest way to show that the two routes are different things rather than two words for social pressure.

In experiment 2, ask what proportion of the hundred never conformed at all. It is usually about a quarter, and it is invisible in the headline figure the whole class has been reading.

## Intended level

First- or second-year undergraduate on a social psychology strand. No
statistical background is needed. It is designed to be run *after* students have
met Asch and Sherif in lectures, because its work is undoing the version of them
students remember rather than introducing them.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. distinguish the two routes by the manipulations that move each;
2. explain why unanimity is not a headcount;
3. say what identification adds that the classic account lacks;
4. read a headline percentage as a summary of a distribution;
5. explain why classic rates are not constants of the species.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 8 minutes — classic, then one dissenter,
  then private.
- **Students in pairs:** 30 minutes.
- **With the challenge and the debrief:** 45 minutes.

## Preparation

None. Ask the room for a number before opening the tool and write it on the
board; the prediction panel asks the same question and the feedback is more
useful if a public guess already exists.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

Load the presets in this order and read the three right-hand columns of the
comparison table each time:

| Preset | Conformity | Normative | Informational |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Classic public unanimous | 35% | 34% | 3% |
| One person disagrees first | 12% | 10% | 2% |
| Private written response | 14% | 12% | 3% |
| Genuinely ambiguous display | 66% | 34% | 48% |
| A group the participant is not part of | 7% | 5% | 3% |
| A visibly expert majority | 67% | 34% | 51% |

Three things to say while doing it.

1. From classic to one dissenter, the majority goes from five-to-one to
   four-to-one and the rate falls by two thirds. Unanimity is not arithmetic.
2. Private responding removes the audience. The normative bar falls; the
   informational bar does not move. That is what makes them two claims rather
   than two words.
3. Now load the ambiguous display and *then* switch the response to written. The
   rate hardly falls. These people are not caving in; they are being persuaded.

## Prediction question

*On what percentage of critical rounds will the participant go along?*

Both "around a third" and "the question is not answerable yet" are treated as
good answers, for different reasons. The feedback for "two thirds or more" is
worth reading aloud: the overestimate is itself a finding about how the classic
study is remembered.

## Activity sequence

1. **Commit to a percentage.** It gates the laboratory.
2. **Run the six presets** in the order above. Every one is kept in the table.
3. **Build one of your own.** Set the six controls, press *Record this
   condition*, and give it a name by comparing it with the presets around it.
4. **Find a condition above 80%** and one below 5%. Say what each would look
   like as a real situation.
5. **Experiment 2.** How many of the 100 never conformed? Change the number of
   rounds and watch what does and does not move.
6. **The challenge.** Five manipulations, three routes.

## Debrief questions

1. Why does one dissenter matter more than removing two confederates?
2. In the ambiguous condition, is agreeing with the group irrational?
3. The classic condition gives 35% of rounds and 13 people out of 100 who never
   conformed. Which of those numbers is "the result"?
4. Identification is not in the classic two-route model at all. What does adding
   it change about what conformity *is*?
5. Replications across decades and countries have produced a wide range of
   figures. Is that a problem for the finding, or is it the finding?
6. Nothing in this model represents what the participants thought the study was
   for. What would you have to add, and what would it predict?
7. When is conformity the right thing to do?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "Asch showed that people conform." | The classic preset: 35% of rounds, and 13 of 100 never conform. Most rounds are answered correctly. |
| "A bigger majority means more conformity." | Move the dissenter slider, then imagine adding confederates. The step from unanimous to not-unanimous is the whole effect. |
| "Informational and normative are just two words for pressure." | The private-response manipulation, run in the easy and the ambiguous conditions. |
| "Private responding shows what people really believe." | It removes the audience, not the uncertainty. In the ambiguous condition the private rate stays high, and that is not concealment. |
| "The conformity rate is about 35%." | Ask: in which condition, with which group, in which decade, on which task? |
| "Conforming is weak." | The expert-majority preset produces 67%, mostly informational, and quite possibly correct. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **No historical study is reproduced.** The task, stimuli, procedure and
  numbers are invented. The presets are named for legibility, not accuracy.
- **The parameters are chosen, not estimated.** They are printed on screen and
  above. They put the classic conditions in roughly the right order of magnitude.
- **Two routes is a simplification.** Referent informational influence,
  self-categorisation, perceived experimenter demand, politeness and boredom are
  all absent.
- **Going along is not believing.** The model estimates a public response.
- **Ethics of the original work.** The classic studies involved deception and
  would need careful justification today. Nothing here re-runs them, and the
  session is a good place to discuss why a simulation is the right vehicle.
- **Nobody is assessed.** No task is administered to the person using the tool.

## Accessibility considerations

- Native ranges, selects, radios and buttons; no dragging, no timing, no
  hover-only content. The simulated task is never presented to the learner, so
  there is no perceptual demand at all.
- Each experiment exposes only its own controls and its own result. The pinned
  primary is 125px, so it stays visible while the six controls are scrolled.
- The two-route chart prints each route's percentage beside its bar and is
  followed by a sentence naming both values and what they imply. The histogram
  prints each count above its bar and is paired with a visible table.
- Every range carries an `aria-valuetext` naming both ends of its scale in words.
- Focus moves to the new experiment heading when a navigation button is pressed.
- Reduced-motion and forced-colours rules are provided. No horizontal page scroll
  at 320px, 375px, 1366px, 1440px or 1920px.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Reconstruct Sherif.** Using only the six controls, build the condition that
   the autokinetic studies correspond to. What did you have to set, and what does
   the route split say about why convergence there persisted?
2. **The 80% condition.** Find one. Then describe the workplace or classroom it
   would correspond to, and who would be paying the cost of dissent in it.
3. **Write two abstracts.** One for a paper reporting the classic condition, one
   for a paper reporting the low-identification condition, both honest. What
   would a textbook do with them?
4. **Add the missing term.** Design a seventh control for something the model
   lacks. Say which route it would feed and what it would predict.

## The model

Documented at the top of `tool.js`.

```
normative     = 0.68 * unanimityN[d] * audience * identification * clarity
informational = 0.75 * ambiguity * (0.4 + 0.6*standing) * unanimityI[d]
p             = 1 - (1 - normative) * (1 - informational)

unanimityN = [1.00, 0.30, 0.22, 0.18]   (d = 0..3 dissenters)
unanimityI = [1.00, 0.60, 0.50, 0.45]
audience   = 1.00 aloud, 0.35 written, 0.15 anonymous
```

Identification, clarity, ambiguity and standing are the sliders divided by 100.
Audience appears only in the normative term; ambiguity and standing only in the
informational one; identification only in the normative one.

**Experiment 2** draws 100 simulated participants from `mulberry32(20260311)`.
Each has a susceptibility multiplier drawn as `0.15 + 1.85u²` and then normalised
so the multipliers average exactly one — which is why the group mean reproduces
the headline rate rather than sitting below it. Per-round outcomes are Bernoulli
draws against `p × susceptibility`, from a fixed 100×24 grid of uniforms, so
changing the number of rounds does not reshuffle anybody.

**Reference values a lecturer can check.** The six presets give 35, 12, 14, 66, 7
and 67 per cent. In the classic preset at twelve rounds the distribution is 13
people who never conformed, then 36, 22, 19 and 10 across the four bands, with a
group mean of 35% of rounds. Pressing **Worked example** loads five presets into
the comparison table and leaves the low-identification condition in force.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Asch (1951, 1956)** for the original series, and — importantly — for the fact
  that Asch himself emphasised how often participants resisted.
- **Deutsch and Gerard (1955)** for the normative/informational distinction and
  for the private-response manipulation this tool is built around.
- **Bond and Smith (1996)** for the meta-analysis showing that conformity rates
  vary systematically with culture and with year of publication.
- **Turner (1991)** and **Abrams et al. (1990)** for referent informational
  influence and the ingroup/outgroup manipulation that motivates the
  identification control.
- **Friend, Rafferty and Bramel (1990)** on how textbooks came to report Asch's
  findings as evidence of conformity rather than of independence.
- **Sherif (1936)** for the ambiguous-task condition, and **tool 07 in this
  module** for norm formation specifically.
- **Milgram (1974)** is a different phenomenon and a different literature; if
  students raise it, the useful distinction is between an instruction from an
  authority and an unspoken majority position.

Full references are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the invented
parameters are not mistaken for estimates derived from any of them.
