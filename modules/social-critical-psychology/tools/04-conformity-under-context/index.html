Social and Critical Psychology

Conformity Under Context

Six things you can change about a fictional group judgement, two different routes by which people end up agreeing, and the distribution that every headline conformity percentage is hiding.

Simulated - a fictional task, a model with visible parameters, no historical dataset reproduced

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to tell informational from normative influence by what moves each, and say why “people conform” is not a finding without a context.

About 30 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

The task, and what it is not

Six people look at a fictional display and say how many marks it contains. The original studies used line lengths and points of light; nothing here reproduces their stimuli, their procedure or their data. The percentages come from a small two-route model whose parameters are printed on screen. They are chosen to put the classic conditions in roughly the right order of magnitude, which is a teaching decision, not a replication.

How to use this

  1. Commit to a percentage before you see any conditions.
  2. Experiment 1: load the classic condition, then change one thing at a time. Every condition you run is kept in the comparison table.
  3. Watch the split between the two routes, not only the total.
  4. Experiment 2: look at the 100 simulated participants behind the percentage you have been reading.

First, a judgement

A fictional participant sits with five other people. They are shown a display and asked in turn how many marks it contains. The answer is obvious. On twelve of the eighteen rounds, all five of the others give the same clearly wrong answer out loud before the participant replies.

On what percentage of those twelve rounds will the participant go along with the group?

What this demonstrates

Two routes, distinguished by what moves them

Informational influence is a response to uncertainty: other people are evidence about what is the case, and the harder the judgement the better evidence they are. Normative influence is a response to the social consequences of dissenting: it needs an audience. The manipulation that separates them is not a debate about definitions - it is the private response condition, which strips out the audience and leaves the evidence. Where private responding barely helps, the person is not caving in; they have been persuaded.

Unanimity is not a headcount

Adding a fourth, fifth or sixth confederate does very little. Removing the unanimity does a great deal, and the size of the remaining majority hardly matters. What a single dissenter changes is not the arithmetic of the group but the availability of another answer: once one person has said something else out loud, the majority position stops being the only thing it is possible to say.

It matters who the group is

The classic account represents the others as an audience and as a source of information, and is silent about whether they are your group. Turn identification down and the normative route weakens sharply even with everything else held constant. This is the social-identity amendment: influence flows along the lines of who counts as us, and a unanimous majority of people you do not regard as comparable to yourself is a much weaker thing than the number suggests.

The headline percentage is a distribution

A rate of a third across critical rounds is compatible with everybody conforming a third of the time, and it is also compatible - which is closer to what happens - with a quarter of people never conforming at all, a middle group conforming occasionally, and a minority conforming most of the time. Experiment 2 shows the distribution behind whatever number you have been reading. "People conform" and "most people mostly resisted" can be two descriptions of the same result.

Classic effects are not constants

The rates in the original studies are not physical constants of the species. They depend on how ambiguous the task is, who the majority are taken to be, what dissent costs in that setting, and what the participants think the study is for. Later replications in different decades and countries have produced a wide range of figures, and reading that variation as noise around a true value gets the situation backwards: the variation is the finding.

What this does not demonstrate

  • No historical study is reproduced. The task, the stimuli, the procedure and the numbers are invented. The presets are named after recognisable set-ups so the comparison is legible; they are not those experiments and their outputs are not those results.
  • Two routes is a simplification. Referent informational influence, self-categorisation, obedience to a perceived experimenter demand, politeness and simple boredom are all absent from the model.
  • Going along is not the same as believing. The model estimates a public response. Whether the person's private judgement changed is a separate question, and the private condition is the closest this tool gets to it.
  • Conformity is not a character flaw. Agreeing with five other people who all say the same thing is often sensible, and the ability of a group to converge is the basis of most of what people know. The interesting question is when it goes wrong.
  • Nobody is assessed. No task is administered to the person using the tool, and the simulated participants are not modelled on anybody.
