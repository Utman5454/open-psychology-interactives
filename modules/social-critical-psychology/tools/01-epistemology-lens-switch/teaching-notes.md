# Teaching notes — Epistemology Lens Switch

`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/01-epistemology-lens-switch/`

The argument in one sentence: the incident log is not a record of behaviour, and
whether you can see that depends on what you decided the object of study was.

---

## Intended level

Second- or third-year undergraduate on a social, critical or research-methods
strand. It assumes students have met the words *positivism*, *critical realism*
and *social constructionism* but not that they can use them. It works well as the
first session of a critical psychology module, because everything later in the
module — categories, discourse, power, person-versus-setting — is a special case
of the move made here.

No statistical background is needed.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. state what each of the three positions takes the research object to be;
2. explain why the method is downstream of that decision rather than prior to it;
3. write the strongest claim a given design licenses, and the nearest claim it
   does not;
4. distinguish social construction from fabrication, and coherence from truth;
5. notice when a problem has been relocated inside individuals before any data
   were collected, and ask who benefits from locating it there.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 10 minutes — one lens run cleanly, then the
  first row changed and the specification re-read.
- **Students in pairs, two lenses each:** 30 minutes.
- **With the five claims and the debrief:** 45 minutes.

## Preparation

None. Read the case panel aloud, or ask a student to. The one sentence that has
to land before anyone touches the tool is: *a logged incident is a referral typed
into the behaviour system by a member of staff.*

If you have twenty minutes rather than forty-five, cut the challenge and run two
lenses.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

Declare **realist / positivist** and press **Worked example** (it fills in a
coherent build for whichever position is currently declared). Read the bottom row
of the specification aloud, slowly:

> "Recorded incidents are higher in lessons with these features; the association
> is of this size, with this uncertainty."

Ask the room what word is missing. It is *disruption*. Then change the first row
only — set the research object to the constructionist option — and read the
coherence line and the tension note. Nothing about the school has changed. The
study has become a different study, and it happened at the first decision.

Then press **Run another lens on this case**, declare **social constructionist**,
and build it. When the comparison table has two rows, the point is made without
you saying it.

## Prediction question

*Three research teams are given exactly the same material. What will differ most
between the three studies?*

Most cohorts split between "only the write-up" and "the method". Both answers are
useful: the first underestimates how early the divergence happens, the second
locates it one step too late. The tool's answer is the fourth option — what they
take the object to be — and the specification table is the argument for it.

## Activity sequence

1. **Read the case.** Six pieces of evidence, held in common by all three teams.
2. **Commit to the prediction.** It gates the tool.
3. **Declare a position and build a study.** Five decisions. The specification
   fills in live; the feedback under it says what the current choice commits you
   to and, where relevant, what tension it creates.
4. **Read the appraisal.** Three sentences that matter: what it could claim, what
   it could not, what it makes hard to see.
5. **Run a second lens on the same case.** The comparison table is the payoff.
6. **Deliberately build an incoherent study.** Declare social constructionist and
   choose the observation-schedule method. Read the tension note. Ask whether the
   design is *wrong* or merely *undeclared*.
7. **The five claims.** Two of them are supported by no design on the page.

## Debrief questions

1. All three teams can use the incident log. What is it a record *of*, in each
   study?
2. The realist/positivist claim is about "recorded incidents". What would have to
   be true for it to be a claim about behaviour, and how would you find out?
3. What stops a critical realist mechanism story from being merely plausible?
   (The answer in the tool is the negative case — the departments where the same
   conditions held and nothing happened.)
4. A constructionist study cannot tell the governors whether behaviour is getting
   worse. Is that a limitation of the position or a refusal of the question, and
   does the distinction matter to the governors?
5. Option D throughout locates the problem in a minority of pupils. Who benefits
   from that location? Who bears the cost? What would it take to show it was the
   right one?
6. The tool calls a fully aligned build "internally coherent — which is the
   minimum, not the aim". What else would you want before calling a design good?
7. Which column of the comparison table does your own training make you fill in
   first?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "Constructionism means the disruption isn't real." | The debrief panel. Categories with institutional force have real consequences — exclusion is not imaginary. What is being studied is how an interaction becomes an attributable act by a named pupil. |
| "Critical realism is just positivism with interviews." | Run both builds. The positivist claim is an association with an uncertainty attached; the critical realist claim names an unobservable mechanism and states what would have disconfirmed it. Different objects, different warrant. |
| "So the quantitative study is the naive one." | The appraisal for the constructionist build says what it cannot say. The governors' question — is it getting worse — is a reasonable question that only one of these designs can touch. |
| "Mixing positions is cheating." | The tension note says the opposite: mixed designs are normal and often better. What is not defensible is mixing silently. |
| "The tool is telling us constructionism is right." | Show them the "What it makes hard to see" line for each lens, and the two claims in the challenge that no design supports. |
| "The 58% rise is the finding." | It is a rise in referrals across a period in which the referral rule changed. This is the single most useful thing in the session. |
| "Screening would settle it." | Claim 5. Even a perfect screening study establishes an association between scores and referrals, which is not a cause of a *change*, and the policy is the obvious candidate. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **Three positions is a simplification.** Realism, empiricism, positivism,
  critical realism, relativism, constructionism and constructivism are not four
  boxes, and serious writers disagree about the boundaries. The tool uses
  recognisable family resemblances so the downstream consequences are visible.
- **Nobody declares a lens in real life.** Assumptions are inherited from
  training, funders and journals. The declaration is a teaching device.
- **Coherence is a weak criterion.** The tool says so on screen. A coherent study
  can be badly designed, unethical, or answer a question nobody needed answered.
- **The case is fictional.** Meadowbank Academy, its figures, its policy, its
  room moves and its focus-group quotations are invented. No number here
  estimates anything, and no real school or dataset is described.
- **Nobody is assessed.** The tool evaluates a study specification about a
  fictional case. It asks nothing about the person using it.
- **The fourth option is not a joke.** Person-centred realism is a real research
  programme. It is on the list so students can name it and see what it assumes,
  not so they can feel superior to it.

## Accessibility considerations

- Native radios, buttons and fieldsets throughout. No dragging, no timing, no
  hover-only content.
- Only one decision is expanded at a time, which keeps the controls column under
  about 700px so the pinned specification stays in view at 1366×768. Collapsing
  is done by toggling `hidden`, not by rebuilding, so keyboard focus survives.
- Focus moves to the first option of a decision only when a navigation button is
  pressed — never as a side effect of choosing a radio, which would break
  arrow-key navigation within a group.
- Coherence is written in words on every row ("follows from your position", "in
  tension: critical realist") as well as tinted, so nothing depends on colour.
- The specification is a table rather than a diagram, so there is no chart
  needing a text equivalent.
- Announcements go through the shell's polite live region; long option text is
  announced with its radio because the option text is the label.
- Reduced-motion and forced-colours rules are provided. Usable at 320px and at
  projector widths.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **The undeclared mixture.** Find a published paper in the reading list and
   work out which of the five rows it fills in from which position. Most papers
   are mixed. Does the paper say so?
2. **Write the fourth lens.** Specify a feminist, decolonial or
   psychoanalytically informed study of the same case. What is the object? What
   would count as evidence?
3. **The governors' meeting.** In pairs, one student presents the positivist
   study and one the constructionist study to a governing body that has to decide
   what to fund. Notice which one is easier to act on, and whether that is a point
   in its favour.
4. **Make the log honest.** Redesign the referral form so that it can record an
   interaction rather than an act by a named pupil. What breaks?

## The model

Documented at the top of `tool.js`.

There is no random generation and no seed; the tool is deterministic. Every
decision offers the same four options, in the order *measure / mechanism /
construct / individual*, and no option is labelled with the position it belongs
to. Coherence is the count of decisions whose home position equals the declared
position, out of the number of decisions made so far.

| Home | Position | Where it appears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `measure` | Realist / positivist | First option of every decision |
| `mechanism` | Critical realist | Second option of every decision |
| `construct` | Social constructionist | Third option of every decision |
| `individual` | Person-centred realism (undeclared fourth position) | Fourth option of every decision |

**Reference values a lecturer can check.** Pressing **Worked example** with
critical realism declared (the default when nothing is declared) selects the
second option of all five decisions and reports "A coherent critical realist
study". Choosing the third option of decision 1 while realist/positivist is
declared produces "0 of your 1 decision follow from realist / positivist
premises" and the tension line for that option. The challenge answers are:
claim 1 — none; claim 2 — realist/positivist; claim 3 — critical realist;
claim 4 — social constructionist; claim 5 — none.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Bhaskar (1975/1978)** for the stratified ontology — the empirical, the actual
  and the real — that the second lens depends on, and **Bhaskar (1979)** for its
  application to the social sciences.
- **Sayer (2000), *Realism and Social Science*** is the most usable single
  introduction for undergraduates, and is explicit that critical realism accepts
  the social production of knowledge without accepting judgemental relativism.
- **Burr (2015), *Social Constructionism*** for the third lens, including the
  distinction between "constructed" and "unreal" that students most often miss.
- **Potter and Wetherell (1987)** for the move from language as a description of
  a mental state to language as action, which underwrites the constructionist
  method option.
- **Danziger (1990), *Constructing the Subject*** for how psychology's
  investigative practices produced its objects — the historical version of the
  argument this tool makes synchronically.
- **Hacking (1999), *The Social Construction of What?*** for the question the
  debrief panel asks: construction of the idea, the person, or the experience,
  and why the answer changes what follows.
- **Woolgar and Pawluch (1985)** on "ontological gerrymandering", worth setting
  against the third lens rather than in support of it.

Full references are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the tool does not
appear to derive its fictional case from any of them.
