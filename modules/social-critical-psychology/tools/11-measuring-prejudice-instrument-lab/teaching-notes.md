# Teaching notes — Measuring Prejudice: What Does the Instrument Capture?

`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/11-measuring-prejudice-instrument-lab/`

Twenty predictions made from the latency score alone come out at about 24 per
cent. Answering "an even shortlist" every time, without looking at anything,
comes out at 43 per cent.

---

## Intended level

Second- or third-year undergraduate, or taught postgraduate. It assumes comfort
with the idea of a correlation but no statistical technique. It is the natural
partner to tool 03 in this module (the attitude–behaviour gap) and to the
research-methods tools on measurement, and it works well immediately after tool
10, because both turn on the difference between what a procedure produced and
what somebody said it showed.

## A note on why nobody takes the task

There is no sorting task for the learner here, and that is deliberate. A tool
that generated a number about a student's own associations in front of a
seminar group and then explained that such numbers do not predict behaviour
would be teaching one thing and doing another, and it would put a label on a
person in a room. All 200 respondents are fictional. If a student asks to take a
real version, that request is itself a good five minutes of discussion: what
would they do with the number?

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. say what each of five instruments directly observes;
2. explain why a small average association supports no individual prediction;
3. keep self-report, latency, behaviour and institutional outcomes apart as four
   objects at four levels;
4. state why no individual attitude score can confirm or refute a claim about
   institutional discrimination;
5. recognise the moment a construct is replaced by the score a task produces.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 10 minutes — three predictions by hand, then
  the twenty-trial run, then the institutional row of the table in experiment 2.
- **Students working through both experiments:** 25 minutes.
- **With the challenge and the debrief:** 50–60 minutes.

## Preparation

Run experiment 1 yourself and keep going until you get one wrong, which will not
take long. Note the two numbers you are going to put on the screen: the
correlation (−0.12 in this sample) and the base rate (43 per cent).

Decide in advance how you want to handle the student who concludes that latency
tasks are worthless. That overcorrection is as much a misreading as the one the
tool is aimed at, and both the opening feedback and the second experiment's
running notes are written to head it off.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

1. **Three by hand.** Put respondent 1 on the screen, read the score aloud, and
   let the room vote before revealing. A large score belonging to somebody who
   shortlisted three arrives quickly, and that is the moment.
2. **Press "Run twenty predictions for me".** It always follows the direction the
   score points. Read the result: about **24 per cent** correct, against a base
   rate of **43 per cent**. Guessing "even" every time, with no information at
   all, does substantially better.
3. **Open the table** and read the range column. A single score is consistent
   with every one of the five possible shortlists.
4. **Then experiment 2, last row.** The appointments record is a fact about an
   organisation over twelve years. Ask the room what study of individual
   attitudes could confirm it. The answer is none, and the answer runs in both
   directions.

## Prediction question

*How well will the sorting score predict any individual's shortlist?*

"Barely — a small average relationship and no useful individual prediction" is
the intended answer, and the feedback insists on holding both halves. "Not at
all" is the useful overcorrection: they are related, just weakly, and concluding
that the task measures nothing throws away a real finding about a population.

## Activity sequence

1. **Commit to the prediction.** It opens both experiments.
2. **Experiment 1**, at least five respondents by hand, using "You cannot tell
   from this score" whenever it is honest — the tool treats that as a real
   answer and counts it separately.
3. **The twenty-trial run**, then the table beneath the scatter.
4. **Experiment 2**, all five instruments, in any order. Each ends with what is
   inferred, what remains unexplained and what the instrument cannot support.
5. **The five-instrument table**, which is the synthesis.
6. **The challenge**, then the debrief.

## Debrief questions

1. The correlation is −0.12 and it is not zero. What is it a fact *about*?
2. You were right about some respondents. What made you right?
3. What, exactly, does a latency difference score observe? Name three things
   other than an attitude that contribute to it.
4. The self-reported gap narrowed from 22 points to 13 over ten years. Give two
   readings of that, and say what evidence would separate them.
5. The appointments record has not moved in twelve years. Which of the other
   four instruments could confirm it? Which could refute it?
6. Suppose every Lowlander in Vantry scored zero on the sorting task tomorrow.
   What would you expect the appointments record to do, and why?
7. At what point does a research programme stop studying prejudice and start
   studying its own instrument? What would tell you it had happened?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "The latency task gets at what people really think." | It gets at how fast an association becomes available under time pressure. Familiarity, block order, category salience and general speed all contribute, and none of them is an attitude. |
| "The correlation is small, so the task is useless." | It is a real fact about a population. The error is in the inference to an individual, not in the measurement. |
| "Self-report is just social desirability." | What people are willing to say is itself worth knowing, and a change in what is sayable is a finding about a society, not noise. |
| "The appointments gap proves discrimination." | It is a serious, patterned disparity that demands explanation. Which process produced it decides what to do about it, and the record alone does not say. |
| "If two measures disagree, one must be right." | They are measuring different things. Nothing in the design could adjudicate, and "the real one, underneath" is a rhetorical promotion. |
| "So we should screen panel members with the task." | Claim 2 in the challenge, and the 24-per-cent figure. Selecting people on a measure that performs worse than a constant guess is not a cautious policy. |
| "The behavioural task is the real test." | One act, in a study, with nothing at stake, by somebody who may have worked out what it was about. It is evidence of itself. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **Nothing here measures the learner**, by design.
- **All data are generated** by a documented seeded model. No figure is a
  prevalence estimate, a norm, a published effect or a finding about anywhere.
- **Vantry is invented**, with no real-world referent, so that nothing on the
  page can be read as a claim about a real population.
- **It does not show that latency measures are worthless**, and it does not show
  that self-report is trustworthy.
- **It does not identify the cause of the Vantry appointments gap.** Several
  processes are consistent with it, and separating them needs evidence this tool
  does not contain.
- **It is not a review of the real literature.** The arguments about
  latency-based measures, their reliability and their predictive validity are
  live, technical and contested.

## Accessibility considerations

- Nothing is timed and there is no reaction-time task for the learner, so no part
  of the activity depends on motor speed.
- The scatter is hidden from assistive technology and paired with a sentence
  giving the correlation, the selected respondent's score and what a perfectly
  predictive score would look like, plus a full table of mean and range by
  outcome band and the printed rule-accuracy check.
- The selected respondent is marked by a ring *and* a full-height dashed stem, so
  it is found by shape and position rather than by colour.
- Every control is a native radio or button in a labelled fieldset. Requesting a
  reveal without a prediction produces an explanatory message, and "You cannot
  tell from this score" is treated as a real answer with its own feedback.
- Both experiments announce every change through the shell's polite live region.
- Either reset button restores the whole page, including the prediction gate, so
  a learner cannot end up with one experiment reset and the other not.
- Headings run h1 to h5 without skipping. Focus moves to the first experiment's
  heading when the gate opens. Targets are at least 2.75rem. Forced-colours rules
  provided. No horizontal page scroll at 320px, 375px, 1366px, 1440px or 1920px.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Design the study that would settle it.** What evidence would distinguish the
   candidate explanations of the Vantry appointments record? Write the design,
   then say what it would cost and who would have to agree to it.
2. **Change the seed.** The model is documented at the top of `tool.js`. Change
   the coefficient on z in the behavioural latent so the correlation is −0.40 and
   re-run the twenty-trial prediction. At what value does prediction from the
   score start to beat the base rate, and would that value be plausible?
3. **The other direction.** Write 300 words on what would be lost if psychology
   stopped using latency measures entirely.
4. **Find the drift.** Take one published research programme built on a single
   instrument and identify a sentence in it where the construct and the score
   have swapped places.
5. **The fifth level.** All five instruments here stop at the institution. What
   would a sixth, societal-level instrument look like, and what would it not be
   able to show?

## The model

Documented in full at the top of `tool.js`. `mulberry32(20260811)` with
Box-Muller normal draws. For each of 200 respondents:

    z  ~ N(0,1)                     a latent association strength
    D  = 0.34 + 0.22z + 0.26e1      latency difference score, seconds
    b  = -0.30z + e2                a behavioural latent
    S  = round(2 + 0.85b), 0..4     same-region candidates shortlisted, of four
    W  = 13 + 2.0z + 8e3            self-reported warmth gap, points

The construction gives a population correlation of about −0.19, deliberately of
the same order as the correlations reported in meta-analyses of real
latency–behaviour relationships. Rounding and clamping attenuate it.

**Reference values a lecturer can check**, all computed at run time and printed
on the page:

| Figure | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Realised correlation, score against shortlist | −0.12 |
| Sample median score | 0.32 s |
| Shortlists that were even (2 of 4) | 43% of 200 |
| Respondents whose shortlist was not even | 114 |
| Above-median rule, accuracy on those 114 | 57% (chance there is 50%) |
| Twenty-trial automated run, accuracy | about 24% |

The last two are the pair to put side by side: a rule that beats chance slightly
on the cases where it commits still loses badly to a constant guess across the
whole sample, because it can never say "even" — which is what 43 per cent of the
sample did.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Greenwald, McGhee and Schwartz (1998)** for the original latency-based
  association procedure, and **Greenwald, Poehlman, Uhlmann and Banaji (2009)**
  for the first large predictive-validity meta-analysis.
- **Oswald, Mitchell, Blanton, Jaccard and Tetlock (2013)** and **Forscher et al.
  (2019)** for the smaller estimates and the argument about what follows from
  them - the correlation used in this simulation sits in that range.
- **Blanton and Jaccard (2006)** on arbitrary metrics, which is the cleanest
  statement of why a score with no natural unit cannot be read as a quantity of
  anything.
- **Cronbach and Meehl (1955)** for construct validity, and **Borsboom, Mellenbergh
  and van Heerden (2004)** for the argument that validity is a property of the
  attribute and the measurement process rather than of a correlation table.
- **LaPiere (1934)** and **Wicker (1969)** for the attitude-behaviour problem this
  material inherits.
- **Robinson (1950)** on the ecological fallacy, and the individualistic fallacy
  running the other way, which is what claim 1 in the challenge is about.
- **Reskin (2003)** for the argument that accounts of institutional inequality
  built on individual attitudes get the causal direction wrong.
- **Danziger (1990)** for how a discipline's instruments come to constitute its
  objects, which is the debrief's last section in one book.

Full references are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the simulated
numbers are not mistaken for anything derived from them.
