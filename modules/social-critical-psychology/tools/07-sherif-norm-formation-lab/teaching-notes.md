# Teaching notes — Norm-Formation Laboratory

`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/07-sherif-norm-formation-lab/`

Round 11 is the round that matters. Nobody is watching, and the group's range
comes with you anyway.

---

## Intended level

First-year undergraduate on a social psychology strand, or any group meeting
Sherif for the first time. No statistics and no prior reading are needed. It
pairs naturally with tool 04 in this module: run this one first, because the
contrast between an unanswerable judgement and an obvious one is what makes the
two-route distinction land.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. describe what happens to an unanchored judgement when others become visible;
2. explain why persistence rules out the simplest explanation;
3. account for why different groups reach different norms;
4. distinguish compliance with a demand from a shared definition of a situation;
5. state the boundary conditions, above all the role of ambiguity.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 6 minutes using the simulated-participant
  route.
- **Students individually, all fifteen rounds:** 20 minutes.
- **With the historical note and the debrief:** 35 minutes.

## Preparation

Run it yourself first, honestly, before demonstrating it. The effect is far more
convincing to a room when the person at the front has been surprised by their own
round 11.

Say before anyone starts that nothing is stored, nothing is scored, and that
holding your own is as legitimate an outcome as converging. Some students find
the exercise uncomfortable otherwise.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

Press **Run a simulated participant instead**. It plays all fifteen rounds and
opens the four-group comparison.

Then point at three things in order:

1. the trace during rounds 6–10, which everyone expects;
2. the phase summary for rounds 11–15, which is the finding;
3. the four-group table, where the norms in a representative run come out at
   11.4, 3.6, 12.4 and 29.3 cm from an identical display.

Say while pointing at the last one: *from inside any one of those groups, the
norm looks like a fact about the display.*

## Prediction question

*What will happen across the fifteen rounds?*

"Converge and stay converged" is the intended answer. "Converge on the true
answer" is the most useful wrong one: there is no true answer, and the feedback
uses that to introduce ambiguity as the boundary condition rather than as a
detail of the method.

## Activity sequence

1. **Commit to the prediction.** It gates the laboratory.
2. **Rounds 1–5, alone.** Read the phase summary: most people settle into a
   range with nothing to settle on.
3. **Rounds 6–10, with three others.** Their opening values are far apart.
4. **Rounds 11–15, alone.** Nobody is watching.
5. **Show the other groups.**
6. **Read the historical note** and the five criticisms beside it.

## Debrief questions

1. In rounds 6–10 nobody asked you to agree and nobody could see your answer.
   What were the three other estimates giving you?
2. Why is round 11 the round that matters?
3. Four groups, one display, four norms. What does that make a norm a fact
   about?
4. Is converging here a failure of independence? What would you have used
   instead?
5. Sherif's participants sat in a dark room with a real perceptual illusion; you
   looked at a drawing. What does that change about what the demonstration can
   show?
6. Which of the five criticisms beside the historical note would most change how
   you read the original result?
7. Is the norm you formed here the same kind of thing as a norm about queueing,
   or about who speaks first in a meeting?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "I just went along with them." | Round 11. Nobody was watching, and the same explanation cannot cover both phases. |
| "This is the same as Asch." | Asch's judgement was easy and public. Here it is unanswerable and private. Tool 04 in this module separates the two routes explicitly. |
| "The group found the right answer." | There is no right answer. Four groups found four. |
| "So I'm a conformist." | Nothing here measures anybody. Individual differences in how much weight people give to other estimates are large and uninteresting for this purpose. |
| "The simulation proves the effect." | It proves what the model does. The evidence that people do it is in the literature, and the page says so twice. |
| "Norms always form like this." | Most norms are backed by sanctions, institutions or material interest. Ambiguity-driven convergence is one mechanism among several. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **The other participants are not people.** They are generated by a rule built
  to converge.
- **No autokinetic effect is involved.** The ambiguity is stipulated, not
  perceptual — which is also what makes the task fully usable without seeing the
  drawing.
- **Nothing measures the learner.** Estimates draw a chart and are discarded.
- **The historical study is described separately** and with its criticisms, and
  nothing on the page is presented as a replication of it.
- **Ambiguity is a boundary condition**, not a detail: the effect is largest
  exactly where the judgement is least answerable.

## Accessibility considerations

- Nothing is timed and nothing is scored. The whole task is self-paced, and a
  simulated-participant route runs all fifteen rounds for anyone who would rather
  not answer.
- The stimulus is deliberately uninformative and the sentence beneath it carries
  everything the drawing does, so a learner who cannot see it is at no
  disadvantage.
- The trace chart is hidden from assistive technology and paired with a sentence
  listing every estimate by phase with each phase mean. The group phase is marked
  by a shaded band *and* a label inside it.
- The number input accepts Enter, validates its range, and explains a rejected
  value rather than reporting an error.
- Announcements go through the shell's polite live region after every round.
- Reduced-motion and forced-colours rules are provided. No horizontal page scroll
  at 320px, 375px, 1366px, 1440px or 1920px.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Break it.** Run it again and deliberately hold your first estimate for all
   fifteen rounds. What does the group do? What does that tell you about who is
   converging on whom?
2. **The generational version.** Read Jacobs and Campbell (1961) and design the
   version of this tool in which one participant leaves and a new one arrives
   every five rounds. Predict how long the norm survives.
3. **Make it answerable.** Rewrite the task so the judgement can be checked. What
   do you predict happens to rounds 11–15, and which literature would you look in?
4. **The critical version.** Write 300 words on whether "a norm" in this
   laboratory and "a norm" in a workplace are the same kind of object.

## The model

Documented at the top of `tool.js`. All randomness comes from
`mulberry32(20260214)`, reseeded on reset.

- The three other participants are created **after** round 5, from anchors at
  0.45, 0.85 and 1.75 times the learner's own opening mean (times a small random
  factor). The emerging norm is therefore not imposed from outside.
- On each group round, every simulated participant moves 42% of the way towards
  the running mean of the group including the learner's last estimate, plus
  noise scaled to the running mean.
- The learner's own estimates are never modelled, constrained or nudged.
- The three comparison groups are four simulated participants each, drawn around
  openings of 0.35, 1.1 and 2.6 times the learner's opening mean and run through
  the identical rule.

**Reference values a lecturer can check.** In the simulated-participant run from
a fresh reset, the four group norms come out at approximately 11.4, 3.6, 12.4 and
29.3 cm, with within-group spreads of 1.3, 0.7, 0.8 and 4.6 cm. Because the
learner's own estimates feed the anchors, a human run will produce different
numbers; the pattern — a wide range between groups and a narrow one within each —
is what is stable.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Sherif (1935, 1936)** for the original autokinetic studies, the convergence
  of ranges rather than of single answers, and the persistence result.
- **Jacobs and Campbell (1961)** for the generational transmission version, where
  a norm survives the replacement of everybody who created it.
- **Deutsch and Gerard (1955)** for the reason this is treated as the cleanest
  case of informational influence.
- **Turner (1991)** for the argument that the frame is not merely informational
  but depends on whom the others are taken to be — worth setting against the
  simple reading.
- **Orne (1962)** on demand characteristics, which is the first of the criticisms
  listed beside the historical note.
- **Bond and Smith (1996)** for the wider point that classic influence effects
  vary with culture and decade.

Full references are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the simulated
numbers are not mistaken for anything derived from them.
