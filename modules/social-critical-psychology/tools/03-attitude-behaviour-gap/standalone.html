Social and Critical Psychology

Attitude-Behaviour Gap Laboratory

Four fictional colleagues answer a questionnaire about speaking up when they disagree in a meeting. All four score 6.2 out of 7. Then they go to work.

Simulated - fictional people, a fictional workplace and a model with visible coefficients

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to name the conditions standing between a measured evaluation and an action, and say which of them add and which gate.

About 30 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

What the model is and is not

The likelihood figures come from a small logistic model whose coefficients are printed in the interface and in the teaching notes. They are chosen to make a teaching pattern visible. They are not estimates of anything, they do not come from any dataset, and they should never be quoted as effect sizes. The model is a deliberately simplified relative of the reasoned-action tradition; its most important simplification is stated on screen.

How to use this

  1. Commit to an answer about Aisha, using only her score.
  2. Experiment 1: predict what four colleagues with the same score actually do, then see the model's answer for each.
  3. Experiment 2: take one of them and move the six conditions one at a time.
  4. Experiment 3: run the whole thing across 300 simulated people and watch the correlation change with the setting and with the measure.

First, a judgement

Aisha is a fictional member of a fictional project team. On a well-constructed seven-point questionnaire measuring attitude towards voicing disagreement at work, she scores 6.2. The scale has good internal consistency and a sensible factor structure.

On what proportion of the occasions where she disagrees will she say so?

Key terms
Correspondence
How closely the questionnaire's specificity matches the action being predicted — in target, action, context and time.
Situational constraint
Features of the setting that decide whether an intention can be acted on at all.
Individual and sample claims
A correlation computed across 300 people, and a statement about one of them. The first does not license the second.

What this demonstrates

A score is an answer to a question, not a summary of a life

Aisha's 6.2 is a reliable and valid measurement of how she evaluates voicing disagreement. It is not a measurement of how often she does it, and nothing about improving the questionnaire would make it one. The gap between the two is not measurement error to be engineered away; it is occupied by other people, a chair, a room, a rota and a career.

Some conditions add; one of them gates

Norms, confidence and habit shift the odds. Opportunity does not: if the occasion does not arise, nothing else matters, which is why it multiplies in the model rather than adding. Marek has every internal condition Rowan has and does the behaviour far less often. A great deal of applied psychology is aimed at the terms that add, in situations where the term that gates is doing the work.

Correspondence is a property of the instrument

The same person, the same disposition, three questionnaires. A general values scale correlates weakly with a specific act; a measure matched to the action in target, context and time correlates far better. This is the compatibility principle from the reasoned-action tradition. It explains a large part of the historical "attitudes do not predict behaviour" literature without anyone having to be wrong about attitudes.

The correlation is a fact about the sample's conditions

Experiment 3 holds the attitude distribution fixed and changes only how much the conditions vary. In uniform conditions the correlation is high, because attitude is the only thing left varying. In real teams it falls, because the outcome now depends on things the questionnaire never asked about. An attitude-behaviour correlation is therefore partly a description of the setting the study was run in - which is worth remembering before generalising one.

Where this leaves the individual-level account

The reasoned-action models are among the most successful individual-level frameworks in social psychology, and this tool is built on their logic. The critical point is not that they are wrong but that they are a particular level of description. They represent norms as a perception inside a head, and constraints as a perceived control. That is exactly right if you want to predict an individual, and exactly wrong if you want to explain why the chair closes discussion at ten past. Both questions are legitimate; they are not the same question, and the questionnaire only answers one of them.

What this does not demonstrate

  • The coefficients are invented. They are printed on screen and in the teaching notes so that nothing is hidden, and they are chosen to make a pattern visible. They are not estimates, and the correlations that follow from them are not findings.
  • Six conditions is a simplification. Real behaviour also depends on mood, fatigue, what happened in the last meeting, who else is in the room and what is at stake. None of that is modelled here.
  • It does not show that attitudes are unimportant. Attitude is the largest single coefficient in the model. The claim is about what a single measured evaluation licenses on its own, not about whether evaluations matter.
  • It does not test the theory of planned behaviour. A simulation cannot test a theory. This is an illustration of the logic of such models and of one of their standard criticisms, not evidence for or against any of them.
  • Nobody is assessed. The questionnaire is never administered to you. All four colleagues are fictional, and no real workplace is described.
  • Speaking up is not always the right thing to do. The model estimates a frequency, not a virtue. Whether voicing disagreement is wise in a particular meeting is a question the model has no view about. The costs of doing so fall very unevenly across a workforce.
