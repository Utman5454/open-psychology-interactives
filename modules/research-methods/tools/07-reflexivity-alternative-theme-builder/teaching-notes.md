# Teaching notes — Reflexivity and Alternative Theme Builder

`modules/research-methods/tools/07-reflexivity-alternative-theme-builder/`

Two of the nine pairings do not hold together. That is what stops the session
becoming an argument about whether qualitative work has standards.

---

## Intended level

Third-year undergraduate or taught postgraduate, and any project student
writing a reflexivity statement. It is the third of three qualitative tools in
this module sharing one fictional dataset: **Thematic Analysis Coding
Laboratory**, then **Theme or Topic?**, then this one.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. explain how a research question and a theoretical position together decide
   which themes are available;
2. recognise a pairing in which question and lens pull against each other;
3. state what a given reading illuminates and what it leaves less visible;
4. distinguish several defensible readings from the claim that any reading is
   defensible;
5. identify claims that no available reading can support, and say why.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 12 minutes.
- **Students in pairs:** 30 minutes.
- **With the accountability check and debrief:** 45 minutes.

## Preparation

None, though the session is much stronger if students have already met the six
extracts in the coding laboratory.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

Start on **Question 1** (how the moment of deciding is experienced) with
**Lens A** (experiential). Strong match. Read the two themes and the
foregrounding map: Ama, Kwame and Lena central; Jonah and Priya supporting.

Now switch to **Lens C** (critical and institutional), leaving the question
alone. The coherence line changes to **strained**. Read the coherence note
aloud:

> The question asks how the moment is experienced; the lens is built to look
> past the person at the arrangements. The account is not incompetent — it is
> answering a question nobody asked.

That is the moment. Students who arrived believing qualitative analysis has no
standards can see a standard being applied, from outside, without any appeal to
the analyst's skill.

Then: **Save this account**, switch to **Question 3 with Lens C** (strong), and
press **Compare**. The note beneath the table names exactly who moved. Toby
comes in from the margins; Lena leaves the centre entirely. Same six people,
two studies.

## Prediction question

*Two researchers analyse the same six interviews and publish different themes.
Both papers pass review. What follows?* — one of them analysed badly; **they
asked different questions from different positions**; qualitative analysis has
no standards; the truth is in between and the accounts should be merged.

The merge option is the interesting near-miss and gets its own response:
accounts built on different assumptions are not estimates of the same quantity
to be averaged, and blending them produces a results section with no organising
position behind it.

## Activity sequence

1. **Commit to an answer** on the opening question.
2. **Work the strong pairings** — Q1/A, Q2/B, Q2/C, Q3/C. Note how different the
   foregrounding maps are.
3. **Find both strained pairings** (Q1/C and Q3/A) and read the coherence notes.
4. **Save and compare** two accounts, ideally Q1/A against Q3/C.
5. **The accountability check**, run once under the critical lens and once
   under the experiential lens. Exactly one claim changes verdict.
6. Ask which claim changed, and why that is not a defect in the tool.

## Debrief questions

1. Nothing was added to or removed from the dataset by any control. What
   changed, then?
2. Q1 with Lens C produces a coherent-sounding account that the tool calls
   strained. What exactly is wrong with it?
3. Q2/C is the most confident account in the tool and cannot accommodate Lena.
   What should the write-up say about that?
4. Two claims in the accountability check fail under *every* reading. What do
   they have in common, and what is different about how they fail?
5. Claim 5 changes verdict when the lens changes. Is that relativism?
6. Toby is one participant out of six and is decisive for three of the nine
   accounts. What obligation does that create for the author?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "So it is all interpretation, anything goes." | Two strained pairings; two claims that fail under every reading; one claim over-stated whatever lens you use. |
| "The critical reading is the deep one." | Q1/C is strained precisely because depth is the wrong axis. A lens is not better, it is aimed somewhere. |
| "Reflexivity means declaring your biases." | It means stating the position you analysed from so a reader can see what it made visible and what it cost. Every account here prints both. |
| "You should pick the lens that fits the data." | The lens and the question are chosen together, before the analysis, and both are answerable to the data afterwards. |
| "Merge the two accounts for a fuller picture." | The opening question's fourth option. Merging removes the organising position from both. |
| "A theme that covers all six participants is better." | Q2/C covers five and is the strongest account in the tool. Coverage is not the criterion; what matters is that the exception is declared. |
| "The tool analysed the data." | It selected a prepared account. No software analyses qualitative data — the page says so twice. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **Nine accounts is not the space of possibilities.** Narrative, psychosocial,
  feminist and discursive-psychological readings would produce others.
- **The accounts are written, not generated.** The tool selects; it does not
  analyse.
- **The coherence ratings are judgements.** A supervisor could argue that a
  strained pairing becomes workable if the question is rephrased — and that
  argument is a good seminar.
- **Six invented extracts are not a study.** They are short and stripped of the
  context that makes real transcripts hard.
- **Choosing a lens does not license its claims.** The accountability check
  applies to the critical reading exactly as it does to the others.

## Accessibility considerations

- Native radios, selects and buttons; every control labelled, every group in a
  fieldset with a legend.
- **There are no charts.** The foregrounding map is HTML: each row prints the
  participant's name and their standing in words — *central*, *supporting*, *at
  the margins* — so neither bar length nor fill carries meaning.
- The coherence rating is always printed as a word; the coloured border repeats
  it.
- The account card and foregrounding map are pinned to the top of the stage, so
  they stay visible while the question and lens controls beside them change.
- The two-account comparison is a table with row headers.
- Every change announces a full sentence including the coherence note.
- The claim selects are given full width explicitly, because they sit outside
  the shell and their options are long sentences.
- Reduced motion honoured; forced-colours rules provided; usable at 320px and at
  projector widths.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Rescue a strained pairing.** Rewrite Question 1 so that Lens C becomes a
   strong match. What did you have to give up?
2. **Write the reflexivity paragraph** for the Q2/C account, including what it
   cannot see and why Lena is at the margins.
3. **Add a lens.** Sketch a narrative reading of the six extracts. Which
   participants would move, and which question would it fit?
4. **Audit a real paper.** Find a qualitative paper, identify its question and
   its stated position, and judge whether they are a strong, workable or
   strained pairing.

## The model

Nine prepared accounts, indexed by `question/lens`. Each carries themes, a
foregrounding level per participant (0 = at the margins, 1 = supporting,
2 = central), an *illuminates* sentence, a *leaves less visible* sentence and a
coherence rating with a note.

### The coherence matrix

| | Lens A: experiential | Lens B: social-psychological | Lens C: critical / institutional |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Q1** How the moment is experienced | **strong** | workable | **strained** |
| **Q2** What makes asking costly here | workable | **strong** | **strong** |
| **Q3** How the category is produced | **strained** | workable | **strong** |

Four strong, three workable, two strained.

### Foregrounding, for the two accounts worth comparing

| Participant | Q1 / Lens A | Q3 / Lens C |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ama | central | supporting |
| Jonah | supporting | central |
| Priya | supporting | central |
| Kwame | central | central |
| Lena | central | at the margins |
| Toby | supporting | central |

Four of the six change standing. That is what the comparison note reports.

### The accountability check

| # | Claim | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Students avoid asking because they lack confidence | **No** under every reading — imports a disposition nobody reported |
| 2 | Asking is experienced as making oneself visible | **Yes** under every reading |
| 3 | Support processes are designed to discourage use | **No** under every reading — attributes intent to people nobody interviewed |
| 4 | Students who have worked elsewhere find the norm easier to resist | **Partly** — one participant, stated as though about a group |
| 5 | Whether asking is costly depends on the setting rather than the person | **Reading-dependent**: *no* under the experiential lens, *yes* under the other two |

Claim 5 is the one to discuss. It is not relativism: the experiential lens has
no vantage point from which to compare settings, so a claim about settings is
not something it can pay for, whatever the data contain.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Braun and Clarke (2019; 2022)** on reflexivity as the analyst's active role
  rather than a bias to be removed, and on themes as outputs of analysis.
- **Finlay (2002)** for the distinction between reflexivity as method and
  reflexivity as confession.
- **Willig (2013)** on how epistemological position constrains what a
  qualitative analysis can claim — the source of the coherence idea used here.
- **Madill, Jordan and Shirley (2000)** on realist and contextualist positions
  producing different, both-defensible readings.
- **Yardley (2000)** on commitment, coherence and transparency as criteria for
  qualitative research, which is what the accountability check operationalises.
- **Braun and Clarke (2023)** on over-claiming, including causal and intentional
  language.

References are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the tool does not
appear to derive its invented extracts or accounts from any published study.
