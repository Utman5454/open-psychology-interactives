# Teaching notes — Theme or Topic?

`modules/research-methods/tools/06-theme-or-topic-challenge/`

The candidate students get wrong is never the obvious one. It is the true,
supported, reasonable-sounding sentence in third place.

---

## Intended level

Second- or third-year undergraduate, and every project student about to write a
qualitative results section. It follows the **Thematic Analysis Coding
Laboratory** in this module and uses the same fictional dataset; running them in
order takes about an hour.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. distinguish a topic summary from a developed theme;
2. name the central organising concept a theme is built around;
3. recognise a descriptive statement that is a legitimate stage and not yet a
   theme;
4. identify claims that go beyond what interview data can support, and say why
   in each case;
5. rewrite a topic heading so that it carries an analytic claim.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 10 minutes — cluster 1 alone.
- **Students in pairs:** 20 minutes for all three.
- **With the rewrite challenge and debrief:** 35 minutes.

## Preparation

None. It helps if students have already met the six extracts in the coding
laboratory, but the clusters carry enough quotation to stand alone.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

Cluster 1. Read the four candidates aloud in order and take a vote on each
before checking.

- **"Asking for help"** — everyone spots it.
- **"Students feel anxious about asking questions in seminars"** — most rooms
  call this a theme. Ask what it explains. It reports a shared feeling and says
  nothing about what produced it, and it cannot accommodate Lena, for whom
  asking went fine. That is the whole lesson.
- **"Visibility is the price: the first question turns a private gap into a
  public one"** — ask what one idea is holding the extracts together, and
  whether anyone could disagree with the statement.
- **"Seminar silence is caused by student anxiety"** — ask what would have to
  have been done to establish that.

## Prediction question

*What makes a statement a theme rather than a topic summary?* — it covers
something appearing in most transcripts; it is more abstract; **it makes an
analytic claim organised around one idea**; it is a full sentence.

Both near-misses get a written response. "More abstract" is answered by
*identity* and *institutional processes*, both highly abstract and both topic
summaries. "A full sentence" is answered by *students find university procedures
confusing*, a full sentence and a staging post.

## Activity sequence

1. **Commit to an answer** on the opening question.
2. **Cluster 1** as above.
3. **Cluster 2.** The over-reaching candidate here attributes an *intention* to
   the university. Ask who would have had to be interviewed for that.
4. **Cluster 3.** The staging post rests on one participant and turns Toby into
   a demographic. The over-reaching candidate imports self-esteem and a
   between-person comparison at the same time.
5. **The rewrite challenge**, which accepts two different themes for the same
   extracts and says so.

## Debrief questions

1. What is the central organising concept of the theme in cluster 2, in your own
   words?
2. "Students feel anxious about asking questions" is true and supported. What
   more does a theme have to do?
3. The three over-reaching candidates fail differently. Name the three failures.
4. The rewrite challenge has two acceptable answers that would produce different
   papers. On what grounds would you choose between them?
5. Take the cluster 3 theme and try to move one extract out of it. Does the
   theme change? What does your answer tell you?
6. Why is "poor communication at the university" worse than "communication
   channels", even though it says more?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "A theme is what most people said." | Prevalence is a habit from content analysis. A theme can rest on two extracts showing one concept from different angles. |
| "Make it more abstract and it becomes a theme." | *Identity*. *Institutional processes*. Both abstract, both topic summaries. |
| "It has to be a full sentence." | *Students find university procedures confusing* is a full sentence and a staging post. |
| "Themes should be neutral descriptions." | Then nobody could disagree with them, and a claim nobody can disagree with is not a finding. |
| "Qualitative work cannot over-claim, because it does not test anything." | Three candidates here over-claim, in three different ways. |
| "My headings are fine, they are just short." | Ask what each heading claims. If the answer is "what the section is about", it is a folder name. |
| "Frustration with X is a theme." | It is the single most common weak theme in student work: a topic with an emotion attached. The rewrite challenge names it. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **Four categories is a teaching simplification.** Real candidates sit on a
  continuum, and several here could be argued into a neighbouring category.
- **The developed themes are defensible, not uniquely correct.** Each cluster
  could support other themes built on other organising concepts, and the rewrite
  challenge accepts two.
- **The extracts are invented** and appear here as short quotations and codes
  rather than whole transcripts.
- **A well-formed theme can still be a bad one.** Phrasing is not evidence; a
  theme must be supported across the dataset and survive the cases that do not
  fit.
- **This is not a test of writing.** It is about what a statement claims.

## Accessibility considerations

- Native selects, checkboxes and buttons; every control labelled, every group in
  a fieldset with a legend.
- **There are no charts.** All output is text.
- The cluster — its name, its codes and two short quotations — is pinned to the
  top of the stage, so the material being judged stays visible while the four
  candidates are classified.
- Every feedback block prints **both** the learner's classification and the
  tool's, in words, so the coloured border is redundant.
- Each candidate control carries a visible "Candidate A" tag as well as its
  position in the list.
- The stage track marks current and checked clusters with visually hidden text
  as well as styling.
- Focus moves to the challenge heading on unlock; forced-colours rules provided;
  usable at 320px and at projector widths.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Write the fifth candidate.** For any cluster, write a statement that would
   be a *better* developed theme than the one offered, and defend it.
2. **Audit a paper.** Take a published qualitative paper and classify its theme
   headings using the four categories. Most papers contain at least one topic
   summary.
3. **The negative case.** Cluster 1's theme is built so that it survives Lena's
   positive experience. Write a theme for the same cluster that does not, and
   say what goes wrong.
4. **Two papers, one dataset.** Using the rewrite challenge's two acceptable
   themes, sketch the abstract of each paper in three sentences.

## The model

There is no scoring beyond a count of matches, and the feedback says explicitly
that a supervisor might argue the staging-post/theme line the other way.

| Category | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `topic` | Names the subject matter; a reader learns what the extracts are *about* |
| `staging` | True, supported, descriptive; a legitimate stage, not yet a theme |
| `theme` | An analytic claim organised around one central concept |
| `overreach` | A statement these data cannot carry, for a stated reason |

### The answer set

| Cluster | Topic | Staging post | Developed theme | Over-reach and why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. First question | "Asking for help" | "Students feel anxious about asking questions in seminars" | Visibility is the price; the first question makes a private gap public | "Seminar silence is caused by student anxiety" — asserts a cause, and contradicted by Lena |
| 2. Knowing the rules | "Institutional processes" | "Students find university procedures confusing" | Getting help depends on already knowing how getting help works | "The university deliberately hides its procedures" — attributes intent to an organisation nobody interviewed |
| 3. What kind of student | "Identity" | "Mature students find university culture different from workplaces" | Asking is read as a claim about what kind of student you are | "Students with lower self-esteem are less likely to seek support" — imports a construct *and* a between-person comparison |

The rewrite challenge accepts **two** of five options — *being answered without
being reached* and *what you find out depends on who you happen to know* —
because they are two different themes for the same extracts, and the feedback
says choosing between them is an analytic decision rather than a matter of
phrasing.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Braun and Clarke (2006; 2019; 2022)** for the central organising concept and
  for the "topic summary" diagnosis, which is their term.
- **Braun and Clarke (2021), "Can I use TA?"** on themes as analytic outputs
  rather than as things that emerge.
- **Connelly and Peltzer (2016)** on the practical difference between a
  category and a theme, if students want a second formulation.
- **Sandelowski and Leeman (2012)** on writing qualitative findings so that they
  carry a claim.
- **Braun and Clarke (2023)** on over-claiming in qualitative write-ups,
  including causal language.

References are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the tool does not
appear to derive its invented clusters from any published study.
