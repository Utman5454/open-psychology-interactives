# Teaching notes — Thematic Analysis Coding Laboratory

`modules/research-methods/tools/05-thematic-analysis-coding-lab/`

The tool never says which code is right. If a student finds that frustrating,
the frustration is the seminar.

---

## Running it from the front

Extract 3 is the one to slow down on. Read the semantic pass and the critical pass aloud one after the other. The semantic pass gives you "finding out about extensions informally". The critical pass gives you "the rules are learnt through networks, so who you know decides what you get". Both are in the words; neither is extracted from them.

If a student asks which pass is correct, the honest answer is that the question is malformed. A pass is answerable to the data, to the research question, and to whether its assumptions have been stated — not to a key.

## Intended level

Second-year undergraduate meeting qualitative analysis for the first time, and
final-year project students about to code their own interviews. No prior
qualitative experience is assumed. It is the first of three qualitative tools in
this module that share the same fictional dataset; the natural order is this
one, then **Theme or Topic?**, then **Reflexivity and Alternative Theme
Builder**.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. describe coding as an analytic act rather than an extraction;
2. produce both semantic and more interpretive codes for the same extract;
3. say what a coding decision brings forward and what it pushes out of view;
4. distinguish a code from a filing category and from an imported theoretical
   diagnosis;
5. name at least one assumption they brought to an extract before coding it.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 12 minutes — extracts 3 and 4 are enough.
- **Students in pairs:** 30 minutes for all six.
- **With the challenge and debrief:** 45 minutes.

## Preparation

None, though the session goes better if students have read a short account of
reflexive thematic analysis first, so that "reflexive" is not heard as
"introspective".

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

Extract 3 — Priya, the extension and "the trick". Read the semantic pass and the
situated pass aloud, one after the other.

The semantic pass gives you *finding out about extensions informally*. The
situated pass gives you *the hidden curriculum of entitlement*, *rules that are
published and not communicated*, *advantage reproduced through who you happen to
live with*.

Both are in the words. Neither was extracted from them. Then ask the room which
one they would have written, and — the real question — what research question
they were imagining when they wrote it.

Follow with extract 4 and the bank code **low academic self-efficacy**. Tick it
and read the note aloud. It is the cleanest example in the tool of a construct
arriving dressed as a description.

## Prediction question

*Two researchers code the same interview and produce noticeably different codes.
In a reflexive thematic analysis, what does that tell you?*

All four options get a written response. The two that matter are:

- **"The coding is unreliable, agree a scheme and recode"** — the right move for
  content analysis, and it removes the instrument this method relies on.
- **"Coding is subjective, so any reading is as good as any other"** — the
  misreading worth catching in week one. Rejecting agreement as the criterion is
  not the same as having no criterion, and several bank codes exist to prove it.

## Activity sequence

1. **Commit to an answer** on the opening question.
2. **Extract 1 (Ama).** Write a code before ticking anything. Then tick
   *Low confidence* and read what it does.
3. **Compare with three passes.** Ask which pass the student's own code sits
   closest to, and whether they still agree with their own semantic/latent
   classification after reading them.
4. **Extract 3 (Priya).** The demonstration above.
5. **Extract 4 (Kwame).** The construct-as-code problem.
6. **Extract 5 (Lena).** The only positive case, and the reflexive prompt asks
   directly whether the student coded it more thinly than the others. Most have.
7. **Extract 6 (Toby).** The participant does the analyst's job aloud. The
   reflexive prompt asks whether that made them trust him more.
8. **The challenge**, which is about what a code is *for*.

## Debrief questions

1. Three passes over Ama's extract produce three different sets of codes. On
   what grounds could a reader reject one of them?
2. What is the difference between "help-seeking as an admission of deficit" and
   "participant has low academic self-efficacy"? Both sound analytic.
3. Lena's extract is the only one where asking went well. What happens to the
   eventual themes if it gets three codes and the others get twelve?
4. Toby's extract supports the situated reading most strongly, and he is the
   least typical participant. What should the write-up say about that?
5. In-vivo codes keep the participant's own words. What can they not do?
6. If agreement is not the criterion, what is? Give three things a reader is
   entitled to.

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "Which code is the right one?" | The question is malformed. A code is answerable to the data, to the research question, and to whether its assumptions are stated. |
| "We should calculate inter-rater reliability." | Appropriate to content analysis and to coding-reliability designs. In reflexive TA it removes the analyst's engagement, which is the instrument. |
| "So it is all subjective." | Tick *Not trying hard enough* on extract 2, or *low academic self-efficacy* on extract 4. Both are indefensible for stateable reasons. |
| "Latent coding is more advanced." | It reaches further and owes more. A semantic pass over Toby's extract performs very well, because he did the analytic work aloud. |
| "The software does the analysis." | Every act in this laboratory happens before the software would be any use. Packages store, retrieve and count. |
| "Coding is the analysis." | Coding is the beginning. Themes are developed later and across the whole dataset — which is the next tool. |
| "Reflexivity means writing about my feelings." | It means saying where you were standing and what that made visible and invisible. Each prompt is written to model that. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **Six short extracts are not a dataset.** Real coding happens across whole
  transcripts, where a phrase means something different because of what came
  twenty minutes earlier.
- **The extracts are invented** for teaching. No participant, interview,
  institution or study is represented.
- **Three passes are not the three possibilities.** They make the range
  visible. Narrative, psychosocial or feminist readings would produce others.
- **Coding is not the analysis**, and a tidy codebook does not guarantee a good
  theme.
- **Nothing here measures the learner.** No code is scored, stored, transmitted
  or compared with anyone else's.

## Accessibility considerations

- Native text input, select, checkboxes and buttons; every control labelled,
  every group in a fieldset with a legend.
- **There are no charts.** Every output is text, so there is no visual-only
  information needing a paired table.
- The extract and the learner's own code are pinned to the top of the stage, so
  the words being coded stay visible while the controls beside them are used.
- Each coding pass names its level in its own heading and prints *Brings
  forward:* and *Puts in the background:* as labelled sentences, so the coloured
  border is redundant. Each bank code prints its level as text above the label.
- The stage track marks current and compared extracts with visually hidden text
  as well as styling.
- Focus moves to the laboratory heading on unlock; forced-colours rules
  provided; usable at 320px and at projector widths.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Code a seventh extract.** Write four sentences in the voice of a student
   who has never needed to ask for anything, and code it three ways.
2. **The codebook audit.** Take all six extracts' situated passes and ask what
   study they add up to. Then do the same for the semantic passes. Which one
   could be written up, and what would each be about?
3. **Reverse the reflexive prompt.** For any extract, write the paragraph a
   researcher would put in their methods section about the position they coded
   from.
4. **Find the smuggled construct.** Take a published qualitative paper and find
   one code that is a theoretical label rather than a description of something
   noticed.

## The model

There is no model in the statistical sense and no scoring anywhere. The data
structure is:

- six extracts, each with a **bank of six candidate codes** tagged `semantic`,
  `latent` or `critical` (displayed as *Situated*), each carrying a written note
  on what that code does and what it costs;
- three **coding passes** per extract — *staying close to what is said*,
  *reading for what the speaker is doing*, *reading for the setting that makes
  this sayable* — each with its codes, a "brings forward" sentence and a "puts
  in the background" sentence;
- one **reflexive prompt** per extract with four positions, each with a note.

Three bank codes are deliberately indefensible and are there to be ticked:

| Extract | Code | What is wrong with it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 (Ama) | Low confidence | Converts a situation into a property of a person; the extract does not say it |
| 2 (Jonah) | Not trying hard enough | A judgement wearing the clothes of a description; makes the second attempt disappear |
| 4 (Kwame) | Low academic self-efficacy | A construct with a literature and an implied cause, none of it in the extract |
| 5 (Lena) | Being brave | A flattering code that relocates the phenomenon into character and makes the seminar unchangeable |
| 6 (Toby) | Mature student perspective | A filing category, not a code |

Note that two of these are complimentary. Flattering codes deserve the same
suspicion as critical ones, and students find that harder.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Braun and Clarke (2006; 2019; 2022)** — the whole tool is built on their
  account, particularly the 2019 paper on what "reflexive" adds and why coding
  reliability is not the criterion.
- **Braun and Clarke (2021), "One size fits all?"** on the difference between
  reflexive TA, coding reliability TA and codebook TA. If a student wants to
  calculate agreement, that paper says when they should.
- **Byrne (2022)** for a worked example of a reflexive TA written up
  transparently.
- **Finlay (2002)** on reflexivity as method rather than confession.
- **Charmaz (2014)** on in-vivo codes and on coding as construction rather than
  discovery.

References are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the tool does not
appear to derive its invented extracts from any published study.
