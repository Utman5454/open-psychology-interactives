Research Methods

Sampling Bias Simulator

A fictional population of 4,000 students whose true average you are allowed to see. Recruit from it five different ways, decide who declines, and watch the difference between an estimate that scatters and one that is systematically wrong.

Simulated — a generated population, drawn from a documented seed

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to separate sampling variability from selection bias, explain why increasing the sample size cures one and not the other, and say what a probability sample still needs in order to stay unbiased.

About 25 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

You can see the truth; a researcher cannot

The population mean is printed on screen because the tool generated the population. Every argument in the debrief turns on the fact that a real study has no such line to check itself against, and that a biased estimate looks exactly like an unbiased one from the inside.

  1. Answer the opening question to unlock the simulator.
  2. Pick a recruitment method and press Recruit a sample.
  3. Press it again several times and watch where the dots land.
  4. Now raise the sample size, and watch what does and does not change.

Notes for whoever is running this

The sequence that makes the point: choose convenience, press Recruit 20 samples, and note where the cloud of dots sits. Then drag the sample size to 1,000 and press it again. The cloud gets much tighter and does not move towards the line. That is the whole distinction, and no amount of talking lands it as well.

Then choose simple random sample, which sits on the line, and raise the non-response tilt to 90%. The dots march away again. A probability sample is only unbiased while the people it selects actually take part.

Fuller guidance and the debrief questions are in teaching-notes.md beside this page.

Challenge — which changes reduce the bias?

A survey of study hours has been run by advertising in the library and has produced an estimate that is 2.6 hours too high. The team has budget for exactly one change.

Which of these would reduce the bias, as opposed to the scatter? Select all that apply.

What this demonstrates

Scatter and offset are different failures

Sampling variability is why two honest studies of the same population report different numbers: each drew a different set of people, and the estimate wobbles around what it is aiming at. Selection bias is why a whole series of studies can wobble around the wrong thing. Only the first shrinks when the sample grows.

A large biased sample is worse than a small one

Not because the estimate is further from the truth — it is not — but because the interval around it is narrower and the p-value smaller, so the wrong answer is asserted with more confidence and is harder to argue with. This is precisely what happened to some famous election polls with sample sizes in the millions.

Probability sampling is a claim about the whole process

Randomly selecting names from a register makes the estimate unbiased only if the people selected actually respond. Raise the non-response tilt and the random sample drifts as far off as the convenience sample. The randomness protects the selection step; it does nothing about the step where people decide whether to answer.

Quotas fix what you measured and nothing else

Filling year-of-study quotas by convenience produces a sample with exactly the right year composition and the wrong answer, because commuting and part-time work were never balanced. Every weighting and quota scheme has this shape: it corrects the variables you thought of, and leaves the ones you did not.

What this does not demonstrate

  • The population is generated, not observed. Four thousand fictional students built from a seeded generator with a stated model. No real institution, register or survey is involved.
  • The selection weights are invented. How much more likely a commuter is to be in the library on a Tuesday is a number chosen to make the effect visible, not a measured quantity.
  • Real non-response is not one dial. Here it depends on two attributes in a simple way. In practice it depends on the topic, the mode, the incentive, the season and the sender, and it varies over the fieldwork period.
  • Bias in the mean is not the only bias. Selection can distort variances, correlations and subgroup comparisons in ways an average never reveals, and sometimes in opposite directions.
  • Unbiased is not accurate. A simple random sample of 20 from this population is unbiased and still frequently more than an hour out. Unbiasedness is a property of the procedure over many repetitions, not a promise about your one study.
