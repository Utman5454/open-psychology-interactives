# Teaching notes - The Normal Curve and z-Scores

`modules/research-methods/tools/13-normal-curve-z-scores/`

**What it teaches.** A normal distribution is fully described by its
mean and standard deviation. A z-score is a change of units, not a
change of shape, so standardising never normalises a distribution.
Density, the curve's height, is not a probability; only shaded area
is, and the familiar 68/95/99.7 figures are properties of the normal
model, not of any real dataset.

**Before students start.** The 68/95/99.7 figures describe the model,
not real data. Flag this before anyone applies them to a dataset that
has not been checked for normality.

**What students do.**
1. Predict what percentage of a Normal(50,10) distribution lies above
   a score of 70.
2. Drag the mean, standard deviation and a raw score, and read the
   resulting z-score, percentile and shaded area.
3. Highlight the one, two and three standard deviation bands and
   compare their areas.
4. In Experiment 2, judge which of two tests makes a shared mark more
   unusual before revealing both z-scores.
5. Sort the closing challenge on what standardising does and does not
   do.

**What to look for.** The same raw mark of 62 sits near the 98th
percentile on one test and only around the 68th on another, purely
because the two tests have different means and spreads. The peak of
the curve falls every time the spread is increased, since the area
underneath must stay at one.

**Debrief.** Why must the peak of the curve fall when the standard
deviation rises? A mark of 62 was near the 98th percentile on one test
and only the 68th on another; what does a raw mark tell you on its
own? A colleague says "the data are roughly normal, so about 68% are
within one standard deviation." What would you want to check before
agreeing?

**Common misconception / caution.** "About 5% are above a z of 2"
confuses the two-tailed area outside plus or minus two standard
deviations with the one-tailed area above z equals 2, which is closer
to 2.3%. The familiar 68/95/99.7 figures are properties of the normal
model, and real datasets, reaction times, income, symptom counts,
routinely depart from them.

**Use in class / timing.** Eight minutes covers Experiment 1 as a
demonstration. Both experiments with the challenge and debrief take
about 30 minutes.
