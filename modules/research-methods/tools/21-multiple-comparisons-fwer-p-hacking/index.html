Research Methods

Multiple Comparisons, FWER and Forking Paths

Run enough tests on nothing and something will come out significant. The uncomfortable part is that you do not have to run them deliberately, or even know that you did.

Simulated — generated data containing no effect at all

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to say what family a family-wise error rate belongs to, and how analytic flexibility inflates false positives without anyone cheating.

About 30 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

This is not a technique

The second experiment lets you search a fictional dataset until something comes out significant. It is there to show you what that search does to the meaning of the number you end up with, and every choice it offers is one a careful researcher might make in good faith. Nobody in this laboratory is cheating. That is exactly why the problem is hard, and why the answer is transparency about what was decided when, rather than better intentions.

  1. Answer the prediction question to open Experiment 1.
  2. Run a family of tests where every null is true, and count the hits.
  3. Apply a Bonferroni correction and read both of its consequences.
  4. In Experiment 2, analyse one empty dataset until something works.

First, a prediction

A fictional study runs 20 independent tests at α = .05. Every single null hypothesis is true: there is nothing to find anywhere in the data.

What is the probability that at least one of the twenty comes out significant?

Experiment 1 — the family of tests

Each square below is one test in one simulated experiment. A square is marked when that test came out significant. Most of the tests have nothing to find; you can give a few of them a real effect and watch what a correction does to those as well.

Key terms
Family
The set of tests a claim is being made across. The error rate here is a property of that set, not of any test inside it.
Family-wise error rate
The probability of at least one false positive somewhere in the family, when every null in it is true.
Bonferroni correction
Dividing α by the number of tests. It has a second consequence as well as the intended one.
Forking paths
The many analysis decisions available, each defensible on its own, which between them produce many possible results from one dataset.

Experiment 2 — the garden of forking paths

    Challenge — four studies, four practices

    Multiplicity is not one thing. Label each of these four fictional studies. Only one of the four is a problem, and it is not the one with the most tests in it.

    Study A

    A trial names one primary outcome and three secondary outcomes in its protocol before any data are collected, states that the three secondaries will be tested at a corrected threshold, and reports all four results whichever way they came out.

    Study B

    A team measures twelve variables with no prior hypotheses, looks for patterns, reports everything it measured and everything it tried, and describes the findings as hypothesis-generating and in need of an independent replication.

    Study C

    A team measures four outcomes and tries two exclusion rules. One combination reaches p = .04. The paper reports that outcome and that exclusion rule, describes them as the study's analysis, and does not mention the others.

    Study D

    A preregistered study states one hypothesis, one outcome, one exclusion rule and one analysis, collects the data, runs that analysis and reports the result. It came out non-significant and was published anyway.

    What this demonstrates

    The error rate belongs to the family, not to the test

    Each individual test in Experiment 1 is behaving exactly as advertised: a 5% chance of a false positive, every time. Nothing is broken. What changes is the question you are implicitly asking — "did anything come out?" — and the answer to that question has its own error rate, 1 − (1 − α)k, which reaches a half by fourteen tests and 64% by twenty. Deciding which tests belong in the family is a judgement about the claim being made, not a property of the software, and it is the judgement that most corrections quietly depend on.

    A correction is a trade, and both sides are visible

    Bonferroni tests each hypothesis at α/k, which does what it promises: the family-wise false-positive rate drops back to about α. The readout shows the price in the same breath. With five real effects among twenty tests, the correction takes the detection rate for those real effects down sharply, because every one of them is now being judged against a threshold twenty times stricter. There is no setting of that dial that gives you both, and choosing where to sit on it is a decision about which mistake you would rather make.

    Flexibility does the same damage without any tests being run

    In Experiment 2 the analyst runs one test at a time and reports one p-value. There is no correction to apply, because on the face of it there is nothing to correct for. But the dataset contains no effect at all, and among the seventy-two defensible analyses of it there are almost always several below .05. The number that ends up in the paper is not a random draw from a null distribution. It is the smallest of however many were looked at, including the ones considered and abandoned before anybody clicked anything. That is the garden of forking paths, and it does its damage whether or not the paths were consciously walked.

    Four practices that look alike and are not

    Planned multiplicity is knowing in advance that you will run several tests and saying so, usually with a correction. Exploratory analysis is looking without a prior hypothesis and labelling the result as provisional; it is legitimate and useful, and it is not confirmatory evidence. Undisclosed selective reporting is running several analyses and presenting one as though it were the plan; this is the only one of the four that is a problem, and it rarely feels like misconduct from the inside. Confirmatory inference is testing a stated hypothesis with a stated analysis fixed before the data, and it is the only route by which a p-value means what it says. Preregistration is not a moral test — it is simply a record of which of these four you are doing.

    What this does not demonstrate

    • No real study, and no real dataset. Everything is generated from a documented seed, and the ninety students in Experiment 2 are invented. The generating model contains no group difference on anything, which is why every significant result you find there is a false one by construction.
    • Independence is assumed in Experiment 1 and false in Experiment 2. The formula 1 − (1 − α)k requires independent tests. Real families of tests overlap, which usually makes the true family-wise rate somewhat lower than the formula says — and never as low as α.
    • Bonferroni is not the only correction, or the best one. Holm's step-down procedure controls the same rate with more power and no extra assumptions; false-discovery-rate procedures control a different and often more appropriate quantity. The tool uses Bonferroni because it is the one whose arithmetic can be read off the screen.
    • Seventy-two is a small garden. A real analysis has more outcomes, more covariates, more ways to code a variable and more decisions about who counts as a participant. The count here is bounded only because a menu had to fit on a page.
    • Correcting after the fact is not a repair. Applying a correction to the analyses you happened to run does not recover the ones you thought about and did not, and no statistical procedure can reconstruct a decision process that was not recorded. The remedy is a record, not a formula.
