Research Methods

ANCOVA / MANOVA Decision Laboratory

Before either of these analyses is a calculation, it is a decision: what is this variable for? Assign the roles, watch a covariate adjustment happen — and watch it stop meaning what people think it means.

Simulated — fictional studies and generated data

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to say what a covariate buys you, and why it is precision rather than equivalence.

About 30 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

Neither analysis rescues a design

A covariate cannot turn two intact groups into a randomised trial, and a multivariate test is not a way to run several analyses without paying for them. Both are useful tools with narrow jobs, and most of the harm they do comes from being reached for as repairs. Several of the scenarios in this laboratory have the answer "neither", and saying so is the skill being practised.

  1. Assign the roles in the opening scenario and choose an analysis.
  2. In Experiment 1, adjust for a covariate and watch the adjustment move.
  3. Break the equal-slopes assumption and see what the adjusted difference becomes.
  4. In Experiment 2, compare two outcomes one at a time with the two of them together.

First, a decision

Scenario — the September test

A fictional evaluation randomly assigns 80 students either to a new tutoring programme or to the usual timetable. Everyone sits a baseline test in September, before allocation, and an outcome test in June. The question is whether the programme raised June scores.

What role does each variable play?
Which analysis fits this design best?

Experiment 1 — what adjustment actually does

Eighty fictional students, forty in each group. The horizontal axis is the September score, the vertical axis is the June score, and each group has its own regression line. The adjusted difference is the vertical gap between those lines, measured at the overall September mean.

Key terms
Covariate
A variable measured before the outcome and used to sharpen the comparison. Sharpening it is not the same as making the groups equivalent.
Adjusted difference
The group difference the model estimates at a common covariate value. It answers a conditional question.
Equal slopes
The assumption that the covariate relates to the outcome the same way in each group. Without it there is no single adjusted difference to report.
MANOVA
Testing two or more outcomes jointly rather than one at a time. What it adds depends on how the outcomes correlate.

The covariate adjustment

Adjustment asks a conditional question: how far apart would the groups be if they had started at the same place? Whether that question has an answer depends on the design, not on the software.

Challenge — four studies, four decisions

One choice per study. More than one of these has the answer "neither".

Study 1 — two intact classes

The tutoring programme is given to one whole class and not to another. The classes were not randomly formed and differ by about nine points on the September test. The researchers want the causal effect of the programme on June scores.

Study 2 — four reading measures

A randomised study of a reading intervention measures speed, accuracy, comprehension and enjoyment. The team argues that these four are facets of one construct and wants a single omnibus test of whether reading performance differs.

Study 3 — three unrelated outcomes

A randomised study measures hours of sleep, an exam mark and self-reported height. The team plans a MANOVA "so that we only have to run one test and do not have to correct for multiple comparisons".

Study 4 — the covariate measured afterwards

In a randomised trial of the tutoring programme, the team notices that students in the programme attended more sessions overall. They propose adding attendance as a covariate, "to control for engagement".

What this demonstrates

A covariate's main job is precision, not correction

Under random allocation the groups already start in roughly the same place, so adjusting barely moves the estimated difference. What it moves is the residual variance: the covariate explains part of the variation in the outcome that would otherwise be noise, and the comparison becomes more precise. That is a good enough reason to use one, and it is a completely different reason from the one usually given.

Adjustment answers a conditional question

The adjusted difference is the vertical gap between two regression lines at a particular value of the covariate. When groups were randomly formed, that gap estimates the causal effect. When they were not, it estimates what the difference would look like among people matched on the covariate — which is a real quantity, and is not the causal effect unless nothing else differs between the groups. Reaching for ANCOVA to repair non-equivalent groups is the most common misuse of the technique, and the tool shows that the arithmetic gives you no warning at all.

Equal slopes is an assumption with a visible cost

If the two groups have different regression slopes, there is no single adjusted difference to report. The gap between the lines depends on where you measure it, and the disclosure in Experiment 1 shows three different answers from the same data. That is not a technicality but a finding — the effect depends on the covariate, which is a covariate-by-treatment interaction and is worth reporting as one.

Multivariate is a different question, not a cheaper one

A multivariate test asks whether the group centres are separated in the space defined by all the outcomes together, using the correlations among them. When the outcomes are highly correlated and the differences point the same way, it adds very little — the outcomes are one thing measured twice. When the differences run across the within-group correlation, it can find a separation far larger than anything visible on either axis. Both are real; neither makes it a device for running several tests without paying for them, and the follow-up univariate tests inherit the whole multiplicity problem.

What this does not demonstrate

  • No real study. The tutoring programme, the reading intervention and every number in them are invented. The data are generated from a stated model with a documented seed.
  • No test statistics. The tool reports adjusted differences and separations in standard-deviation units rather than F or Wilks' lambda, because what matters here is what the quantities mean, and a test statistic invites the discussion to collapse into a threshold.
  • Two outcomes, not many. Experiment 2 uses two so that the plane can be drawn. Real multivariate analyses with five or ten outcomes behave the same way in principle and are far harder to see, which is a reason for caution rather than confidence.
  • The design menu changes nothing computational. Switching between random allocation and intact classes alters only the interpretation offered. That is the honest state of affairs: the software cannot tell how the groups were formed, and neither can the numbers.
  • Causal inference has a larger literature than this. Propensity methods, instrumental variables, difference-in- differences and regression discontinuity all address non-equivalent groups more carefully than covariate adjustment, and each carries assumptions of its own.
