The Alpha Trap

Build a questionnaire scale by choosing items, and watch how easily Cronbach’s alpha can be pushed upwards by asking the same question in five different ways.

Simulated data — a fictional item bank, not a real questionnaire

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to say what a high alpha does and does not tell you about a scale.

About 20 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

How to use this

  1. Commit to a prediction before you touch the item bank.
  2. Round 1. Tick items to push alpha to 0.85 or above. Any items you like.
  3. Look at what your scale turned out to measure.
  4. Round 2. Build a scale you would actually defend, and compare the two.
  5. Work through the two challenges at the foot of the page.

First, a prediction

You are about to be asked to get Cronbach’s alpha above 0.85 using a bank of 24 items measuring “academic conscientiousness”. Commit to an answer now — the point of the exercise is largely lost if you read the outcome first.

1. The construct has five content areas. How many do you think your alpha-chasing scale will end up covering?
2. Suppose we then correlate that scale with a relevant outcome. What would you expect?

Key terms
Cronbach's alpha
An index of how consistently a set of items covary. It is a function of two things: how many items there are, and how strongly they correlate on average.
Mean inter-item correlation
How strongly the items agree with each other, averaged over every pair.
Unidimensionality
Whether the items are measuring one thing. It is a different question from internal consistency.
Construct validity
Whether the scale measures what it claims to. Answering it needs evidence from outside the scale itself.
Near-duplicate items
Items whose wording is almost the same as each other's.

What this demonstrates

Alpha is a function of two things you control

Cronbach’s alpha rises with the number of items and with the average correlation between them. Neither is a fact about the construct. Write an item five times over and both go up at once. That is how a scale of paraphrases reaches 0.91 while covering a fifth of the ground the construct occupies.

Internal consistency is not unidimensionality

A high alpha is often read as “the items measure one thing”. It does not establish that. Alpha can be high for a clearly multidimensional scale, simply because it has many items; and it can be high because one narrow cluster dominates. Deciding how many dimensions there are is a question for factor analysis, not for alpha.

Internal consistency is not validity

Reliability limits validity but does not supply it. In this simulation, adding near-duplicates loads the total score with variance specific to one phrasing — variance the outcome does not care about. The scale becomes a more precise measure of something narrower. That is the bloated specific factor, and it is a real failure mode in published scales, not an artefact of this demonstration.

Very high alpha is a warning, not a prize

For a broad construct, an alpha above about 0.90 in a short scale usually means the items repeat each other. The useful question is never “is alpha high enough?” but “is this scale made of items that between them cover what I claim to be measuring?”

What this does not demonstrate

  • These are not real data. Every correlation comes from a fictional model written for teaching. No respondents were measured, and no number here is a norm, a validated coefficient or a cut-off.
  • No sampling error is modelled. The figures are the values the model implies for a population. A real alpha is an estimate from a sample and carries a confidence interval, which is often wider than people expect.
  • Alpha is not being condemned. It remains a useful summary of internal consistency, and a very low alpha is a genuine problem. The argument here is against treating it as a sufficient certificate of quality.
  • The construct is invented. “Academic conscientiousness” and its five areas were written for this page. Nothing here should be read as a claim about the structure of conscientiousness, and the items are not a usable questionnaire.
  • Breadth is not automatically better. A scale deliberately built to measure one narrow facet is perfectly legitimate — provided it is described that way. The failure modelled here is claiming broad coverage while measuring narrowly.
  • This does not assess you. Nothing here measures anybody. You are choosing items, not answering them.
