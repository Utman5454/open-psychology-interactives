# Teaching notes - The Alpha Trap

`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/12-alpha-trap/`

**What it teaches.** Cronbach's alpha rises with the number of items and with how strongly they correlate, and neither is a property of the construct being measured. A scale can climb in alpha while narrowing to a fraction of what it claims to cover, because near-duplicate items inflate the denominator of a validity coefficient without adding anything to its numerator.

**Before students start.** None needed. Decide whether the room predicts individually on the page or as a show of hands; the page has a skip button for the second option.

**What students do.**
1. Predict how many of the five content areas the final scale will cover, and what it will correlate with a relevant outcome.
2. Press "add another way of asking it" five times, reading alpha and effective content areas each time, and the outcome correlation from the fifth press onward.
3. Drop the repeated items and compare the two scales side by side.
4. Diagnose three fictional published scales, then predict and check what happens when the repaired scale has its repeats added back.

**What to look for.** Alpha climbs from .684 to .852 over the five additions while the outcome correlation peaks after the first addition, at .581, then falls to .536 by the fifth. "Content areas covered" stays at five out of five throughout, which is why the readout also carries effective content areas, a figure that drops from 5.00 to 2.50 over the same five presses.

**Debrief.** A reviewer writes "the reliability of this scale is excellent, alpha equals .93." What has that established, and what has it not? When is a low alpha the more informative result?

**Common misconception / caution.** Students often conclude that a high alpha means the items measure one thing. Alpha is not a dimensionality test, though, and this scale manages to end up both unidimensional and narrow, a separate problem again. No sampling error is modelled anywhere in the tool, so none of these figures carry a confidence interval, and a very high alpha in a short scale is a redundancy warning rather than a mark of quality.

**Use in class / timing.** A tight demonstration runs 8 to 10 minutes. With both challenges and the debrief, allow 30 to 35.
