Personality and Individual Differences

Facet-Level Detective

Two people, the same broad trait score, and behaviour that looks nothing alike. Work out what the broad score is hiding.

Simulated data — fictional people, illustrative scores

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to say what a broad domain score averages away, and why two people with the same score can behave differently.

About 20 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

How to use this

  1. Commit to an explanation before seeing any facet scores.
  2. Read four observations and decide, from behaviour alone, which of the two people each one describes.
  3. Reveal the facets and check your reasoning.
  4. Build your own pair of profiles in the studio.
  5. Finally, change the questionnaire rather than the people, and watch the "fixed" broad score move.

    What this demonstrates

    A broad score is an average, and averages destroy information

    This is arithmetic before it is psychology. If a domain score is the mean of narrower scores, then every pair of facet values with the same mean produces the same domain score. The broad score is not failing when it treats these two people identically; it is doing exactly what an average does.

    Breadth buys prediction of breadth

    None of this makes broad traits useless. Broad scores predict broad outcomes — aggregates of many behaviours over long periods — better than any single facet does, and they are more stable and more replicable. The mistake is using a broad score to predict a narrow, specific behaviour, which is the one job it is worst at. Matching the breadth of your predictor to the breadth of what you want to predict is the whole of the bandwidth–fidelity trade-off.

    The narrow level is less stable than it looks

    Facets are not simply a better version of domains. There is less agreement about how many facets a domain has and where the boundaries fall; facet scores are usually built from fewer items and so are measured less reliably; and facet findings replicate less consistently. Going narrower buys specificity and pays for it in precision and in consensus.

    The broad score belongs to the questionnaire as well as the person

    The final stage is the uncomfortable one. Two published measures of the same domain that sample its facets in different proportions can rank the same two people in opposite orders, and both can be perfectly reliable. When a study reports a domain score, part of what it reports is a decision about item sampling made by whoever wrote the questionnaire.

    What this does not demonstrate

    • These are not data. The people are invented and the scores are illustrative values on an arbitrary scale, with no norms, no standard error and no reference sample. Nobody was measured.
    • Two facets per domain is a simplification. Facet systems in use have between two and six per domain, and they do not agree with each other. The two-facet structure here is enough to make the arithmetic visible and no more.
    • Behaviour is not this tidy. The model turns facet scores into behavioural tendencies with a straight line and no error, no situation and no interaction between traits. Real behaviour has all three — the Person–Situation Interaction Theatre in this module is where situations enter.
    • Facets are not more "real" than domains. Nothing here shows that the narrow level is the true one. Both levels are descriptions at a chosen grain, and which is appropriate depends on what you are trying to predict.
    • No one is being assessed. You are reading about fictional people, not answering anything about yourself. The tool produces no score for its user and no personality description of anybody real.
    • Facet names are borrowed, values are invented. The facet pairs are named after a published two-aspect account of the Big Five so that students learn vocabulary that exists. No published item, norm or finding is reproduced.
