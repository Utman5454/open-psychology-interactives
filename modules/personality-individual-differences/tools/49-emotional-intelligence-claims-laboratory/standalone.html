Learning objective

By the end you should be able to tell convergent evidence from method overlap, and say what incremental validity asks that a raw correlation does not.

About 30 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

What these measures are

All six are fictional descriptions of method types — a questionnaire, tasks with scored answers, ratings by other people. No published emotional-intelligence measure is reproduced, quoted, paraphrased or scored here, and no number on this page is an estimate of any real quantity. The tool takes no position on whether emotional intelligence exists. It is about what a correlation matrix does and does not license somebody to advertise.

How to use this

  1. Commit to an answer to the question below.
  2. Read the correlation matrix, then reorder it by method and read it again.
  3. Use the three sliders to ask what would have to be true for the shared label to be honest.
  4. Judge the five marketing claims against the evidence as you have it set.

First, a judgement

A fictional research group administers four measures, all described in the literature as measures of emotional intelligence: a questionnaire about how you usually handle feelings, a task whose answers are scored against a key, an emotion-recognition accuracy test, and ratings of social effectiveness by colleagues.

Challenge — five things somebody wants to say

Each of these is the kind of sentence that appears in a brochure, a press release or a training-day slide. Judge each one against the evidence as you currently have the laboratory set. There is no stored answer key: change the settings and some of these verdicts change with them.

Claim 1. "Our emotional-intelligence questionnaire measures a distinct capability that ordinary personality questionnaires miss."
Claim 2. "Emotional intelligence predicts effectiveness at work better than IQ does."
Claim 3. "Because our task has right and wrong answers, it measures an ability rather than a self-image."
Claim 4. "All of these measures assess the same underlying emotional intelligence."
Claim 5. "Scoring high on our emotion-recognition test shows you will be socially effective."

What this demonstrates

A label is not a construct

Three of the measures here carry the same name and behave differently. That is not a quirk of the simulation: it is the ordinary consequence of a name being attached to measures built on different principles. A questionnaire asks what you think you are like. A scored task asks what you can do. These are different questions, and there is no reason in advance to expect the same answer.

Method leaves fingerprints, but it is not the whole story

Two questionnaires correlate partly because both are questionnaires: the same person's tendency to endorse positive statements about themselves shows up in both. Turn that effect off and the overlap between the self-report scale and the personality questionnaire shrinks, but does not disappear. What is left is content overlap — the scale is largely asking about dispositions already covered by agreeableness and emotional stability. "It is only method variance" is a testable claim, and here it fails.

Incremental validity is the question worth asking

A measure that correlates with an outcome has cleared a low bar. The question a sceptical reviewer asks is what it adds once the obvious, cheaper measures are already in the model. In this matrix the self-report scale correlates respectably with observed effectiveness, and adds very little once a personality questionnaire is in — while the performance task, which correlates less, adds more. Raw correlations and incremental contributions can point in opposite directions.

Construct proliferation

New constructs are cheap to name and expensive to test. The discipline's protection against this is not scepticism about names but a routine: show that measures of the construct converge with each other, diverge from measures of established constructs, and add something to prediction. When those three checks are run and reported honestly, an attractive label costs nothing. When they are not, the label does the work the evidence has not.

Why the good version matters too

The "If the label were true" setting is not a joke condition. It shows that the evidence which would justify the claim is obtainable, describable in advance, and not particularly exotic. The complaint made here is not that emotional intelligence could not be a real and useful construct. It is that a name shared by measures which correlate at this level has not yet earned the treatment it gets.

What this does not demonstrate

  • Nothing here settles whether emotional intelligence exists. The correlations are generated by a model with three sliders. A real debate is conducted with real data, and serious researchers disagree about it in good faith.
  • No real measure is reproduced or evaluated. The six descriptions are fictional method types. No published questionnaire, task, item or scoring key appears here, and no specific test is being criticised.
  • The numbers are not estimates. They follow from the sliders. They are chosen to make a teaching pattern visible, not to represent any published finding, and should never be quoted as effect sizes.
  • Self-report is not worthless. For some questions — how somebody experiences their own emotional life — asking them is the appropriate method, and a performance task would be the wrong instrument. The problem here is a mismatch between what one method can support and what is being claimed.
  • Scored tasks are not automatically better. A consensus key defines the correct answer as the one most people give, which is a defensible choice with real limitations, and such tasks share variance with ordinary cognitive ability.
  • Nobody is assessed. The tool produces no score for anyone, asks nothing about the person using it, and cannot tell you anything about your own emotional abilities.
  • Six measures and one outcome is a simplification. Real construct-validation programmes use many indicators, multiple outcomes, several samples, and confirmatory models rather than a single matrix.
