# Teaching notes — Personality Disorder Continuum

`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/55-personality-disorder-continuum/`

Identical trait extremity, two very different profiles, and a category line the
students move themselves.

---

## Before you run this

Say three things out loud before anyone touches the tool.

1. **Nobody is being diagnosed, including themselves.** The tool asks nothing
   about the person using it, produces no score for them, and returns no
   diagnosis for any profile. There is no character to judge and no named
   condition anywhere in it.
2. **The difficulties these labels point at are real and often severe.** The
   argument here is about what evidence supports what conclusion, not about
   whether people struggle.
3. **Some people in the room will have a personal connection to this** — their
   own diagnosis, a family member's, or clinical placement experience. Say that
   the session is about concepts and thresholds, and that nobody is expected to
   share anything.

If you are teaching this in a clinical or applied programme, the stigma section
of the debrief is the part not to skip.

## Intended level

Second or third-year undergraduate, or postgraduate conversion. It assumes
familiarity with trait dimensions. It follows well from any tool that
establishes traits as continua, and pairs particularly well with the
Person–Situation Interaction Theatre, whose argument about situations does a
lot of work in the "difficulty in one setting only" configuration.

## Learning objectives

After the activity a student should be able to:

1. explain why unusual traits alone are not sufficient for a disorder-level
   interpretation;
2. show that identical trait levels can carry very different impairment;
3. distinguish subjective distress from functional impairment;
4. describe what changes when a continuum is cut into a category;
5. state what a comprehensive assessment covers that six numbers cannot.

## Estimated duration

- **Demonstration from the front:** 8 minutes — the first two presets and one
  rule switch.
- **Students in pairs:** 25 minutes.
- **With the challenge and the full debrief:** 45 minutes. Do not compress the
  debrief; it is where the ethical content lives.

## Preparation

Nothing to install. Open the page, answer or skip the opening question, and the
tool unlocks.

## Two steps

**Step 1** is the dimensional view: the six sliders, the profile and what it
does and does not support. **Step 2** draws a category line across whatever
step 1 was left set to: the rule, the threshold and the fictional population.
The profile is shared between them, which is what makes the second step land —
nothing about the person changes when the category does.

## The demonstration worth doing from the front

1. Load **Unusual, and working**. Trait extremity 85 — a genuinely distinctive
   person. Everything else low. Read the interpretation.
2. Load **The same traits, much more difficulty**. Point at the trait extremity
   figure: *still 85*. Everything the tool says has changed.
3. Go back to the first configuration and leave it alone. Move to **step 2**
   and switch the category rule from **Count unusual traits** to **Unusual
   traits and difficulty in functioning, together**. The profile changes sides
   without changing at all.
4. Move the threshold slider slowly from 40 to 80 and read the percentages.
   Ask what has happened to the people between those two lines.

Students find the rule switch more unsettling than the sliders. That is the
correct reaction to have to a cut-off.

## The prediction question

> **What most makes a personality pattern a clinical question rather than
> simply a personality?**

"How unusual the traits are" is the answer to dislodge, and the tool dislodges
it in one click. The fourth option — that it depends where the line is drawn
and who is drawing it — is treated as a serious answer rather than a cynical
one, because it is.

## Activity sequence

1. **Commit to an answer.**
2. **Compare the first two presets.** Ask what is identical.
3. **Turn on the second profile.** Same trait extremity, used flexibly, in
   settings that fit. Ask what would have to be true of a person's life for the
   second row to describe them.
4. **Distress without impairment.** Ask whether this person should be offered
   help. (Yes. That is the point: help does not require a category.)
5. **Difficulty in one setting only.** Ask what they would want to know about
   that setting.
6. **Switch rules and move the line.** Count who moves.
7. **The challenge**, then the debrief.

## Debrief questions

1. Two profiles with identical trait extremity. What actually differs, and why
   does that difference matter more?
2. Why does the tool refuse to include distress in the functioning composite?
3. A profile sits two points below the line. What is true of that person that
   is not true of someone two points above it?
4. If categories do not carve nature at a joint, why do services use them?
5. A pattern appears at work and nowhere else. What are the three most likely
   explanations, and in what order would you check them?
6. What would you want to know that six numbers cannot tell you? (Compare their
   list with the panel in the tool.)
7. Who is harmed when this label is applied badly, and how?

## Likely misconceptions

| What students say | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| "Extreme traits mean a disorder." | The first two presets. Identical extremity, different conclusions. |
| "If they are distressed, something is wrong with them." | The distress preset. Distress deserves help and is not evidence about personality. |
| "If they are not distressed, there is no problem." | Impairment can be substantial with little reported distress. Both dimensions exist for a reason. |
| "Dimensional models solve it." | They move the problem. Services still need thresholds, and dimensions can make difficulty harder to communicate and to fund. |
| "So the diagnoses are meaningless." | No. They point at real and often severe difficulty, and they predict outcomes and guide treatment. The argument is about how the line is drawn and used. |
| "This is basically just being difficult." | This is the stigma the debrief addresses head-on: it is precisely the reading that produces worse care. |
| "I can work out which disorder this profile is." | There is no answer to find. No named condition appears anywhere in the tool, deliberately. |

## Limitations and cautions

- **Nothing here diagnoses anybody**, and the tool cannot be used to reach a
  diagnosis for any profile, real or fictional.
- **No diagnostic material is reproduced.** No interview, criterion set,
  checklist, item or published questionnaire, and no named condition.
- **The 800 profiles are invented** to make the arithmetic of a cut-off
  visible. They are not a prevalence estimate.
- **The functioning composite is illustrative.** Its weights are chosen for
  teaching and no clinical system computes anything this way.
- **Six dimensions is a simplification** of dimensional models that use more,
  organise them into domains and facets, and are themselves contested.
- **This is not clinical training** and does not qualify anybody to assess
  anybody.

## Accessibility considerations

- Both charts are hidden from assistive technology and paired with visible
  tables. The dimensional table gives every value with its band and gains a
  second column when the comparison profile is shown; the categorical table
  states which side of the line the profile falls on under each rule and what
  proportion of the fictional population falls above it.
- Every slider announces its value as a band and restates what the dimension
  means — "rigidity low — whether the pattern can flex when a situation calls
  for something else".
- The category line is dashed and the profile is marked with a cross, so
  neither is distinguished by colour alone.
- Judged selections open with "Belongs in the answer" or "Does not belong"
  before any colour is involved.
- The content note appears before any interaction and is inside a `role="note"`
  region.
- Reduced motion honoured; forced-colours rules provided; usable at 320px and
  at projector widths.

## Optional extension tasks

1. **Write the two paragraphs.** Describe the two same-extremity profiles as a
   colleague would, without using any diagnostic vocabulary.
2. **Defend the threshold.** Argue for a specific position for the line, given
   what follows from being on either side of it in a real service.
3. **The referral letter.** What would you want to be told, and in what order,
   before meeting someone described this way?
4. **Find the stigma.** Rewrite a sentence you have heard about
   personality-disorder diagnoses so that it describes the difficulty without
   describing the person as the problem.

## The model

Also documented at the top of `tool.js`.

All six dimensions are inputs; none is computed from any other. One composite
is calculated, and only for the categorical view:

```
functioning difficulty = 0.30·rigidity + 0.25·persistence
                       + 0.25·impairment + 0.20·interpersonal impact
```

Subjective distress is deliberately absent from it, so that distress and
impairment remain distinguishable on screen.

### The two rules

| Rule | Above the line when |
| --- | --- |
| Count unusual traits | trait extremity ≥ threshold |
| Traits and difficulty together | trait extremity ≥ threshold **and** functioning difficulty ≥ threshold |

### The fictional population

800 profiles generated once from seed 51284: trait extremity ~ N(50, 18) and
functioning difficulty ~ N(28, 16), correlated at about .55, both clipped to
0–100. At the default line of 60, 29.5% fall above it under the trait-counting
rule and 1.8% under the rule that also requires impairment. Those numbers
exist to make the arithmetic visible and are not prevalence estimates.

## Citation and evidence notes

- **Livesley and Jang (2000)** and **Widiger and Trull (2007)** on the case for
  dimensional models of personality pathology.
- **Skodol et al. (2011)** on impairment in personality functioning as the
  organising requirement rather than trait elevation.
- **Tyrer, Reed and Crawford (2015)** for the severity-based approach adopted in
  ICD-11, and for the argument that severity carries more information than type.
- **Zimmerman (2011)** and **Clark (2007)** for the objections — that
  dimensional systems can be harder to use clinically and to communicate.
- **Sheehan, Nieweglowski and Corrigan (2016)** on the stigma attached to these
  diagnoses, including among professionals.
- **Ring and Lawn (2019)** for service users' accounts of what the label
  changed about how they were treated.
- On cultural context in assessment, **Ryder and Chentsova-Dutton's** writing on
  cultural–clinical psychology is a good entry point, alongside the cultural
  formulation material in the current diagnostic manuals.

Full references are deliberately not embedded in the page, so the tool does not
appear to derive its invented numbers from any of them.
