Positive Manifold Visualiser

Six fictional tasks, a correlation matrix you control, and a first factor that appears whatever structure you put underneath it.

Simulated — fictional tasks, no real intelligence-test data

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to say why a first factor with positive loadings follows arithmetically from the positive manifold, and settles nothing about what produces it.

About 25 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

What a factor is here

A factor in this tool is a property of a correlation matrix — a summary of numbers you generated with sliders. It is not a biological entity, a brain structure or a measured quantity.

First, a judgement

Across a century of research, scores on almost any pair of cognitive tasks correlate positively. This is called the positive manifold, and factor analysing such a matrix always produces a first factor on which everything loads positively.

Key terms
Positive manifold
The finding that scores on diverse cognitive tasks tend to correlate positively with one another.
General factor
A factor on which every task loads positively.
Group factor
Variance shared by some tasks and not others — the three verbal-ish ones here, for instance.
Specific variance and measurement error
What belongs to one task alone, and what is noise. Neither of them contributes to any correlation.

What this demonstrates

The pattern is real; the explanation is a theory

That cognitive tasks correlate positively is one of the most replicated findings in psychology. It holds across samples, centuries, task types and cultures, and it is not an artefact of test construction — it appears even with tasks chosen to be as unlike each other as possible. What produces it is a different question, and the factor analysis does not answer it.

A first factor is arithmetic, not evidence

If every correlation in a matrix is positive, the first principal component will have positive loadings on everything. That follows from the numbers. It happens when there is one general ability, and it happens when there are two group factors that happen to correlate, and it happens when a dozen partly overlapping processes are sampled by every task. Extracting the factor tells you the manifold is there; it does not tell you why.

Rival explanations survive the same evidence

At least four accounts fit the positive manifold: a single general capacity; a small number of broad abilities that overlap; mutual reinforcement between abilities during development, so that being good at one thing helps you get better at another; and sampling, where every task draws on an overlapping subset of many narrow processes. All predict a positive manifold. Choosing between them needs evidence from outside the correlation matrix — developmental, experimental, genetic, intervention.

Group structure hides inside a general factor

The configuration to spend time on is "Broad group factors". Every task still loads positively on the first factor, and the truth underneath is two clusters. A researcher who extracted one factor and stopped would report a general ability and would be describing something that is not there.

What this does not demonstrate

  • The factor is not a thing. It is the leading eigenvector of a matrix you generated with three sliders. Nothing here shows that any corresponding entity exists in a person, a brain or a genome, and the tool takes no position on whether one does.
  • No real data. Six fictional tasks with illustrative names. No intelligence test, subtest, item, norm or published correlation matrix is reproduced.
  • Nobody is measured. The 220 simulated people are draws from a generator, and the tool produces no ability score for anyone, real or fictional.
  • Two group factors is a simplification. Real hierarchical models have several broad abilities beneath the general factor, and deciding how many is contested in its own right.
  • Principal components are not factor analysis. The tool extracts the first principal component because it can be computed transparently and shown working. Common-factor models partition variance differently and would give slightly different loadings.
  • Nothing here bears on group differences. The simulation contains one undifferentiated population and can support no claim about any comparison between groups of people.
