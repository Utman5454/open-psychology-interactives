Personality and Individual Differences

“Explain This Person” Courtroom

One fictional behaviour, eight explanations that all fit it, and a jury that has to work out which of them could ever have been wrong.

Fictional case — there is no correct answer, and the tool does not have one

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to tell an explanation that fits from one that predicts, and say what evidence would choose between two accounts that both fit.

About 25 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

How this is scored

Not on picking the right explanation — there isn't one. The verdict responds to whether you requested evidence that discriminates, whether your confidence is proportionate to what that evidence can support, and whether you kept compatible explanations alive instead of forcing a single cause. Converging confidently on one answer is scored down.

The case

Priya is fictional. Her behaviour was written to be compatible with every explanation offered below — which is not a trick, but the ordinary condition of explaining somebody from the outside.

The jury room

Rate, investigate, decide

Rate every explanation on how plausible you find it, then request the evidence you would want. Watch what the evidence does — and what it cannot do.

      What this demonstrates

      Fitting is cheap; predicting is not

      All eight explanations accommodate the behaviour, because all eight were written to. That is not a flaw in the case — it is what most behaviour is like. What separates them is not how comfortably they fit what you have already seen but how much they commit you to about what you have not: an explanation that predicts the same as every rival cannot be tested against any of them, however true it happens to be.

      Underdetermination is the normal situation

      The evidence available does not select one explanation, and no amount of thinking harder about it will. This is not a failure of reasoning; it is a property of the evidence. The useful response is to say precisely what would settle it — which is why the six-month follow-up is on the list and marked unavailable.

      Compatible is not the same as competing

      A great deal of argument about why people do things is conducted as though every explanation excluded every other. Most do not. A person can have a strong achievement motive, high self-efficacy in this domain, values that made the silence uncomfortable, and be responding to a real incentive — all at once, and all truly. The diagram joins only the pairs that genuinely pull in opposite directions, and there are fewer than students expect.

      Confidence should track the evidence, not the story

      A coherent narrative feels like knowledge. The test is whether your confidence would have been lower had the evidence come out differently — and for explanations that predict nothing distinctive, the answer is no, because no result could have disturbed them.

      What this does not demonstrate

      • There is no correct answer, and no hidden one. The tool does not know why Priya volunteered, because Priya does not exist and no fact of the matter was written down. Any student who feels cheated by that has located the point.
      • Distinctiveness is not truth. A bold, testable explanation can be wrong, and a vague one can be right. The claim is only that the bold one can be found out.
      • This is not a model of how explanation is scored in science. Real theory choice involves simplicity, scope, consistency with other findings and much else. Two dimensions are enough to make one point, and no more.
      • The evidence findings are stipulated. They were written to bear on some accounts and not others. Real evidence is messier, and often bears on nothing anybody predicted.
      • No real person is being explained. The case is fictional. Nothing here is a method for working out why someone you know did something, and applying it that way would be a misuse.
      • Nobody is assessed. You rate explanations of a fictional case. The tool asks nothing about you.
