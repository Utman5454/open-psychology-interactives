# Teaching notes - "Explain This Person" Courtroom

`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/24-explain-this-person-courtroom/`

**What it teaches.** Eight explanations were each deliberately written
to fit the same fictional case plausibly, and every one of them does;
the tool stores no fact about which one is "true." What separates them
is not how well they fit the case retrospectively but distinctiveness,
how much a prediction differs from its rivals and could in principle
have been shown wrong. The broad trait account and the role-experience account predict
almost nothing the others do not and so could barely ever have been
shown wrong; the situational-pressure, reputation and incentive
accounts commit to something distinctive about timing or reward that
the others deny.

**Before students start.** Read the case aloud. Resist answering "but
which one is it?"; there isn't one, and saying so early spoils the
discovery.

**What students do.**
1. Rate all eight explanations as likely or unlikely before requesting
   any evidence.
2. Read the distinctiveness column and compare it against their own
   ratings.
3. Request evidence one piece at a time and watch each explanation's
   standing update, or fail to.
4. Try to request the six-month follow-up, marked unavailable, and
   read why that is the evidence that would discriminate most.
5. Look at the competition diagram to see which pairs of explanations
   actually compete and which are compatible.

**What to look for.** Students usually rate the low-distinctiveness
accounts, especially the broad trait explanation, among the highest,
because comfortable fit reads as explanatory power. Requesting
evidence changes the standing of the accounts that made a testable
prediction about it and leaves the rest completely untouched, whatever
the finding says. Fewer pairs compete on the diagram than students
expect; several of the eight are compatible and could both be true at
once.

**Debrief.** The trait account has the lowest distinctiveness score.
Does that make it false, unhelpful, or neither? Which two
explanations, on the diagram, genuinely compete, and which pairs turn
out to be compatible?

**Common misconception / caution.** Underdetermination is not the
same as ignorance: some accounts genuinely were undermined once
evidence came in, and the exercise is not an argument that nothing can
ever be explained. Distinctiveness is not truth either; a bold
explanation can turn out wrong, and a vague one can turn out right,
the claim is only that the bold one could be found out.

**Use in class / timing.** A single case, from rating through to the
verdict, takes about 10 minutes. As a full seminar with argument
between groups, allow up to 45 minutes.
