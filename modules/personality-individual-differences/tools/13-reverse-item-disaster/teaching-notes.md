# Teaching notes - Reverse-Item Disaster

`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/13-reverse-item-disaster/`

**What it teaches.** The same six raw answers can be scored four
different ways, giving three distinct numerical results, not four:
scoring correctly recodes the two reverse items with the right
constant, using the wrong maximum recodes them with a different, wrong
constant, and the two remaining methods, noting the reverse wording
but never actually recoding, and simply forgetting to, land on
identical numbers whether that came from a careless slip or a genuine
misunderstanding. Some scoring errors are loud and some are silent,
and the silent one is the more dangerous.

**Before students start.** Familiarity with Cronbach's alpha and
item-total correlation is assumed; the six items and six fictional
respondents, A through F, are fixed throughout.

**What students do.**
1. Predict what happens to the ranking of six fictional respondents
   when reverse items are left unrecoded.
2. Compare all four scoring methods side by side: totals, ranking,
   item-total correlations and Cronbach's alpha.
3. Step through repairing one respondent's two reverse answers by
   hand, using the recoding formula.
4. Diagnose which error, if any, a set of item statistics reveals.

**What to look for.** Correct scoring gives an alpha of .78 and a
clean ranking. Leaving the reverse items unrecoded collapses alpha to
about -.04, with four of the six items showing negative item-total
correlations, and reorders all six respondents. Using the wrong
maximum instead of the right one adds exactly two points to every
respondent's total, changes nobody's rank, and leaves alpha completely
unchanged: this error leaves no trace in the statistics students are
taught to check.

**Debrief.** Two of the four scoring methods on offer produce numbers
nobody could tell apart by inspecting them. What does that imply about
trusting a scale's reported alpha as evidence that it was scored
correctly?

**Common misconception / caution.** A correctly reverse-scored item is
not thereby equivalent to a positively-worded item measuring the same
thing; reverse wording is harder to read, is answered less
consistently by people moving quickly, and tends to form its own
cluster in a factor analysis. Reverse-keying exists to counter the
tendency to agree with everything, and it earns that benefit at a real
cost rather than for free.

**Use in class / timing.** Comparing the four scoring methods takes
about 10 minutes. With the repair walkthrough and diagnosis task,
allow 25 minutes.
