# Teaching notes - State versus Trait Tracker

`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/04-state-versus-trait-tracker/`

**What it teaches.** A trait is a person's typical level across many
occasions, not the value read off any single one. Four fictional
people are sampled four times a day for a fortnight; each has a stable
mean and a characteristic amount of day-to-day wobble around it, and
one reading can land almost anywhere in that spread. What a single
administration can never establish, however carefully it is taken, is
how variable a person is, because variability is a property of a
series, not of one point in it.

**Before students start.** None needed.

**What students do.**
1. See one reading for each of four people and rank them, then reveal
   the fortnight each reading came from.
2. Move the within-person variability and measurement-error sliders
   and watch the four running means and their spread respond
   differently.
3. Use the stability chart to compare how quickly a calm and a
   volatile person's running mean settles near its true value.
4. Try the two challenges: what a single reading can and cannot
   divide, and what raising measurement error does and does not
   change.

**What to look for.** With the default fortnight, two of the four
people share almost the same mean, around 62, while their spreads sit
worlds apart, around 4.5 for one and 18.6 for the other; a single
reading from the more volatile of the two can land twenty points from
his own mean. Both running means eventually converge on their true
average, but at very different rates: the calmer person's settles
within the first couple of readings, the volatile person's keeps
drifting for several simulated weeks before it stabilises. Raising
measurement error adds noise to every reading without moving any of
the four means.

**Debrief.** Two people share a trait score. Does that mean they
behave alike from one day to the next? What would it take to find out
how variable someone actually is, and why can a single reading never
answer that?

**Common misconception / caution.** High day-to-day variability is not
itself a sign of a problem; it is a dimension people differ on, and
the most volatile person here is written no less favourably than the
calmest one. Variability and measurement error are also easy to run
together: the tool keeps them as separate sliders because raising one
changes what a single reading might mean, while raising the other
changes how much a person truly moves.

**Use in class / timing.** A quick pass through the ranking, sliders
and stability chart runs about 10 minutes. With both challenges,
allow 25 minutes.
