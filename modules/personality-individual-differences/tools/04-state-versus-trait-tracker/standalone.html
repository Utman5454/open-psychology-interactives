Learning objective

How to use this

  1. Rank four people by their typical level, using one moment each.
  2. See the full fortnight and find out how you did.
  3. Then take the controls: within-person variability, measurement error, life events, and the random seed.
  4. Watch the running average settle, and see how many observations each person needs.
  5. Answer the two challenges.

Key terms
State
Where somebody is at one moment.
Trait
A person's distribution across occasions — which takes more than its average to describe.
Within-person variability
How much one person moves about from occasion to occasion. It is a characteristic of that person rather than leftover noise.
Measurement error
Variation coming from the instrument rather than the person. In a single reading it is indistinguishable from real fluctuation.

What this demonstrates

A trait is a distribution, not a setting

The tempting picture of a trait is a dial fixed at some value, with everything else counting as interference. The picture that survives contact with experience-sampling data is a distribution: a person has a typical level and a characteristic amount of movement around it, and both are stable properties of them. Asking whether someone "is" anxious is asking about the centre of a distribution and ignoring its width.

Within-person variability is a characteristic, not noise

Ada and Bo share a mean. A trait questionnaire describes them identically, and they are not living the same fortnight. How much a person fluctuates is measurable, reasonably stable across months, and predicts things the mean does not — which makes treating it as error a substantive loss rather than a tidy simplification.

Error and fluctuation look the same once and differ across many

In a single observation, measurement error and genuine within-person movement are indistinguishable: both push the number away from the person's typical level. Across repeated observations they separate, because error averages towards zero and the person's own variability does not. This is the argument for repeated measurement, and it is why the running-average chart settles at different rates for different people.

How many observations is a question about the person

The stability curve reaches a usable estimate quickly for Ada and slowly for Bo. There is no general answer to "how many observations do I need" — it depends on how much the person varies and how precisely you need to know their level. A study design that samples everyone equally is implicitly measuring some people much better than others.

What this does not demonstrate

  • These are not data. Four invented people and a pseudo-random generator. The 0–100 scale is arbitrary, with no norms and no reference sample, and no number here comes from a study.
  • Real sampling is much messier. Observations here are equally spaced and never missing. Real experience sampling has non-response that correlates with what is being measured, which is a serious and unsolved problem.
  • No autocorrelation and no drift. Apart from event decay, consecutive observations are independent, and the typical level does not move over the fortnight. Both are wrong about real series, and inertia is itself an interesting individual difference.
  • Nothing here is about mental health. The variable is deliberately unnamed and the events are ordinary ones. High variability is not a symptom, and this tool does not assess or screen for anything.
  • The decomposition is given, not estimated. The tool knows the true trait level because it generated it. In real data you would have to estimate it, with uncertainty, usually with a multilevel model.
  • Nobody is assessed. You are reading simulated observations of fictional people. The tool asks nothing about you.
