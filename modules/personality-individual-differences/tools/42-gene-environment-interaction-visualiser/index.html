Gene × Environment Interaction Visualiser

Three theories, two straight lines, and one decision about who to recruit. Name the pattern before you see the rest of the range.

Simulated — fictional sensitivity scores, no gene and no clinical outcome

Learning objective

By the end you should be able to tell diathesis–stress from differential susceptibility by their patterns, and say how the sampled range decides which one you appear to see.

About 25 minutes. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

What is deliberately absent

No gene is named and no genotype appears — the grouping variable is a fictional sensitivity score. No disorder, diagnosis or clinical risk estimate is used; the outcome axis is an unnamed standardised outcome. A slope here is an average tendency across a group, never a prediction about a person, and no figure corresponds to any published finding.

What this demonstrates

Three theories, one geometry

Diathesis–stress, differential susceptibility and vantage sensitivity are the same two straight lines with the crossing point in three different places. That is worth knowing before reading any of the literature: the theoretical disagreement is about where the lines cross and whether anybody looked there.

Range restriction decides the answer

Sample only the adverse half of the environmental range and a genuine differential-susceptibility pattern is indistinguishable from pure vulnerability. No amount of statistical power fixes this, because the missing information is not in the data at all. A study that recruits only from high-risk settings cannot find an advantage in good conditions, and its failure to find one is not evidence of absence.

A significant interaction is the beginning, not the finding

All three theories predict that the slopes differ. Showing that they differ therefore does not choose between them. The claim that has to be tested is about the location of the crossover and about whether the sensitive group is genuinely better off at the supportive end — which requires having measured people in supportive conditions.

Sensitivity is not a defect

Under differential susceptibility the same responsiveness that produces worse outcomes in bad conditions produces better ones in good conditions. Calling it a risk factor describes half the curve. It also changes what follows practically: a group that responds more to the environment in both directions is the group with most to gain from improving it.

What this does not demonstrate

  • No gene is involved. The grouping variable is a fictional sensitivity score. Real candidate-gene interaction findings have a poor replication record, and nothing here should be read as supporting any specific genetic claim.
  • No clinical content. The outcome is unnamed and standardised. No disorder, diagnosis, symptom or risk estimate appears, and the tool cannot be used to assess or predict anything about anybody.
  • Straight lines are a simplification. Real interactions are often curved, and the crossover of two curves is harder to locate and easier to over-interpret than the crossover of two lines.
  • Two groups is a simplification. Sensitivity, if it exists as modelled, is continuous. Splitting a continuum into two groups loses information and can manufacture apparent interactions.
  • No sampling uncertainty is shown. The lines are drawn from parameters rather than fitted, so there are no confidence bands. Real interaction estimates are imprecise, and that imprecision is a large part of why this literature is contested.
  • Nobody is assessed. You manipulate parameters. The tool asks nothing about you.
