# A General Factor With No General Ability — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** Final year or postgraduate
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps one of the generating models from the
**Positive Manifold Visualiser**, the sampling account, and runs it live
against a fitted single factor. The side-by-side comparison with common-factor
and mutualism models and the hierarchical and bifactor solutions are in the
longer version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/32-positive-manifold-visualiser/`.
One model is enough for the point being made, which is that the manifold does
not choose between models.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. state what the positive manifold is;
2. explain why it does not establish that a general ability exists;
3. explain how overlapping sets of small processes produce positive
   correlations;
4. distinguish a factor that fits from a factor that corresponds to something;
5. say what evidence would be needed to choose between accounts;
6. recognise that this argument does not establish the sampling account either.

## Preparation

Students should have met factor analysis and the idea of a general factor.
The paired activity **Turn the Axes, Change Every Number** is a good precursor.

## Suggested use

**In a lecture on intelligence, immediately after g is introduced**, and
specifically after the moment when the manifold is offered as the evidence for
it.

**In a seminar on inference from models**, where it works as a clean case of two
theories fitting the same data.

**Projected.** The slider to zero is worth doing first: no overlap, no
manifold, nothing removed from the model to achieve it.

## Prediction question

> Every cognitive test correlates positively with every other, and one factor
> accounts for a large share of the variance. What does that establish?

Not much on its own. Note that "that g exists but not what it is" is marked
partial rather than wrong: the factor certainly exists in the sense that the
arithmetic returns one, and what does not follow is that anything in a person
corresponds to it.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Read the model description before touching anything.** Fifteen hundred
   independent processes. No general ability. Say it twice.
3. **Take the slider to zero.** Every correlation zero. No manifold.
4. **Raise it to 40 per cent.** Every one of the fifteen correlations is
   positive and the average is around 0.40.
5. **Press "Fit a single general factor".** Loadings around 0.65, forty per
   cent of the variance, largest residual about 0.015.
6. **Ask what a journal would say about that fit.** It would call it excellent.
7. **Move the slider again with the factor fitted.** The fit stays good
   everywhere.

## Debrief questions

- What is in this model, and what is not?
- Where do the positive correlations come from?
- The single factor fits to within about 0.015. What does that tell you?
- If both accounts fit these correlations, what would tell them apart?
- Does this show that there is no general ability?
- General-factor scores predict a great many outcomes. Is that affected by
  anything here?

## Likely misconceptions

- **This disproves g.** It does not, and the caution says so plainly. It shows
  the correlations cannot establish it. Running the argument in one direction
  only is the obvious way to misuse this page.
- **This establishes the sampling account.** Equally not. Both accounts fit.
- **A good fit means a true model.** A small residual means the model
  reproduces the data. Two incompatible models can both do that.
- **The tests must be similar to each other.** The manifold holds between tests
  with little in common on the surface, and this model produces it from random
  overlap rather than shared content.
- **If g is only a description, g scores should not predict anything.** A
  description can predict perfectly well without naming a cause, and the
  predictive record is untouched by this argument.

## Limitations and cautions

The model is a toy. Real cognitive processes are not fifteen hundred
independent things, real tests do not sample them at random, and real
correlation matrices are not this tidy. The tidiness is what makes the
demonstration legible and it is not a claim about how minds are built. Nothing
here is about any person or any group; it is a statement about what a
correlation matrix can be used to infer.

## Accessibility considerations

Overlap is a native range input reporting its value in text. The correlation
matrix and the fitted loadings are real tables with row and column headers, so
every number is reachable without a figure, and the caption states in words
whether all the correlations are positive and what the smallest is. A live
sentence restates the current setting and its consequence.

## Optional extension

Ask students what observation would distinguish the two accounts, given that
both fit the correlations. Answers that come up are developmental (does the
manifold strengthen or weaken with age), neural, genetic, and experimental
(does training one thing transfer). All of them go outside the correlation
matrix, which is the transferable lesson.

## Evidence and citation notes

The sampling account is Thomson (1916), *A hierarchy without a general factor*,
**British Journal of Psychology**, 8(3), 271–281, developed in Thomson (1951),
**The Factorial Analysis of Human Ability**, University of London Press. For the
original claim it was answering see Spearman (1904), *General intelligence,
objectively determined and measured*, **American Journal of Psychology**, 15(2),
201–293. On mutualism as a third account producing the same manifold see van der
Maas et al. (2006), *A dynamical model of general intelligence*, **Psychological
Review**, 113(4), 842–861. On the general problem of inferring a latent entity
from a fitting model see Bartholomew, Deary and Lawn (2009), *A new lease of
life for Thomson's bonds model of intelligence*, **Psychological Review**,
116(3), 567–579.
