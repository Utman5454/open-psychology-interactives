# It Correlates. So What? — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** Second or final year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the correlation model and the live
regression from the **Emotional-Intelligence Claims Laboratory**, cut to four
measures and one control. The ability-based measure, the shared-method
controls, the six-measure matrix and the marketing claims to judge are in the
longer version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/49-emotional-intelligence-claims-laboratory/`.

## A word before you teach it

All four measures are fictional descriptions of method types. No published
emotional-intelligence measure is reproduced, quoted, paraphrased or scored,
and the page takes no position on whether emotional intelligence exists. The
argument is about what a correlation licenses somebody to advertise, and it
would run identically for any construct at all.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. explain why a correlation is a weaker claim than adding something new;
2. define incremental validity and say what it is measured against;
3. explain why an overlapping measure still correlates well with the outcome;
4. recognise that modest overlap is not itself an objection;
5. say what a decision-maker needs before adopting a measure.

## Preparation

Students should have met multiple regression, or at least the idea that
predictors can share information.

## Suggested use

**Whenever a new measure or construct is introduced**, in any area, as the
question to ask about it.

**In a research methods seminar on validity**, after criterion validity and
before students design a study of their own.

**Projected, with the vote taken first.** "That it predicts the outcome" is
marked partial rather than wrong, and the discussion about why is the useful
part.

## Prediction question

> A new measure correlates 0.45 with an outcome that matters, in a large
> sample, and it replicates. What does that establish?

Nothing until you know what else predicts the outcome. The finding is real; the
question is whether the information is new.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Read the table before touching the slider.** All three measures correlate
   with the outcome. Ask which of them a press release would be about.
3. **Note the base figure.** Personality and reasoning together explain 23.1
   per cent. That is what a new measure has to beat.
4. **Slide slowly from 0.38 to 0.76.** Ask the room to watch the first tile.
   It goes 0.46, 0.48, 0.47, 0.36. Now ask them to watch the third. It goes
   10.1, 9.5, 6.2, 0.3.
5. **Press the guided button and read the note.**
6. **Ask which sentence both versions of the measure would appear in.** The
   same one.

## Debrief questions

- Which of the two numbers would appear in a press release, and why?
- At an overlap of 0.5 the measure still adds 9.5 points. Is overlapping bad?
- What would you have to know before deciding whether to adopt this measure?
- The comparison here is a personality questionnaire and a reasoning test.
  What if the comparison had been nothing at all?
- Can a measure be worth having even if it adds no prediction?

## Likely misconceptions

- **A replicated correlation settles it.** It establishes a relationship. It
  says nothing about whether the information is new.
- **Overlap with an existing measure is fatal.** At 0.5 overlap this measure
  still adds 9.5 percentage points. The page says so deliberately.
- **Incremental validity is a property of the measure.** It is relative to
  whatever you compare against, and choosing that comparison is substantive.
- **Prediction is the only thing that matters.** A measure can earn its place
  by being quicker, cheaper or more acceptable.
- **This is about emotional intelligence.** It is about what a correlation
  licenses, and would work identically for any construct.

## Limitations and cautions

Every figure is a population value implied by the model, with no sampling
error. That matters more here than in most of these activities: incremental
validity is estimated poorly at the sample sizes most studies use, and real
confidence intervals on a figure like the third tile are wide. The comparison
set is chosen for the learner, and the clean separation between the two
comparison measures is a property of the construction.

## Accessibility considerations

Overlap is a native range input reporting its value in text. Every figure in
the chart is also a readout tile, and the three correlations are in a real
table with headers, so the lines are never the only route to the numbers. Each
line is labelled by name at its end, and the tile reporting what the measure
adds states its meaning in words and carries a non-colour state signal.

## Optional extension

Ask students to find the setting at which they would still recommend adopting
the measure, and to defend the threshold. There is no correct answer, and
having to state one out loud is the point: the decision is about what a
decision-maker would do differently, not about a coefficient.

## Evidence and citation notes

On incremental validity as the standard a new measure has to meet see Hunsley
and Meyer (2003), *The incremental validity of psychological testing and
assessment*, **Psychological Assessment**, 15(4), 446–455. On the specific case
of emotional intelligence over personality and cognitive ability see Joseph and
Newman (2010), *Emotional intelligence: An integrative meta-analysis and
cascading model*, **Journal of Applied Psychology**, 95(1), 54–78, and
O'Boyle, Humphrey, Pollack, Hawver and Story (2011), *The relation between
emotional intelligence and job performance*, **Journal of Organizational
Behavior**, 32(5), 788–818. On jingle and jangle in construct naming see Kelley
(1927), **Interpretation of Educational Measurements**, World Book Company.
