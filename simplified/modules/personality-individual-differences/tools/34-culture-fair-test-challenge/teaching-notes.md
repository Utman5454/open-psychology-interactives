# Take the Words Out and See What Is Left — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 6–8 minutes
**Level:** Second or final year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the design decisions, the live demand
recomputation and the fictional participants from the **Culture-Fair Test
Challenge**. Two of the six decisions, a fourth participant, the explicit
construct-relevant share and the closing exercise on comparability evidence are
in the longer version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/34-culture-fair-test-challenge/`.

## A word before you teach it

What is modelled is opportunity to learn a test format. The three fictional
people are described only by their prior experience of testing formats, devices
and puzzle conventions. None has a nationality, ethnicity, region, language or
group membership, no score is produced for anyone, and no group difference is
simulated. If the discussion starts attaching format unfamiliarity to real
populations, that is the moment to say that unfamiliarity with a format is a
fact about exposure and not a property of a people.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. distinguish construct-relevant from construct-irrelevant demands;
2. explain why removing language does not make a test culture-free;
3. recognise an abstract figure format as itself something met or not met;
4. identify the demands no design decision removes;
5. distinguish reducing a demand from establishing comparability;
6. treat format unfamiliarity as being about exposure.

## Preparation

None.

## Suggested use

**Whenever a non-verbal or culture-fair test is mentioned**, which in most
syllabuses is during the intelligence lectures.

**In a seminar on test bias**, before the statistical machinery, so that the
question the machinery answers is already clear.

**Projected, with the vote taken first.** "It is now culture-free" is chosen
often enough to be worth naming as a phrase to retire.

## Prediction question

> A designer removes every word from a reasoning test. What has that achieved?

It has removed the reading demand, and only that. Note that "nothing useful" is
marked partial rather than wrong: reading is a real demand and removing it is a
real improvement, and the error is treating that as finishing the job.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Look at the opening design.** Written instructions, verbal items, a strict
   limit, paper. Reading is at 1.00 and five of six demands are substantial.
3. **Press "Now take every word out of it".** Watch the top bar collapse.
4. **Then read the note.** Having met abstract puzzles went from 0.40 to 0.85
   and knowing when to guess went from 0.65 to 0.85.
5. **Try to get all six below the line.** It cannot be done. Four can be
   brought down; two stop at 0.30 whatever you choose.
6. **Ask what those two are.** Knowing how a formal test works, and knowing
   when to guess. Both are learned by having sat tests before.
7. **Change the response medium and watch the participant table.** Who carries
   the demand moves with the design.

## Debrief questions

- What did removing the words achieve, exactly?
- Why did two demands go up when the words came out?
- Which two demands can you not design away, and what do they have in common?
- The best design still leaves two demands at 0.30. Is testing therefore
  hopeless?
- What would you need, beyond a good design, before comparing scores across
  groups?
- The three people carry different amounts of the same design's demand. What is
  that a fact about?

## Likely misconceptions

- **A non-verbal test is culture-free.** There is no such thing, and the phrase
  is worth retiring.
- **Removing language removes bias.** Bias is a claim about whether scores mean
  the same thing across groups and is established with evidence. Design
  decisions and comparability evidence are two different jobs.
- **Abstract figures are neutral.** An abstract puzzle is a format, and this
  model puts its demand higher than the verbal items it replaced.
- **If no design is perfect, design does not matter.** The worst design here is
  far worse than the best, and the difference is large.
- **The participant loads are predicted scores.** They are not scores, not
  predictions, and not estimates of anybody's reasoning.

## Limitations and cautions

The demand levels are invented to make the trade-offs legible and are not
measurements, and the six demands are not a complete list. The impossibility
result is a property of these numbers; what generalises is the reason for it,
which is that some demands are about knowing how to sit a test and no item
format removes them. Nothing here models any real group.

## Accessibility considerations

Design decisions are native radio buttons in labelled fieldsets, and each
option's description states what it adds to which demands. The chart labels a
substantial demand in words as well as by colour, and its description lists all
six values. The participant table is a real table with headers and names in
words where most of each person's demand comes from.

## Optional extension

Ask students to design the fairest task they can, then to say what evidence they
would still need before comparing two groups' scores on it. The answer, that
design does not substitute for a test of measurement invariance, connects
directly to the paired activity **The Same Difference, Twice Over**.

## Evidence and citation notes

On construct-irrelevant variance see Messick (1989), *Validity*, in Linn's
**Educational Measurement**, 3rd edition. On why culture-fair is a direction
rather than an achievement see Helms (1992), *Why is there no study of cultural
equivalence in standardized cognitive ability testing?*, **American
Psychologist**, 47(9), 1083–1101, and Cole (1999), *Culture-free versus
culture-fair*, in Sternberg's **Handbook of Intelligence**. On test-taking
familiarity as a learned skill see Kulik, Bangert-Drowns and Kulik (1984),
*Effectiveness of coaching for aptitude tests*, **Psychological Bulletin**,
95(2), 179–188.
