# No Best Battery — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 6–8 minutes
**Level:** Second or final year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the selection under a time budget and the
five properties recomputing together from the **Intelligence-Test Battery
Builder**. The purpose is a control the learner switches rather than a scenario
they are assigned, because switching it without touching the battery is the
demonstration. Further task families, a scenario-dependent burden tolerance, a
graded fit score and the treatment of norms and ceilings are in the longer
version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/31-intelligence-test-battery-builder/`.

## A word before you teach it

The page scores batteries and never people. It produces no ability score for
anybody, reproduces no published test, item, norm or scoring rule, and cannot
be used to assess or screen anyone. If the discussion drifts towards what a
score would mean about a person, the honest answer is that nothing on the page
supports any such claim, by construction.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. explain why there is no best battery in the abstract;
2. say that validity attaches to a use rather than to a task;
3. name the trade-offs a fixed time budget forces;
4. explain why measuring one area several ways is not breadth;
5. treat dependence on prior opportunity as a property of a task;
6. say why reliability is a precondition rather than an achievement.

## Preparation

None.

## Suggested use

**When cognitive assessment is introduced**, before any specific instrument is
described, so that "is this a good test?" is already known to be the wrong
question.

**In a seminar on validity**, as the concrete version of the claim that validity
belongs to an interpretation and a use.

**Projected.** The moment worth staging is pressing the guided button, then
changing only the purpose while the class watches the top row of tiles, which
does not move.

## Prediction question

> What makes one test battery better than another?

It depends what it is for. Note that "free of cultural content" is marked as a
partial answer rather than an error: it is a real consideration whose weight is
itself set by the purpose, which is the answer the activity is after.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Press "Build the best battery for research".** Four tasks, 48 minutes, all
   four areas reached, composite reliability 0.96, three requirements of three.
3. **Change only the purpose to the brief screen.** One of three. Read the
   checklist: 48 minutes against a limit of 20, burden 10 against a limit of 8.
4. **Point at the four tiles.** They have not moved. They are the same battery.
5. **Try to satisfy all three purposes at once.** It cannot be done, and that is
   not an accident of the task list; it was checked across every possible
   selection inside the budget.

## Debrief questions

- Which numbers on the page changed when you changed the purpose?
- Composite reliability was 0.96. Why did that not make it a good screening
  battery?
- Two tasks measuring verbal ability take 27 minutes and reach two areas out of
  four. What has the time bought?
- What does "depends on prior opportunity 0.85" tell you about a task, and what
  does it not tell you about a person who scores low on it?
- The page does not model norms at all. What would that add?

## Likely misconceptions

- **A better battery is a more reliable one.** Reliability is a precondition. A
  perfectly consistent battery can measure the wrong things, or take so long
  that half the people needing a screen are never seen.
- **Longer is more informative.** Time is the binding constraint, not a
  resource to maximise, and burden is a real cost to a real person.
- **Breadth is length.** Four tasks measuring one area reach one area.
- **A culture-fair test is the goal.** Dependence on prior opportunity matters
  a great deal for some purposes and almost not at all for others.
- **A high loading on prior opportunity says something about the people who
  score lower.** It says something about the task's demands. Reading it the
  other way is the inference the page is built to block.
- **Somewhere there is a battery that does everything.** Within this budget
  there is not, and that was checked exhaustively.

## Limitations and cautions

Generic task families with invented numbers, so the specific trade-offs are not
those any real battery faces; the shape of the argument is what transfers.
Norms are not modelled, although a score means nothing without the reference
sample it is expressed against and choosing that sample is substantive. Fit is
a three-way checklist rather than a graded judgement, and burden tolerance does
not vary between scenarios as it would in practice. None of the properties
here, alone or together, licenses a claim about anybody's ability, and a
battery can be well built for its purpose and still be the wrong thing to have
done to somebody.

## Accessibility considerations

Tasks and purposes are native checkboxes and radio buttons in labelled
fieldsets. A task that will not fit the remaining time is dimmed and described
as not fitting rather than disabled, so it stays in the tab order and an
attempt to add it is refused with a spoken reason. Each requirement is a
checklist item whose own text says whether it is met and what the battery's
value is. The coverage chart labels an unreached area in words as well as by
colour, and its description gives every value.

## Optional extension

Ask students to write a fourth purpose and its three requirements, then to find
a battery that meets them. Writing requirements that are neither trivially met
nor impossible is harder than it looks, and it is the same difficulty as
specifying what an assessment is for.

## Evidence and citation notes

On validity as a property of an interpretation and use rather than of a test
see Messick (1995), *Validity of psychological assessment*, **American
Psychologist**, 50(9), 741–749, and the **Standards for Educational and
Psychological Testing** (AERA, APA and NCME, 2014). On breadth against depth in
selecting measures see Cronbach and Gleser (1965), **Psychological Tests and
Personnel Decisions**, 2nd edition, University of Illinois Press. On why "culture
fair" is a design goal rather than an achieved property see the paired
Simplified Edition activity **Culture-Fair Test Challenge**, and Helms (1992),
*Why is there no study of cultural equivalence in standardized cognitive ability
testing?*, **American Psychologist**, 47(9), 1083–1101.
