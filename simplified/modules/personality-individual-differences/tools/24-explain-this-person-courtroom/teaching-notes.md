# Five Explanations, All of Them Fit — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** First or second year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the case, the competing accounts, the
evidence requests and the live recomputation from **Explain This Person: The
Courtroom**, cut to five explanations and four pieces of evidence. The rating
stage, the confidence scoring, the compatibility map and three further
explanations are in the longer version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/24-explain-this-person-courtroom/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. distinguish an explanation that fits from one that predicts;
2. explain why accommodating every detail is a weakness;
3. say what evidence would choose between two accounts that both fit;
4. distinguish surviving the evidence from being supported by it;
5. treat underdetermination as ordinary rather than as failure;
6. recognise that compatible accounts do not compete.

## Preparation

None.

## Suggested use

**Early in a personality module**, before any theory is presented as an
explanation of anything, so that students have a criterion to apply to all of
them.

**In a seminar on attribution**, where the dispositional-versus-situational
argument usually proceeds as though one side has to win.

**Projected, with the vote taken first.** "It accounts for more of the details"
is the popular answer and the one worth arguing about.

## Prediction question

> Five explanations all account for what Priya did. What would make one better
> than another?

That it predicts something the others do not. Simplicity is marked as a partial
answer, because it is a genuine criterion that settles a different question.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Enter the jury room and read the table before asking anything.** All five
   fit. Nothing has been tested.
3. **Point out the fourth evidence box before requesting anything.** It is
   greyed as a caution and it is the one that would decide. It is visible from
   the start on purpose.
4. **Request the three pieces one at a time.** Ask what fell and why.
5. **Read the third column.** Achievement 3 of 3. Self-efficacy 2 of 3. Trait
   1 of 3. All three still standing.
6. **Ask the question the activity is for.** Are those three in the same
   position?

## Debrief questions

- Two accounts were ruled out. What did they do that the others did not?
- The trait account survived. Was it supported?
- What would you have to observe to separate the three survivors?
- Is it a problem that three accounts are still standing?
- Which of the five could be true at the same time as which others?
- Priya's behaviour was written to fit all five. How would you know, in a real
  case, whether you were in that situation?

## Likely misconceptions

- **The explanation that fits best is the best explanation.** Fitting is cheap.
  All five fit, by construction.
- **A surviving account has been supported.** Only if it risked something. The
  trait account survived having committed itself once out of three.
- **Underdetermination means the exercise failed.** Ending with several live
  accounts and a clear statement of what would decide between them is the
  correct outcome.
- **One of these has to be right.** Several are compatible. Ruling one in does
  not rule another out, and forcing a single cause is the commonest error here.
- **Situational explanations are weaker.** Neither kind is privileged. The
  situational accounts here fell because they committed themselves and were
  wrong, which is a mark of a good explanation, not a bad one.

## Limitations and cautions

Priya is fictional and her behaviour was written to fit every account. Each
account's predictions were supplied rather than derived, and in real life
working out what an account commits itself to is most of the difficulty:
theories are often stated loosely enough to commit themselves to nothing at
all, which is the very failing the activity is about. Evidence here points
cleanly one way, where real evidence is mixed and its direction is itself
arguable.

## Accessibility considerations

Evidence requests are ordinary buttons, and a requested one carries
`aria-disabled` rather than the `disabled` attribute so it stays reachable and
readable. Every account's standing is in a real table with headers and stated in
words, with the reason for a fall spelled out rather than signalled by colour.
Findings appear as text notices, so nothing depends on a figure.

## Optional extension

Ask students to write a sixth explanation that would survive all three pieces of
evidence, and then to say what would refute it. Most first attempts survive by
being unfalsifiable, and noticing that in one's own writing is the transferable
part.

## Evidence and citation notes

On the difference between accommodating and predicting see Meehl (1978),
*Theoretical risks and tabular asterisks*, **Journal of Consulting and Clinical
Psychology**, 46(4), 806–834. On explanations that fit after the fact see
Fischhoff (1975), *Hindsight is not equal to foresight*, **Journal of
Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance**, 1(3), 288–299. On
the dispositional bias in explaining others see Ross (1977), *The intuitive
psychologist and his shortcomings*, in **Advances in Experimental Social
Psychology**, volume 10, and, for the correction that situational accounts are
not automatically preferable either, Malle (2006), *The actor-observer
asymmetry in attribution*, **Psychological Bulletin**, 132(6), 895–919.
