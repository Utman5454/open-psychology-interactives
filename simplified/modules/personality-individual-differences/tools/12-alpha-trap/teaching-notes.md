# Chasing Alpha — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** Second year, or first year alongside an introduction to reliability
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the live alpha calculation and the three
numbers that move together from **The Alpha Trap**. The item pool, the choice
of which item to paraphrase, the correlation matrix and both challenges are in
the longer version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/12-alpha-trap/`.

One structural change from the original is worth knowing before you teach it.
There, items are added to the scale. Here the scale is a fixed ten items, so
every near-duplicate displaces a question about something else. That is how
scale construction works when a questionnaire has a length budget, and it is
what makes the cost of alpha-chasing visible rather than merely arguable.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say what alpha is a function of;
2. explain why near-duplicates raise it efficiently;
3. explain why that costs content coverage at fixed length;
4. explain why a high alpha is not evidence of a good measure;
5. distinguish internal consistency, unidimensionality and validity;
6. say what evidence would separate a good scale from a repetitive one.

## Preparation

Students should have met Cronbach's alpha and the 0.70 convention.

## Suggested use

**Immediately after alpha is introduced**, and specifically after the moment
when somebody says a scale with alpha of 0.92 must be a good one.

**In a research methods seminar on scale construction**, before students write
their own items. The lesson lands hardest just before it becomes relevant.

**As a five-minute demonstration in a lecture**, projected, with the vote first.

## Prediction question

> You push alpha from 0.82 to above 0.94 by swapping items for near-duplicates.
> What happens to the correlation with a tutor's rating of dependability?

It falls, from 0.62 to 0.46. Note that "alpha cannot tell you" is marked as a
defensible principle rather than an error: it is the right general instinct,
and in this fully specified model we can go further and compute the answer.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Open the scale at zero duplicates.** Alpha 0.82, validity 0.62, all five
   content areas covered.
3. **Move to four duplicates.** Alpha has risen, validity has already fallen,
   and coverage is still five of five. Ask what that means: the damage starts
   before any area is lost.
4. **Press "Chase alpha as far as it goes".** Alpha 0.94, coverage one of five,
   validity 0.46, and about four tenths of the total score is variance about
   how a question is worded.
5. **Read the four statements in the note.** All four describe the same
   questionnaire. Ask which one appears in a methods section.

## Debrief questions

- What two things is alpha a function of?
- Why do near-duplicate items raise it so efficiently?
- At four duplicates the scale still covers all five areas and validity has
  already dropped. Why?
- A colleague reports alpha of 0.94 and says the scale is unidimensional. What
  is wrong with that inference?
- What evidence would tell you whether a high-alpha scale is good or repetitive?
- Should we prefer scales with lower alpha?

## Likely misconceptions

- **High alpha means a good measure.** It means the items covary. Nine
  rephrasings of one question covary beautifully.
- **High alpha means one dimension.** Alpha is not a test of dimensionality. A
  set of items measuring two distinct things can return a respectable alpha.
- **Low alpha is therefore good.** No. The argument is against treating alpha
  as a target, not against alpha. A scale whose items do not covary has its own
  problems.
- **The duplicates are bad items.** Each is a perfectly reasonable item on its
  own, and the model gives them almost the same general loading as the
  originals. What is wrong with them is that there are nine of them.
- **The validity coefficient settles it.** One criterion is thin evidence. It
  is here to show that it moves independently of alpha, not to be the last
  word.

## Limitations and cautions

Every number is a population value implied by a specified model, not an
estimate, so nothing carries the sampling error a real alpha or validity
coefficient would. Items are treated as continuous and standardised, so no
distinction is drawn between alpha, standardised alpha and ordinal alpha, and
there is a single wording family rather than the several a real item pool would
contain. The size of the divergence is a property of these loadings, chosen to
make the trap reliable in a classroom. What generalises is the direction and
the reason for it, not the magnitude.

## Accessibility considerations

Scale composition is a native range input reporting its value in text. Every
number in the figure is also a readout tile and appears in the chart
description, so the lines are never the only route to the data, and each line
is labelled by name at its end. A live sentence states what the ten items now
ask about and how much of the score is wording variance. The vertical axis is
explicitly labelled as starting at 0.3 rather than zero.

## Optional extension

Ask students to write two items for a construct of their choice, then to write
a third that would raise alpha without adding anything. Most produce a
paraphrase within seconds, which is the point: the trap is easy to fall into
because the move is easy to make.

## Evidence and citation notes

On what alpha is and is not, see Sijtsma (2009), *On the use, the misuse, and
the very limited usefulness of Cronbach's alpha*, **Psychometrika**, 74(1),
107–120, and Cronbach (1951), *Coefficient alpha and the internal structure of
tests*, **Psychometrika**, 16(3), 297–334. On the attenuation paradox, where
raising internal consistency past a point reduces validity, see Loevinger
(1954), *The attenuation paradox in test theory*, **Psychological Bulletin**,
51(5), 493–504. On bloated specific factors from near-duplicate items see
Cattell (1978), **The Scientific Use of Factor Analysis in Behavioral and Life
Sciences**, Plenum. For practical recommendations see McNeish (2018), *Thanks
coefficient alpha, we'll take it from here*, **Psychological Methods**, 23(3),
412–433.
