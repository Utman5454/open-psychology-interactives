# Turn the Axes, Change Every Number — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** Second year, or first year alongside an introduction to factor
analysis
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the live rotation, the recomputing loading
table and the invariants from **Factor Rotation Playground**. The second marker
set, the oblique control and the closing challenge are in the longer version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/07-factor-rotation-playground/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say what rotation changes and what it leaves untouched;
2. explain why no rotation is more correct than another in any sense the data
   can settle;
3. distinguish the variance carried by one factor from the total variance
   accounted for;
4. defend simple structure as a chosen criterion rather than a discovered fact;
5. explain why a factor label is an interpretive act, and why a genuine
   cross-loading cannot be rotated away.

## Preparation

Students need to have met the idea of a factor loading. They do not need to
have run a factor analysis.

## Suggested use

**Immediately after showing a rotated solution in output from real software.**
The usual question is "why is the computer allowed to do that?", and this
answers it in one movement of a slider.

**Projected, with the vote taken first.** The prediction is the part that
matters: most of a room will say rotation improves fit.

**Before teaching about naming factors.** The label sentence under the tiles
changes as the dial turns, which makes the point faster than saying it.

## Prediction question

> A researcher extracts two factors, then rotates them. What does that do to
> how well the model fits the data?

The answer is nothing at all, and it is the least popular option. The common
answer, "it improves the fit, that is why people do it", has cause and effect
the wrong way round.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote** before opening the playground.
2. **At 0 degrees, read the table aloud.** Factor 1 has five positive loadings
   and five negative ones: it contrasts one group of markers against the other.
   Ask what a researcher would call it.
3. **Turn the dial slowly to about 47.** Ask students to watch one row of the
   table, then to watch the communality column, then the first tile.
4. **Press "Find the simplest structure".** Note that the variance carried by
   each factor has moved and the total has not.
5. **Look at markers 6 and 12.** Marker 6, enthusiastic, loads on both at every
   angle. Marker 12, unusual, loads on neither at any angle. Neither is a
   failure of rotation; they are facts about where those points sit.

## Debrief questions

- Which numbers on the page changed and which did not?
- If the fit is the same at every angle, what makes 47 degrees better?
- The first tile never moves. What does that quantity mean?
- Factor 1 carried 2.62 before and 2.90 after. Is that an improvement?
- Marker 6 loads about 0.5 on both factors. What would you do about it?
- The labels under the chart change as you rotate. What does that tell you
  about factor names in published papers?

## Likely misconceptions

- **Rotation improves the model.** It changes the description. The fit is
  identical to the last decimal.
- **The rotated solution is the true one.** Simple structure is a criterion we
  impose because a solution where each marker belongs to one factor is easier
  to talk about. That is a good reason and it is not evidence.
- **Rotation makes factor analysis arbitrary.** The configuration of points is
  fully determined by the data. What is not determined is which pair of axes
  you read it against, and the honest response is to say which you chose and
  why.
- **A factor with more variance is more important.** Rotation moves variance
  between factors without touching the data, so that ranking is not stable.
- **A cross-loading means a bad item.** Marker 6 sits genuinely between the two
  clusters. No rotation moves it, because the point has not moved.
- **A factor is a thing inside people.** It is a summary of how a particular
  set of items covaried in a particular sample.

## Limitations and cautions

Twelve invented markers with coordinates chosen to make rotation legible. Three
limits are stated on the page. The axes are held at ninety degrees, which
forces the factors to be uncorrelated; oblique rotation relaxes that and also
leaves the fit unchanged. How many factors to extract is decided before any
rotation and matters far more than the rotation does. And nothing here bears on
whether two factors is right for these markers, or whether the same two would
appear in another sample, another language or with different adjectives.

## Accessibility considerations

Rotation is a native range input reporting its angle in text. Every loading is
in a real table with row and column headers, so the figure is never the only
route to the numbers, and the table's last column states in words which factor
each marker reads as. Markers are numbered in the figure and by the same number
in the table, so no point has to be identified by colour. The chart description
gives the angle, the highest-loading marker on each factor, the variance on
each factor and the invariant total.

## Optional extension

Ask students to find an angle at which factor 2 carries more variance than
factor 1, and then to say whether they have made factor 2 more important. The
answer, that they have changed nothing except where a boundary was drawn, is
the whole lesson in one move.

## Evidence and citation notes

On simple structure as a criterion see Thurstone (1947), **Multiple Factor
Analysis**, University of Chicago Press. On rotational indeterminacy and what
it does and does not imply see Fabrigar, Wegener, MacCallum and Strahan (1999),
*Evaluating the use of exploratory factor analysis in psychological research*,
**Psychological Methods**, 4(3), 272–299. On the gap between a factor and a
psychological entity, and on the jingle-jangle problem in naming, see Borsboom
(2006), *The attack of the psychometricians*, **Psychometrika**, 71(3),
425–440.
