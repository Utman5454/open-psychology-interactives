# One Moment, or the Whole Fortnight? — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** First year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the seeded experience-sampling series and
the running-average convergence from **State versus Trait Tracker**, cut to two
people. The four-way ranking, the variability, measurement-error and
life-event controls, the re-seedable dataset and both challenges are in the
longer version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/04-state-versus-trait-tracker/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. distinguish a state from a trait and say which a single observation gives;
2. explain why one observation is a poor estimate of a typical level, and worse
   for some people than for others;
3. explain why averaging occasions shrinks a spurious difference in level;
4. explain why averaging does not shrink within-person variability, and why
   that variability is a characteristic rather than noise;
5. say what a single-number questionnaire throws away.

## Preparation

None. The dataset is fixed, so the numbers below are the numbers on screen.

## Suggested use

**Projected, with the slider driven from the front.** The reveal works best if
nobody has seen the fortnight before the vote.

**Before teaching aggregation or reliability.** Everything about why more
occasions give a better estimate is visible here without the word appearing.

**Before teaching experience sampling or ambulatory assessment**, as the reason
those methods exist.

## Prediction question

Ada reads 67 and Bo reads 47 at the same moment.

> Which of them has the higher typical level?

Almost everyone picks Ada, because twenty points is a large difference. The
answer is that one reading each cannot tell you: Ada and Bo have the same
typical level, and what you are looking at is one ordinary morning.

## Activity sequence

1. **Vote on the single moment.** Take the vote before revealing anything.
2. **Reveal the fortnight.** Do not move the slider yet. Ask what is different
   about the two rows. Students see it immediately: Ada's dots are a line, Bo's
   are a cloud.
3. **Slide to about 10.** The gap is now 6.8 points, and it has changed sign:
   Bo is above Ada. Ask the room what they would have concluded had the study
   stopped there.
4. **Press "Average the whole fortnight".** 61.9 against 62.0.
5. **Ask what did not change.** The spread line under the tiles: Ada 4.2, Bo
   17.4, at every setting from about five moments upward.

## Debrief questions

- What did the single reading actually measure, accurately?
- At ten moments you would have said Bo was higher. What was wrong with that?
- Which of the two would need more occasions before you trusted their average,
  and why?
- Ada and Bo end with the same number on their record. What does that number
  fail to say about them?
- Is Bo's variability a fault in the measurement or a fact about Bo?

## Likely misconceptions

- **The first reading was an error.** It was accurate. Ada really was twenty
  points above Bo at 09:20. It was accurate about the wrong thing.
- **Variability is noise to be averaged away.** Within-person variability is
  reasonably stable within a person and predicts outcomes of its own. The
  activity is built so that it is the one difference that survives.
- **More data always converges quickly.** Ada is within a point by about eight
  occasions; Bo takes roughly fifty. How many you need depends on the person.
- **A trait is fixed.** Nothing here shows that. A fortnight is not a lifetime,
  and the caution says so.
- **The average is the trait.** The average estimates the typical level. The
  trait is the whole distribution, which takes more than one number.

## Limitations and cautions

Two invented people, generated from a formula with a fixed seed, built to share
a level and differ in variability. Real pairs are rarely so tidy. Three things
the model leaves out, two of them stated on the page: there is no measurement
error, which in a real single reading is indistinguishable from genuine
fluctuation and which does shrink with averaging; the typical level does not
drift, which is defensible across fourteen days and not across fourteen months;
and readings are drawn independently, whereas real momentary reports correlate
within a day, so fifty-six real readings carry less information than fifty-six
here. That last simplification makes the demonstration understate how slowly Bo
converges, not overstate it.

## Accessibility considerations

The occasion count is a native range input reporting its value in text. The
answer options carry hue, border style and a corner glyph, plus a visually
hidden sentence naming the state. The two people are drawn in separate bands
rather than overlaid, so no reading has to be assigned to a person by colour,
and included readings are filled while excluded ones are hollow, which is a
difference of shape rather than only of tone. The chart description gives both
running averages, both spreads, both full ranges and the current gap.

## Optional extension

Ask students how many occasions they would schedule if they were designing a
study, and what they would need to know first. The answer, how variable the
people are, is not usually available before the study, which is the practical
problem the method has.

## Evidence and citation notes

On aggregation across occasions and the consistency it produces see Epstein
(1979), *The stability of behavior: On predicting most of the people much of
the time*, **Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 37(7), 1097–1126.
On within-person variability as a characteristic in its own right see Fleeson
(2001), *Toward a structure- and process-integrated view of personality*,
**Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 80(6), 1011–1027. On the
method see Csikszentmihalyi and Larson (1987), *Validity and reliability of the
experience-sampling method*, **Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease**, 175(9),
526–536.
