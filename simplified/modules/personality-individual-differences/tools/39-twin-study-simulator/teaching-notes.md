# The Same Genes, A Different Heritability — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** First or second year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the ACE decomposition, the twin
correlations and Falconer's formulae from the **Twin-Study Simulator**, and
reduces the three variance controls to one. Separately settable components,
simulated pairs with sampling error and the three assumption violations are in
the longer version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/39-twin-study-simulator/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say what heritability is a property of;
2. explain why it is not the proportion of an individual's trait caused by
   genes;
3. explain why it says nothing about changeability;
4. explain why equalising environments raises heritability;
5. explain why an estimate does not transfer to another population;
6. read Falconer's formulae and see that they are correct.

## Preparation

None.

## Suggested use

**At the start of any behavioural genetics teaching**, before a single
heritability figure is quoted, so that the qualifications arrive before the
numbers rather than after them.

**Whenever a heritability figure appears in the news**, as the thing to do to it.

**Projected, with the vote taken first.** The individual reading is chosen by
most of a room and is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing.

## Prediction question

> A study reports a heritability of 0.50. What does that mean?

Half the variation between people, in this population, in these environments,
is associated with genetic variation. Every qualification in that sentence is
doing work.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Before touching the slider, read the table.** The estimates match the
   truth exactly. Say clearly that the arithmetic is not the problem.
3. **Read the bar.** Genetic 1.00, shared 0.30, non-shared 1.00, total 2.30,
   heritability 0.43.
4. **Move the slider to the far right.** Heritability 0.19. Ask what changed
   about the genes. The first segment of the bar has not moved a pixel.
5. **Press "Make everybody's circumstances much more alike".** Heritability
   0.67.
6. **Stay on that.** Equalising the environment made the trait *more*
   heritable. Ask why. If everyone's circumstances were identical, every
   difference left between them would have to come from somewhere else.

## Debrief questions

- Which number did the slider actually move?
- Heritability went from 0.19 to 0.67. Did anybody's genes change?
- What does that tell you about reading heritability as a statement about a
  person?
- Making the environment more equal raised heritability. What does that do to
  the idea that a highly heritable trait is fixed?
- A heritability figure is reported for one country in one decade. What can you
  do with it elsewhere?
- The estimates matched the truth exactly here. Why is that not reassuring
  about real studies?

## Likely misconceptions

- **Half of my trait comes from my genes.** No such quantity exists. Asking it
  is like asking what proportion of a rectangle's area was caused by its width.
- **High heritability means fixed.** The whole demonstration consists of
  changing the environment, and it raises heritability.
- **Heritability means genes have been found.** None is identified anywhere in
  the arithmetic.
- **Non-shared environment means upbringing.** It is what makes two children in
  one family different, which is largely not upbringing.
- **A heritability figure describes the trait.** It describes one population at
  one time across the environments that population happened to contain.
- **The estimates matching the truth means the method is safe.** It means the
  method is correct under its assumptions, which is a different claim.

## Limitations and cautions

There is no sampling error here: the correlations are what the model implies
exactly, whereas a real study estimates them from a finite number of pairs and
real heritability estimates are considerably less precise than two decimal
places suggest. The formulae rest on assumptions this model satisfies and
reality may not, chiefly that identical and fraternal pairs share their
environments to the same degree and that partners do not resemble each other on
the trait. There is no gene-environment interaction and no gene-environment
correlation, both of which are real and both of which this decomposition simply
assigns elsewhere. And none of it bears on anybody as an individual.

## Accessibility considerations

The control is a native range input reporting its value in text. The
correlations, estimates and true values are in a real table with headers. The
variance bar has a key below it naming each segment with its value rather than
relying on colour, the total is marked with a labelled line, and the arithmetic
of the ratio is written out in words beneath the bar.

## Optional extension

Ask students what would happen to the heritability of reading ability if a
country made schooling universal and uniformly good. The answer, that it would
rise, is the same arithmetic as the guided button and is the reason a rising
heritability is not evidence that an intervention failed.

## Evidence and citation notes

On the definition and its qualifications see Visscher, Hill and Wray (2008),
*Heritability in the genomics era*, **Nature Reviews Genetics**, 9(4), 255–266.
On why heritability says nothing about malleability see Block (1995), *How
heritability misleads about race*, **Cognition**, 56(2), 99–128, and Turkheimer
(2000), *Three laws of behavior genetics and what they mean*, **Current
Directions in Psychological Science**, 9(5), 160–164. On heritability varying
with the environmental range in real data see Turkheimer, Haley, Waldron,
D'Onofrio and Gottesman (2003), *Socioeconomic status modifies heritability of
IQ in young children*, **Psychological Science**, 14(6), 623–628. Falconer's
formulae are from Falconer (1960), **Introduction to Quantitative Genetics**,
Oliver and Boyd.
