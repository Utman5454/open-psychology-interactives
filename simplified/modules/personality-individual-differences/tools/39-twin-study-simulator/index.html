The Same Genes, A Different Heritability

Heritability is a fraction. This page leaves the top of the fraction alone and changes the bottom.

The one sentence to take away

Heritability is the proportion of the variation between people in a particular population, living in the range of environments that population happens to contain, that is associated with genetic variation. It is not the proportion of any individual's trait caused by their genes, no such proportion exists, and it says nothing about whether the trait can be changed.

Key terms
Heritability
The share of the variation in a trait, in one population at one time, associated with genetic variation. A property of that population, not of any person in it.
Shared and non-shared environment
The part of the environment that makes members of the same family alike, and the part that makes them different. The second is usually the larger of the two, and it is not upbringing in the ordinary sense.
Falconer's formulae
The arithmetic that turns two correlations into three variance components: double the gap between the identical and fraternal correlations for the genetic share, and so on.

    What this shows

    A number about a population, at a time, in a range of environments

    Key idea: Not one gene changed anywhere on this page. The amount of genetic variation stayed at exactly 1.00 from beginning to end, and the heritability of the trait ran from 0.19 to 0.67 all the same, because heritability is a ratio and the slider was moving the denominator. Three things follow, and they are the three things heritability is most often taken to mean. It cannot be the proportion of an individual's trait caused by their genes, because it changed without any individual changing. It cannot say whether a trait can be altered, because the whole demonstration consisted of altering the environment, and doing so raised heritability rather than lowering it. And it does not travel: a figure from one population, at one time, across the range of environments that population happened to contain, is a description of that and of nothing else.

    The model here is the simple one the formulae assume, and it is simple in ways real data are not. There is no sampling error at all: the correlations shown are the ones the model implies exactly, whereas a real study estimates them from a finite number of pairs and reports them with a confidence interval, and heritability estimates from real twin studies are considerably less precise than the two decimal places here suggest. The formulae also rest on assumptions that this model satisfies and reality may not, chiefly that identical and fraternal pairs share their environments to the same degree, and that partners do not resemble each other on the trait; when those fail the estimates go wrong in specific directions. There is no gene-environment interaction and no correlation between genes and environment, both of which are real and both of which this decomposition simply assigns elsewhere. And one thing that does not need qualifying: none of this bears on anybody as an individual, and heritability was never a statement about a person.
