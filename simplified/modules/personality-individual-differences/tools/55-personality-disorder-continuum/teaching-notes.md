# Where the Line Goes — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 6–8 minutes
**Level:** Final year or postgraduate
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the continuum, the movable line and the two
competing rules from the **Personality Disorder Continuum**, cut from six
dimensions to the two that carry the argument. The other four dimensions, the
profile builder and the worked pair are in the longer version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/55-personality-disorder-continuum/`.

## Before you teach it

The page contains no diagnostic instrument and returns no diagnosis. It asks
nothing about the reader or anybody they know, names nobody, names no
diagnosis, and reproduces no criteria from any classification system. It says
plainly that personality difficulties are real and cause real suffering. The
phrase used throughout is that a profile falls above or below a line, which is
a statement about the line.

Two failure modes are worth watching for in discussion. One is students
concluding that because a boundary is chosen, the difficulties are not real or
the categories are worthless; the page argues against that explicitly and so
should you. The other is the discussion drifting towards diagnosing a public
figure or somebody in the room, which is a good moment to point out that the
page has two numbers per profile and real assessment has a history, a context,
corroboration and time.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. explain why unusual traits are not sufficient for a disorder-level reading;
2. distinguish trait extremity from interference;
3. say what changes when a continuum is cut;
4. explain why prevalence follows the threshold;
5. explain why a chosen boundary is not thereby arbitrary;
6. say why distress belongs to a separate question.

## Preparation

Students should have met the dimensional and categorical debate at least in
outline.

## Suggested use

**Alongside teaching on classification**, whether the syllabus favours a
categorical or a dimensional account, since the point is about the step between
them rather than about which is right.

**In a seminar on prevalence figures**, where the population readout does the
work: the same population, twenty times the prevalence.

**Projected, with the vote taken first.** Both partial answers are worth
discussing at length.

## Prediction question

> What most makes a personality pattern a clinical question rather than simply
> a personality?

Inflexibility, persistence and interference. Both the distress answer and the
where-the-line-falls answer are marked partial rather than wrong, and the
reasons are given on screen.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.** Spend time on the two partial answers.
2. **Open the continuum at a line of 70 on the interference rule.** Two of the
   four profiles are above it.
3. **Press "Show me a profile that changes sides".** Read the note.
4. **Name the two profiles that moved.** Unusual traits and managing well;
   ordinary traits and a great deal of difficulty. Ask which of them a service
   should see.
5. **Go back to the interference rule and move the line from 55 to 90.** Watch
   the population share fall from about 27 per cent to under one.
6. **Ask what changed about the population.** Nothing.

## Debrief questions

- Which profiles changed sides, and what changed about them?
- Which of the two rules would you defend, and on what grounds?
- The share of the population above the line changes twentyfold. Is any of
  those numbers the right one?
- Does a boundary being chosen mean the difficulties are not real?
- Why is distress not one of the two dimensions here?
- What does a category give you that a pair of numbers does not?

## Likely misconceptions

- **A chosen boundary makes everything arbitrary.** It does not, and the page
  says so twice. What lies either side of a chosen line can be perfectly real.
- **Dimensions are simply better than categories.** Categories carry
  information about what has helped before and open doors to care that
  dimensions currently do not.
- **Unusual means disordered.** One of the four profiles has traits a long way
  from the average and is managing perfectly well.
- **Distress is what makes it clinical.** It is a reason for help in its own
  right, and it can be absent where there is a great deal of interference.
- **Prevalence is discovered.** When the underlying dimension is continuous, it
  is set by where the line goes.
- **This is a criticism of clinicians.** It is about what happens
  arithmetically when a continuum is cut, which is true of every cut on every
  continuum.

## Limitations and cautions

Four invented profiles and two invented scales, chosen to make the argument
legible. Real assessment does not consist of two numbers and a threshold. The
population is an invented normal distribution used only to show that a count
follows a cut, and is not an estimate of anything. The page takes no position
on any particular classification system, current or proposed.

## Accessibility considerations

The line is a native range input reporting its value in text, and the rule is a
button carrying `aria-pressed` with a visible word saying which is in force.
Every profile's numbers and its current side are in a real table with headers,
with the side given in words. In the figure each marker is labelled with its
side in words as well as being filled or hollow, both axes are named in words,
and the description states the line, the rule and which profiles are above it.

## Optional extension

Ask students to write the sentence they would put in a report about the profile
with unusual traits who is managing well, under each of the two rules, and then
to say which sentence they would want written about themselves. The asymmetry
between the two is where the ethical weight of the boundary sits.

## Evidence and citation notes

On dimensional against categorical accounts see Widiger and Trull (2007),
*Plate tectonics in the classification of personality disorder*, **American
Psychologist**, 62(2), 71–83. On the arithmetic of imposing thresholds on
continua see Markon, Chmielewski and Miller (2011), *The reliability and
validity of discrete and continuous measures of psychopathology*,
**Psychological Bulletin**, 137(5), 856–879. On distress and impairment as
separable, and on why unusualness is not sufficient, see Wakefield (1992), *The
concept of mental disorder*, **American Psychologist**, 47(3), 373–388. On what
categories do that dimensions currently do not, see First et al. (2004),
*Clinical utility as a criterion for revising psychiatric diagnoses*,
**American Journal of Psychiatry**, 161(6), 946–954.
