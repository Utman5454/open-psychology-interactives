# Reverse-Item Disaster — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality and Individual Differences
**Duration:** 4–5 minutes
**Level:** First year upwards, before any assignment that scores a scale
**Edition:** Simplified. The longer version adds two further ways of getting
the keying wrong, corrected item-total correlations for every item, and a
repair walkthrough over both reverse items. It is at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/13-reverse-item-disaster/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. state the recoding rule and apply it to an answer;
2. explain why an unrecoded reverse item pulls against the rest of a scale;
3. say what a keying error does to the ordering of people;
4. explain why alpha can flag a problem without revealing what it did;
5. say why reverse wording is a trade rather than a free fix.

## Preparation

None. It runs in a browser with no account, no network and no setup.

## Suggested use

**Immediately before students score anything themselves.** This is a
five-minute inoculation and it is worth spending it at exactly that point in a
module rather than earlier.

**Projected, in three minutes.** Show the uncorrected scoring, point at the
alpha of −0.04, and ask what they would do if they saw that in their own
output. Most say "drop items" or "the scale is bad". Then repair it.

**As preparation** before a session on reliability, so that alpha arrives as a
diagnostic with a known blind spot rather than as a number to report.

## Prediction question

Before students open it, ask:

> Two of six items are worded the other way round and nobody recoded them.
> What does that do?

Almost everyone answers in terms of the scale: lower reliability, a bad alpha.
Very few answer in terms of the people. That gap is the activity.

## Activity sequence

1. **As scored.** Alpha is −0.04, and four of the six items correlate
   negatively with the rest of the scale. Only two of them are reverse-keyed;
   the other two look broken because the total they are being compared against
   is broken.
2. **Repair the first reverse item.** The learner computes 6 − 5 and enters it,
   and the rule is applied to that whole column. Alpha moves to **+0.03**,
   which is almost nothing. What does move is item 5, from −0.42 to **−0.89**.
   Repairing one column made the other one obvious.
3. **Repair the second.** Alpha jumps to **+0.78** and every item is positive.
   The respondents' ordering is mentioned once here as a consequence, and is
   not the subject.

## Debrief questions

- Alpha was −0.04. What would you have concluded from that alone?
- Four items had negative item-total correlations and only two were
  reverse-keyed. Why were the other two negative?
- After the first repair alpha was still +0.03. Why did fixing a broken item
  not help?
- Item 5 went from −0.42 to −0.89 when item 3 was repaired. What made it worse?
- Alpha caught this. Name a keying error alpha would not catch.

## The moment worth stopping on

Two of them. First, the middle question: after repairing one of the two reverse
items, alpha has gone from −0.04 to +0.03 and a student watching only alpha
would conclude that nothing was achieved. The item-total correlations tell a
completely different story, and item 5 has become impossible to miss. That is
the argument for reading an item analysis rather than a single coefficient, and
it is on screen for about four seconds unless you stop.

Then the last question. A negative alpha is loud and would be investigated. An
error that recodes 7 − raw instead of 6 − raw shifts every reverse answer by
exactly one point, leaves alpha looking healthy and every total looking
plausible, and quietly biases the scale. That error is in the longer version,
and it is worth naming here even though this activity does not demonstrate it,
because it is the reason "alpha was fine" is not a defence.

## Likely misconceptions

**"A keying error is a reliability problem."** It shows up as one. What it
does is reorder people, and reliability is a property of the instrument.

**"If alpha is acceptable, the scoring is right."** No. Alpha is one summary
of internal consistency and there are keying errors it does not disturb.

**"Reading the item as reversed is the same as recoding it."** It is not, and
it is a common mistake among people who would never simply forget. The
arithmetic is identical to doing nothing.

**"Just drop the badly behaved items."** Dropping the two reverse items here
would raise alpha and leave a scale measuring only agreement with positively
worded statements, which is the problem reverse items exist to address. Note
that this activity says nothing about how choosing different items changes
alpha in general; that is a separate lesson.

**"Reverse items are good practice, so use lots."** They are a trade. See the
cautions.

## Limitations and cautions

- Six respondents is far too few for a real reliability estimate. Every figure
  here is arithmetic on an invented dataset chosen to make the reordering
  visible.
- The dataset was selected so that the reordering happens. A set of perfectly
  coherent respondents would not reorder under this error, because with only
  two of six items reversed the four positive items still dominate the total.
  That is worth saying out loud: the size of the damage depends on how many
  items are reversed and on how consistent the respondents are.
- Reverse wording reduces agreement bias, and it also introduces comprehension
  difficulty, is answered inconsistently by people reading quickly, and
  routinely forms a method factor that can be mistaken for a second
  substantive dimension.
- Alpha is not the only reliability estimate, and it has well-known problems
  of its own that this activity does not touch.

## Accessibility

Keyboard operable throughout, and the recoding entry accepts Enter. A wrong
entry is refused with the rule restated rather than silently corrected. Every
score, rank and reliability figure is text in a table rather than only a
graphic. The rank-order figure names each respondent and their position in
both columns and carries a description stating both orderings in full and how
many people changed place, so nothing depends on tracing a line. There is no
timed content and no motion.

## Extension

Ask what would have to be true of a dataset for this error to change alpha but
not the ordering, and what would have to be true for it to change the ordering
but not alpha. Both are possible, and working out why is a better test of
understanding than reciting the recoding formula.

## Evidence and reading

The literature on acquiescence and on reverse-worded items, and the
long-standing critical work on Cronbach's alpha and what it does and does not
license. Send students to those rather than to this activity: it is a
demonstration of one failure mode, not a treatment of reliability.
