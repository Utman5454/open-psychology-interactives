# Three Findings, Two Lines — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** Final year or postgraduate
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the two-slope model, the three patterns and
the movable window from the **Gene by Environment Interaction Visualiser**, with
the crossover and both slopes fixed so that only the sampled range changes.
Independent control of the crossover, the slopes and the window width, and the
predict-before-you-look exercise, are in the longer version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/42-gene-environment-interaction-visualiser/`.

## A word before you teach it

No gene is named and no genotype appears; the grouping variable is a fictional
sensitivity score and the two groups are two halves of a continuum rather than
two kinds of person. There is no disorder, diagnosis or risk estimate, the
outcome axis is unnamed, and no figure corresponds to any published finding. A
slope here is an average tendency across a group and never a prediction about a
person.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. distinguish the three patterns by their shapes;
2. explain how the sampled range decides which one a study finds;
3. explain why describing a window accurately is not a finding;
4. recognise that range restriction does not announce itself;
5. say what a paper reporting an interaction should report about its range.

## Preparation

Students should have met the idea of a statistical interaction.

## Suggested use

**When gene-environment interaction is introduced**, and particularly before
the diathesis-stress and differential-susceptibility literatures are set
against each other as rival findings.

**In a seminar on generalisation from samples**, where it works as a case that
has nothing to do with sampling people and everything to do with sampling
conditions.

**Projected, with the vote taken first.** Most of a room picks the
vulnerability reading, which is marked partial rather than wrong.

## Prediction question

> A study finds the higher-sensitivity group does worse, with the groups
> converging towards the better end of what was measured. What does that
> support?

Nothing beyond the range it measured. The vulnerability reading is an accurate
description of the window and becomes a claim about sensitivity only by
assuming the lines carry on past the edge of the data.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Open the explorer, which starts on the study as reported.** Verdict:
   sensitivity as a vulnerability.
3. **Point at the faint dashed lines before moving anything.** That is the part
   the study did not measure, and no study can see its own.
4. **Slide the window to the supportive end.** Verdict: sensitivity as an
   advantage. Ask what changed about the lines.
5. **Press "Widen the study to the whole range".** Verdict: both ways.
6. **Read the standing sentence.** Both slopes have been 1.20 and 0.35
   throughout.

## Debrief questions

- Three studies reported three different things. Which of them was wrong?
- What would the first study have had to do differently?
- The faint lines are visible to you. What is the equivalent for a real
  researcher?
- Is there an interaction in the first window? Is it real?
- What should a paper reporting an interaction tell you about its range?
- Does this show that published differential-susceptibility findings are
  artefacts?

## Likely misconceptions

- **One of the three studies made a mistake.** None did. Each described its
  window correctly.
- **The first study found no interaction.** It found one. Different slopes are
  an interaction. What it could not settle is what the interaction means.
- **Restricted range would be visible in the data.** It is not. The lines are
  straight, the statistics are correct and nothing inside the window looks
  wrong.
- **This debunks the literature.** It shows what a restricted range can do. The
  three accounts are genuinely argued about on much better evidence than this.
- **Widening the range always dissolves an interaction.** It can equally reveal
  one that a narrow window hid.
- **The two groups are two kinds of person.** They are two halves of a
  continuous score.

## Limitations and cautions

Both lines are perfectly straight and the data have almost no error and no
confounding, which is what lets the three patterns separate so cleanly. Real
data are far messier, and distinguishing these accounts in practice is hard and
contested. The crossover and slopes are fixed here; in the longer version they
move, and moving them is its own lesson.

## Accessibility considerations

The sampled range is a native range input whose output reads as words rather
than a number. The verdict is stated in words in a readout tile alongside the
two gaps that produce it, so the figure is never the only route to the finding.
Both groups are labelled by name at the ends of their lines, the axis ends are
labelled in words, and the measured window is shaded as well as marked by line
weight.

## Optional extension

Ask students what range of environments a study recruiting through a university
would sample, and what pattern it would therefore be able to find. The answer,
that a narrow and generally supportive range makes vantage sensitivity easy to
find and diathesis-stress nearly invisible, connects the point to how samples
are actually assembled.

## Evidence and citation notes

On differential susceptibility see Belsky and Pluess (2009), *Beyond
diathesis-stress*, **Psychological Bulletin**, 135(6), 885–908. On vantage
sensitivity see Pluess and Belsky (2013), *Vantage sensitivity*,
**Psychological Bulletin**, 139(4), 901–916. On the analytic difficulty of
telling these patterns apart, and on why the observed range matters, see Roisman
et al. (2012), *Distinguishing differential susceptibility from
diathesis-stress*, **Development and Psychopathology**, 24(2), 389–409. On the
wider problem of interpreting interactions from restricted ranges see Duncan and
Keller (2011), *A critical review of the first 10 years of candidate
gene-by-environment interaction research*, **American Journal of Psychiatry**,
168(10), 1041–1049.
