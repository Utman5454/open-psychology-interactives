# Same Score, Different People — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** First or second year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps one domain, the two-level prediction
comparison and the item-mix control from **Facet-Level Detective**. The other
two domains, the observation-matching task and the profile studio are in the
longer version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/09-facet-level-detective/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say what a broad domain score averages away;
2. explain why two people with the same score can differ in patterned ways;
3. compare a facet-level and a domain-level prediction of the same behaviour;
4. explain why the domain-level prediction gives both people the same number,
   whatever the behaviour;
5. explain how the item mix changes which unchanged person scores higher;
6. say why narrower is not automatically better.

## Preparation

None. Students need only to have met the idea of a broad personality trait.

## Suggested use

**When broad trait scores are first introduced**, so that the qualification
arrives at the same time as the concept rather than a year later.

**Before teaching about criterion validity or selection.** The last column of
the second table is the bandwidth-fidelity problem in one screen.

**Projected, with the vote taken first.** The dishonesty option is chosen more
often than it should be, and that is worth a conversation on its own.

## Prediction question

> Two people score the same on a broad trait and behave quite differently over
> several weeks. What is the most likely explanation?

The answer is that the broad score is an average over narrower traits. The
unreliability and situations answers are marked as defensible readings that do
not fit this description, not as simple errors, because both would be right
about other observations.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Reveal the facets.** Sam 84 and 48, Priya 50 and 82, both averaging 66.
3. **Read the second table's last column downwards.** It is 66 four times, for
   both people. Ask why. Because a broad score is all that prediction knows.
4. **Read the two facet columns.** They separate the two on every observation
   by at least 22 points, and they swap over twice.
5. **Go to step 3 and press the guided button.** All items about tidiness:
   Priya 82, Sam 48. Then take the slider to the far right: Sam 84, Priya 50.
6. **Ask what changed about Sam and Priya.** Nothing did.

## Debrief questions

- What does a conscientiousness score of 66 tell you about somebody?
- Why is the last column of the second table the same number four times?
- Observation three describes a chaotic desk and finished work. Which facet is
  that, and which person does the facet model expect it from?
- Two published questionnaires both measure conscientiousness and disagree
  about who is higher. Is either of them wrong?
- If facets are more informative, why does anyone report domain scores?

## Likely misconceptions

- **The broad score was wrong.** It was an accurate average. It predicts broad
  outcomes well and cannot distinguish these two.
- **Facets are the real traits and domains are an approximation.** Neither
  level is more real. Broad measures predict broad outcomes better, narrow
  measures narrow ones, and the level you measure at follows from what you want
  to predict.
- **The tie was a coincidence.** It was constructed, and the page says so. The
  point is not that ties happen but what they conceal.
- **Someone must be misreporting.** The structure of the measure explains the
  observation without any assumption about honesty.
- **One of them is really more conscientious.** The activity shows the opposite:
  which one is ahead depends on a measurement choice somebody made.

## Limitations and cautions

Two invented people and four invented observations, with tendencies produced by
a formula rather than measured. Real domains have five or six facets, not two;
real aspects of a domain correlate positively rather than trading off cleanly,
so a real pair would rarely be such neat mirror images; and real behaviour has
error in it that this model does not. The clean crossover is a property of the
construction. What generalises is the structure of the argument, not the
tidiness of the numbers.

## Accessibility considerations

Both tables are real tables with row and column headers, so no number is
reachable only through the figure. The item mix is a native range input
reporting its value in text, and the two lines in the figure are labelled by
name at their ends rather than distinguished by colour alone. The chart
description gives each person's score at both extremes, the crossing point and
the current values.

## Optional extension

Ask students which facet they would measure if they were hiring for a role that
involves long unsupervised work, and which for a role that involves keeping
records straight, and then what the broad score would have told them in each
case. The answer, the same thing both times, is the argument for measuring
narrowly when the outcome is narrow.

## Evidence and citation notes

On the two-aspect structure used here see DeYoung, Quilty and Peterson (2007),
*Between facets and domains: 10 aspects of the Big Five*, **Journal of
Personality and Social Psychology**, 93(5), 880–896. On bandwidth and fidelity
see Cronbach and Gleser (1965), **Psychological Tests and Personnel Decisions**,
2nd edition, University of Illinois Press, and Ones and Viswesvaran (1996),
*Bandwidth-fidelity dilemma in personality measurement for personnel
selection*, **Journal of Organizational Behavior**, 17(6), 609–626. On facets
predicting outcomes their domain does not, see Paunonen and Ashton (2001),
*Big Five factors and facets and the prediction of behavior*, **Journal of
Personality and Social Psychology**, 81(3), 524–539.
