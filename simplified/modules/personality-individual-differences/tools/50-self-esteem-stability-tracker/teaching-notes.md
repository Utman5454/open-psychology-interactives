# Same Average, Six Very Different Weeks — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** First or second year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the daily simulation and the event delivery
from the **Self-Esteem Stability Tracker**, cut to three people and two
domains. A fourth person, choosing when events land, positive events and a
wider set of domains are in the longer version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/50-self-esteem-stability-tracker/`.

## A word before you teach it

The page asks nothing about the learner, offers no questionnaire and produces
no score for anybody. Low or variable values here are characteristics that
differ between people, not symptoms, and nothing on the page is a clinical
statement or a basis for interpreting anyone's wellbeing. If the discussion
turns to which of the three is doing worst, that is the moment to say the page
is not in a position to answer.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. separate level, variability, contingency and recovery;
2. explain why a single score estimates only the first;
3. explain why ordinary movement does not predict what knocks somebody over;
4. explain why being affected by one kind of event is not general fragility;
5. recognise recovery as a third, separate property;
6. treat variability as a characteristic rather than a symptom.

## Relation to the paired activity

This is not the same lesson as **One Moment, or the Whole Fortnight?**, which is
about a single observation against an average. Here every person's average is
already known and identical, and the question is what else there is.

## Preparation

None.

## Suggested use

**When self-esteem is introduced**, and specifically when a single score is
about to be used as the variable in something.

**In a seminar on contingent self-worth**, where the second event is the point:
the person devastated by the work setback barely registers the falling-out.

**Projected, with the vote taken first.** "The ones who fluctuate have a
problem" is worth surfacing and correcting on both counts.

## Prediction question

> Three people score identically on a one-off measure. What does that tell you
> about their next six weeks?

Very little. Note that "nothing checkable without measuring them repeatedly" is
marked partial: the score does estimate the level, badly, and the useful
complaint is narrower than "nothing".

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Look at the six weeks with nothing delivered.** All three average exactly
   62. Ada and Cleo move by 2.8 a day, Bo by 7.5.
3. **Ask which two are indistinguishable.** Ada and Cleo, on both numbers a
   study would have collected.
4. **Deliver the setback at work.** Cleo drops 22 points; Ada drops 5.5.
5. **Read the last column.** Ada is back in 3 days. Cleo has not returned by
   the end of the six weeks.
6. **Deliver the falling-out with a friend.** Cleo barely moves. Ask what that
   does to the idea that Cleo is fragile.

## Debrief questions

- Which two people would a one-off measure have treated as identical?
- Bo moves most from day to day. Was Bo knocked furthest by the setback?
- Cleo was devastated by one event and barely touched by the other. What is
  that a property of?
- Ada and Cleo both took a knock in the domain that matters to them. What
  differed?
- Which of these four things would a study reporting one number per person
  have?
- Is any of these four a problem?

## Likely misconceptions

- **Fluctuating is unhealthy.** It is a characteristic that differs between
  people, and the page will not call it anything else.
- **The most variable person is the most vulnerable.** Bo moves most and is not
  knocked furthest.
- **Being strongly contingent means being fragile.** Cleo is the least affected
  of the three by one of the two events.
- **Recovering slowly follows from being knocked hard.** They are separate
  parameters here, and separate properties in the literature.
- **This is the same point as aggregating occasions.** It is not. Every average
  here is already known and identical.

## Limitations and cautions

Three invented people, a formula and a fixed seed. The model is tidy in ways
life is not: events arrive when a button is pressed rather than partly in
consequence of how somebody is already doing, which is the largest thing it
cannot represent; recovery is a smooth decay back to exactly the starting
point; and nothing anybody does changes their own contingencies. The two
domains are invented labels rather than a taxonomy, and what a person's
self-esteem is contingent on is a question for research.

## Accessibility considerations

Events are ordinary buttons, and a delivered one carries `aria-disabled` rather
than the `disabled` attribute so it stays reachable. All four properties are in
a real table with headers, and a person who has not recovered is described in
words rather than by an absent value. Each line is labelled by name at its end,
event markers are labelled with what was delivered, and the chart description
gives every average and every day-to-day movement.

## Optional extension

Ask students to design a study that would measure all four properties, and then
to say what it would cost. The answer, daily measurement over weeks, is why so
many studies report one number per person, and knowing that the shortcut is a
shortcut is most of what this activity is for.

## Evidence and citation notes

On stability as distinct from level see Kernis (2005), *Measuring self-esteem
in context*, **Journal of Personality**, 73(6), 1569–1605. On contingency see
Crocker and Wolfe (2001), *Contingencies of self-worth*, **Psychological
Review**, 108(4), 593–623. On the measurement of within-person variability and
what it predicts beyond a mean see Ram and Gerstorf (2009), *Time-structured
and net intraindividual variability*, **Psychology and Aging**, 24(4), 778–791.
