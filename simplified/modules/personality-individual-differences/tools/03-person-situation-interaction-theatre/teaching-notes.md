# How Much Room Does the Situation Leave? — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Personality & Individual Differences
**Duration:** 4–6 minutes
**Level:** First or second year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the live strength control and the two
contrasting settings from **Person-Situation Interaction Theatre**. The
ranking prediction, further settings, the aggregation demonstration and the
transfer exercise are in the longer version at
`modules/personality-individual-differences/tools/03-person-situation-interaction-theatre/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say what a strong situation does to observed between-person variation;
2. say why low observed variance is not evidence of low trait variance;
3. distinguish constraint from affordance;
4. say why two open settings can still rank people differently;
5. reject both one-line summaries of the person-situation debate.

## Preparation

None.

## Suggested use

**Projected, with the slider driven from the front.** Start weak, so the four
people are obviously different and the lines obviously cross. Then take the
slider to the top and ask what changed about the people. Nothing did.

**In the lecture that covers the person-situation debate**, before either
position is stated, so the room reaches "both, and they interact" on its own.

## Prediction question

With the slider at the bottom:

> Amara does most at the party. Will she also do most at the handover?

She will do least. The lines cross, and the crossing is the interaction.

## Activity sequence

1. **Weak settings.** Note the spread and the crossing lines.
2. **Ask why the orders differ.** The party affords sociability; the handover
   affords conscientiousness. The people have not moved.
3. **Press "Make both settings very strong".** Read the note: the spread falls
   from about 26 to about 1.
4. **Ask what a researcher observing only the strong setting would conclude.**
5. **Read the caution**, especially the sentence about people selecting their
   own situations.

## Debrief questions

- What happened to the people when you moved the slider?
- A study finds almost no differences between people. What are the two
  explanations?
- Why do the two settings put Amara and Carla in opposite orders?
- Is "personality matters more than situations" a claim this activity can
  settle?
- In real life, who chooses which situations you are in?

## Likely misconceptions

- **Low observed variance means people are alike.** It can mean the setting
  left no room.
- **A strong situation changes personality.** It changes behaviour. The traits
  in the model are constant throughout.
- **The interaction is a small extra on top of two main effects.** Here it is a
  reversal, which no main effect can describe.
- **Situations can be ranked on one dimension of strength.** They cannot, and
  the caution says so.
- **This shows situations matter more.** It shows both matter and depend on
  each other.

## Limitations and cautions

Four invented people and two invented settings, with behaviour generated from a
formula. Real situations are not arranged along a single strength dimension,
people select the situations they are in rather than being assigned, and the
same setting is not equally strong for everybody. The numbers are units of
nothing. Nothing here measures or predicts any real person.

## Accessibility considerations

The strength control is a native range input reporting its value in text. Each
person's line is labelled with their name and value at both ends, so no line
has to be traced across the figure or told apart by colour. The chart
description gives every person's value in both settings, both spreads, and
whether the settings agree about the ordering.

## Optional extension

Ask students to name a setting in their own week that is very strong and one
that is very weak, and to predict where a friend would look most like
themselves. The answer, the weak one, is the aggregation argument arriving
without the word.

## Evidence and citation notes

The strong-situation idea is Mischel (1977), *The interaction of person and
situation*, in Magnusson and Endler's **Personality at the Crossroads**. On
affordance and why weak situations differ from one another see Ten Berge and
De Raad (1999), *Taxonomies of situations from a trait psychological
perspective*, **European Journal of Personality**, 13(5), 337–360. On
aggregation and consistency see Epstein (1979), *The stability of behavior*,
**Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 37(7), 1097–1126.
