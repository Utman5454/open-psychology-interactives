# Remembering a Word Nobody Said — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Cognitive Psychology
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** First or second year, on memory
**Edition:** Simplified, and narrower than its parent. This keeps the studied
lists with labelled sources and all three judgements from **False Memory and
Source Monitoring**. The third list, the fuller error analysis and the
treatment of forewarning are in the longer version at
`modules/cognitive/tools/10-false-memory-source-monitoring/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say how converging associates produce a memory of a word never presented;
2. separate recognition, confidence and source as three judgements;
3. say why confidence is not a measure of accuracy;
4. say why naming a source is not proof of where something came from;
5. say what this demonstrates and what it does not.

## Preparation

None, and do not tell the room what is coming. The demonstration depends
entirely on the lure being a surprise. If you have taught this before to the
same group, use it as a discussion of forewarning instead, which is what the
longer version supports.

## Suggested use

**Individually and in silence**, then ask by show of hands who said yes to
SLEEP and who said yes to COLD. Neither was ever shown. Ask the people who
said yes how sure they were, and then ask who they said read it out.

**In the lecture on memory as reconstruction**, as the demonstration that
makes the claim concrete rather than rhetorical.

**Before any discussion of eyewitness evidence**, with the caution read out in
full so that nobody leaves with "memory is unreliable" as the takeaway.

## Prediction question

Ask before the test phase, after the lists have been shown:

> Of the ten words coming up, how many do you think you will get wrong?

Almost everyone says none or one, and they are usually right about the studied
and unrelated words and wrong in a very specific way about the other two.

## Activity sequence

1. **Watch the twenty words.** Nothing to press. Twenty seconds.
2. **Judge ten words, three ways each.** The confidence question only appears
   after a yes, and the source question only after that. This ordering
   matters: a student who saw all three at once would notice something odd
   about being asked who read out a word they were unsure of.
3. **Read the three tiles.** The unrelated column is the control: if it is at
   or near zero, the lure endorsements are not a yes bias.
4. **Read the lead**, which names the student's own false memories back to
   them, with their own confidence and the person they named.
5. **Read the caution together**, particularly the last two sentences.

## Debrief questions

- SLEEP was never on the screen. Why did it feel as though it was?
- You said you were sure. What was your confidence actually tracking?
- You said Ravi read it out. Where did that come from?
- The unrelated words were at zero for most people. Why does that matter?
- Does this show that memory is generally unreliable?

## Likely misconceptions

- **I must have missed it going past.** It was never shown. The table lists
  every test word and marks the lures as never shown.
- **This shows memory is unreliable in general.** It shows one specific
  mechanism, using materials built precisely to produce it. The caution refuses
  the general reading explicitly and it is worth reading aloud.
- **Confidence tracks accuracy.** This is the sharpest lesson available here
  and it generalises to eyewitness evidence directly.
- **Being able to say who said it proves it happened.** Source attribution is
  itself a judgement and can be constructed alongside the memory.
- **People who fall for it have poor memories.** The effect is strongest in
  people who encoded the lists well.

## Limitations and cautions

Two lists and ten test words from one person, so the percentages are rough and
a single guess moves them a long way. The lists were written for this activity
rather than taken from a published set, so they are not calibrated and the
effect may be weaker or stronger than published rates. A student who is not
caught has demonstrated nothing about themselves either. Nothing here is a
memory test and nothing is a norm.

## Accessibility considerations

Every judgement is a labelled button, keyboard operable, marked with a glyph
and a hidden note as well as a colour. The display description names the reader
and the word on every study item, so the study phase can be followed without
seeing the panel. Nothing needs pressing during study and nothing is timed out
at test. The chart description gives all three rates and the table lists every
word with what it really was.

## Optional extension

Ask students to construct a third list for a lure of their own choosing, then
swap with a neighbour and test each other. Working out what makes a good
converging list teaches the mechanism better than any explanation of it.

## Evidence and citation notes

The paradigm is Deese (1959), *On the prediction of occurrence of particular
verbal intrusions in immediate recall*, **Journal of Experimental Psychology**,
58(1), 17–22, revived by Roediger and McDermott (1995), *Creating false
memories*, **Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and
Cognition**, 21(4), 803–814. The lists used here are not theirs. For source
monitoring see Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay (1993), *Source monitoring*,
**Psychological Bulletin**, 114(1), 3–28. On confidence and accuracy in
eyewitness identification see Wixted and Wells (2017), *The relationship
between eyewitness confidence and identification accuracy*, **Psychological
Science in the Public Interest**, 18(1), 10–65, which is more nuanced than the
usual classroom summary.
