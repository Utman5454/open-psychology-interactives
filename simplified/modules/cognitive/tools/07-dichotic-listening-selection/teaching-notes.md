# What Got Through — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Cognitive Psychology
**Duration:** 4–6 minutes
**Level:** Second year, alongside the selection theories
**Edition:** Simplified, and narrower than its parent. This keeps the performed
two-stream task and the two probes from **Dichotic Listening and Selection
Theories**. The harder attended task, the naive first trial, the simulated
class data and the theory-by-theory prediction table are in the longer version
at `modules/cognitive/tools/07-dichotic-listening-selection/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. separate detecting a physical change from identifying meaning;
2. say which findings each selection theory was built to handle;
3. say why failure to report is weak evidence of absence of processing;
4. say why a theory that fits everything is not supported by everything;
5. say what a visual analogue of a listening task does and does not show.

## Preparation

**Read the first caution before teaching this.** The demonstration is visual,
and the original experiments were auditory. Two speech streams into two ears
genuinely cannot both be followed; two columns of text can be sampled, however
hard someone tries not to. The visual version therefore *understates* the
effect. Say this early rather than letting a student find the hole.

A skip route is offered on the page for anyone who cannot read two columns at
once, and it reaches the same finding.

## Suggested use

**Individually, in silence.** The whole thing takes about a minute to run and
the two probes take seconds. Then collect answers by show of hands: who caught
the change in capitals, and who could name the category. The split in the room
is the result.

**Immediately before the lecture that introduces Broadbent, Treisman and
Deutsch and Deutsch**, so the theories arrive as answers to something already
observed.

## Prediction question

Before starting, after reading the instructions:

> You are about to ignore the right-hand column completely for about twenty
> seconds. Afterwards I will ask you two questions about it. How much do you
> think you will be able to tell me?

Most students say "nothing". They are usually half right, and which half is
the point.

## Activity sequence

1. **Run the task.** Insist that the attended job is done properly; the hit
   rate is reported afterwards and a student who caught one colour word out of
   five was not attending to the left at all.
2. **First probe, answered before the second is visible.** This ordering
   matters: seeing the category question first would prompt the answer.
3. **Second probe.**
4. **Read the three tiles.** The change caught, the meaning missed, and the
   attended hit rate that says whether the first two mean anything.
5. **Read the takeaway**, then the caution, and spend most of the time on the
   last sentence of it.

## Debrief questions

- You looked straight at all twenty-four ignored words. Why can you not say
  what they were?
- Why is noticing the capitals easier than naming the category?
- Suppose you had missed both. Would that show the ignored words were blocked
  entirely?
- Broadbent's filter blocks the ignored channel. What in your own result does
  it fail to explain?
- Every version of the theory can be made to fit these two results. Is that a
  point in their favour?

## Likely misconceptions

- **Ignored means unprocessed.** The physical change was caught, so something
  was getting through.
- **Not reporting means not processing.** These look identical from outside,
  and that is the central difficulty of the whole literature rather than a
  detail.
- **Late selection is the modern, correct answer.** The evidence constrains
  where selection happens; it does not settle it, and the takeaway says so.
- **This is the same as dichotic listening.** It is a visual analogue and a
  weaker one. Say so.
- **Catching both means the student did badly.** It usually means the attended
  task was not demanding enough, which is a fact about the design.

## Limitations and cautions

The largest limitation is the modality: this is a visual analogue of an
auditory paradigm, and it understates how completely an ignored channel is
lost. One trial from one person proves nothing, and with four options a guess
is right one time in four, so a correct category answer should be treated
sceptically. The attended task is a modest load; a harder one shifts the
pattern, which is what the longer version demonstrates. Nothing here measures
anyone's attention.

## Accessibility considerations

The attended response works from the space bar or a labelled button. The
display description names the attended word and says that an ignored word is
also present, but never reads the ignored word: doing so would give away the
second probe. Because the task requires seeing two columns at once, and a
screen reader necessarily serialises them, a skip route is offered from the
start and is a complete alternative rather than a consolation. Nothing flashes
or moves. The probes are keyboard operable and marked with a glyph and a hidden
note as well as a colour.

## Optional extension

Ask what would have to change for the category to get through: the answer
usually offered is "make the ignored words matter", which is exactly the
manipulation behind the own-name effect, and leads straight into why
attenuation was proposed.

## Evidence and citation notes

The paradigm is Cherry (1953), *Some experiments on the recognition of speech,
with one and with two ears*, **Journal of the Acoustical Society of America**,
25(5), 975–979. Broadbent's filter is in *Perception and Communication* (1958),
Treisman's attenuation in Treisman (1964), *Selective attention in man*,
**British Medical Bulletin**, 20(1), 12–16, and late selection in Deutsch and
Deutsch (1963), *Attention: Some theoretical considerations*, **Psychological
Review**, 70(1), 80–90. For the own-name finding see Moray (1959), *Attention
in dichotic listening*, **Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology**,
11(1), 56–60.
