# Attentional Blink — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Cognitive Psychology
**Duration:** 6–8 minutes
**Level:** Second year, once attention has been introduced
**Edition:** Simplified, and narrower than its parent. This keeps the performed
stream and the lag function from **Attentional Blink**. The full practice
block, the second response mode and the across-block comparison are in the
longer version at `modules/cognitive/tools/05-attentional-blink/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. describe the RSVP paradigm and what the two targets are for;
2. read a lag function and say where accuracy falls and recovers;
3. say why only trials with the first target correct are counted;
4. say why catch trials are necessary;
5. say that missing the second target is not a failure of effort.

## Preparation

**Read the caution on the page before you project this one.** In the scored
block the letters change ten times a second. The background never changes and
nothing flashes or moves, but it is still rapid visual change in one spot, and
the paradigm cannot be slowed down without abolishing the effect. A
worked-example route is offered before the task and produces the same figure
from clearly labelled simulated data. Tell the room it exists before anyone
starts, not afterwards.

## Suggested use

**Individually, on students' own screens**, since the effect is about their own
timing. Then collect the shape of the curve rather than the numbers.

**Projected on the worked-example route** if you want the figure without asking
a lecture theatre to run a ten-hertz stream.

**As the second half of a session on attention**, after something spatial such
as cueing or search, so that the contrast between where and when is available.

## Prediction question

Before starting:

> The X is exactly as bright, exactly as large, and in exactly the same place
> whenever it appears. Will it matter how soon after the digit it arrives?

Most students say no, or say that arriving sooner would make it easier. Both
predictions fail, and the second one fails in an interesting way: the earliest
lag is one of the *best*, not the worst.

## Activity sequence

1. **Four practice streams at less than half speed**, with the answers shown.
   Make sure everyone understands both questions before speed is added.
2. **The block**, 31 streams, about three minutes. Say that some streams
   contain no X, so guessing yes is not free.
3. **Read the curve.** The dip should be around lag 2 or 3, which is 200 to
   300 milliseconds, and recovery by lag 8.
4. **Point at lag 1.** Accuracy there is usually high. Ask why, before giving
   the answer.
5. **Read the notes line**, which reports both the digit accuracy and the
   false-alarm count, and explain why each matters.
6. **Read the caution**, particularly the sentence about the name being a
   metaphor.

## Debrief questions

- The X was equally visible every time. What changed?
- Why do we throw away every stream where the digit was reported wrongly?
- Why does the page count how often you said yes when there was no X?
- Lag 1 is usually one of the best. What does that suggest about what the
  digit is doing?
- Your eyes were open the whole time. What sense of "blink" is meant here?

## Likely misconceptions

- **You stopped seeing for a moment.** Nothing goes dark, the eyes stay open,
  and the stream keeps being seen. The name is a metaphor and the caution says
  so.
- **The X was harder to see at some lags.** It is identical throughout.
- **Trying harder would fix it.** The finding is about people trying hard.
- **Sooner must be harder.** Lag 1 is usually spared, which is the most
  informative single point on the curve.
- **A ragged curve means the effect is not real.** A few streams per point is
  very little data. Say so, and pool the room's results if you want a clean
  curve.

## Limitations and cautions

One person and five streams per point, so every point is rough and the shape
can come out ragged or even inverted. Browser timing cannot guarantee exactly
100 ms per item. The worked-example route uses simulated data with a typical
per-lag profile and is labelled as such everywhere it appears; it should never
be presented as anyone's result. Nothing here measures attention span or
ability, and a student with a shallow dip has not demonstrated anything about
themselves.

## Accessibility considerations

The rate of change is stated before the task, not after. The worked-example
route is offered up front and is a complete alternative rather than a
consolation: it reaches every conclusion the performed route reaches, and is
labelled as simulated in the heading, the lead, the chart title, the chart
description, the table caption and the notes. Both responses are two-key, from
the keyboard or from labelled buttons, and neither is timed. Practice runs at
less than half speed. The chart description gives every value with its lag in
milliseconds and the table repeats them.

## Optional extension

Ask students to pool their lag-2 and lag-8 accuracies on the board and compare
the two class means. The dip is much clearer in aggregate than in any one
person's data, which is itself a useful lesson about how few trials a classroom
demonstration can afford.

## Evidence and citation notes

The paradigm is Raymond, Shapiro and Arnell (1992), *Temporary suppression of
visual processing in an RSVP task: An attentional blink?*, **Journal of
Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance**, 18(3), 849–860.
On lag-1 sparing and what it constrains, see Visser, Bischof and Di Lollo
(1999), *Attentional switching in spatial and nonspatial domains*,
**Psychological Bulletin**, 125(4), 458–469. For a review of the competing
accounts see Dux and Marois (2009), *The attentional blink: A review of data
and theory*, **Attention, Perception and Psychophysics**, 71(8), 1683–1700.
