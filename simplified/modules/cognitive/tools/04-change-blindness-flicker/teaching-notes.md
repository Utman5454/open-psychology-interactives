# Change Blindness — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Cognitive Psychology
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** First year, as attention and scene perception are introduced
**Edition:** Simplified, and narrower than its parent. This keeps the flicker
paradigm and the with-blank against no-blank comparison from **Change
Blindness**. The bank of scenes, the controls over change type and position,
and the run-by-run table are in the longer version at
`modules/cognitive/tools/04-change-blindness-flicker/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say what the blank does to the signal a change normally produces;
2. predict and check how much easier the change is without a blank;
3. explain why this does not show that people store nothing about a scene;
4. say that a slow search is time spent attending elsewhere;
5. say why running the same scene twice would not be a fair comparison.

## Preparation

Read the safety paragraph on the page before projecting it. The picture
alternates about 1.2 times a second, well below the rate associated with
photosensitive seizures, and nothing is bright. A manual stepping route is
offered to everyone, and it is the right route for any student who prefers it.

## Suggested use

**Projected, with the room searching out loud.** Do not tell anyone what
changes. Let it run. Someone will get it eventually, and the length of the
silence before that is the demonstration.

**Individually on students' own screens** if you want the timing to mean
anything, since a room shouting hints destroys it.

**As the opening of a session on attention**, before any theory. The experience
of failing to see something enormous is the argument.

## Prediction question

Before starting, say only this:

> One thing in this picture is about to change. It is roughly the size of a
> building. How long do you think it will take you to spot it?

Almost everyone says a second or two. The gap between that and what actually
happens is the point, and it is much stronger as a failed prediction than as a
claim.

## Activity sequence

1. **Run the flicker.** Let it go for as long as the room can stand.
2. **When a student presses the button, make them say which object** before it
   is revealed. This matters: without it, "I've got it" measures confidence.
3. **Reveal.** The changed object is ringed and named in words.
4. **Run it again without the blank**, on a different scene. It is found
   almost immediately.
5. **Compare the two times** and read the caution, particularly the sentence
   about not storing nothing.

## Debrief questions

- Why does removing the blank make it so much easier? What is the blank doing?
- You could not find it. Does that mean you were not seeing most of the scene?
- If you had been looking at the middle building when it changed, would you
  have missed it?
- Why did the second run use a different scene rather than the same one?
- What does this suggest about eyewitnesses, or about how much of a room you
  could describe after leaving it?

## Likely misconceptions

- **The change was too small or too subtle.** It is a third of a building.
  Show the two versions side by side afterwards if anyone doubts it.
- **We store almost nothing about a scene.** The strong version of this claim
  does not follow, and the caution says so. People who miss a change can often
  describe a great deal of the scene.
- **The blank hides the change.** The blank does not cover the changing object.
  It removes the change's local signal by changing everything at once.
- **This is about peripheral vision.** It happens even when the change is where
  you were looking a moment before.
- **A slow search means you were blind for that time.** You were attending
  somewhere else, one place at a time.

## Limitations and cautions

One person and two scenes. The two runs use different scenes on purpose, which
makes the comparison fairer than the usual demonstration but does not make it a
controlled experiment: the two changes are of similar size but not identical.
Treat the two times as an illustration rather than a measurement. Nothing here
measures anyone's attention, and a student who finds the change quickly with
the blank has not demonstrated anything about themselves.

## Accessibility considerations

The alternation rate and contrast are stated on the page before anything
starts. A manual stepping route is offered to everyone from the beginning and
moves only on a button press, which is also the route for anyone who has asked
for reduced motion. Nothing is timed out and a way to end the search is
available throughout. The scene description names what the scene contains, says
which version is showing, and at the reveal states in words what changed, so
the demonstration does not depend on seeing the picture. The identification
options are named parts of the scene and are marked with a glyph and a hidden
note as well as a colour.

## Optional extension

Ask students to predict where in the scene a change would be found *fastest*,
and why. The answer is wherever attention was already going, which leads
naturally into what makes something attention-grabbing in the first place.

## Evidence and citation notes

The flicker paradigm is Rensink, O'Regan and Clark (1997), *To see or not to
see: The need for attention to perceive changes in scenes*, **Psychological
Science**, 8(5), 368–373. For the argument that change blindness does not imply
sparse representation see Simons and Rensink (2005), *Change blindness: Past,
present, and future*, **Trends in Cognitive Sciences**, 9(1), 16–20.
