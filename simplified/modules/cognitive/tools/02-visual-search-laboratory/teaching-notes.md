# Visual Search Laboratory — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Cognitive Psychology
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** First or second year, once attention has been introduced
**Edition:** Simplified, and narrower than its parent. This keeps the performed
searches and the two search functions from **Visual Search Laboratory**.
Target-absent trials, the untimed inspection stage, the full practice mode and
the transfer challenge are in the longer version at
`modules/cognitive/tools/02-visual-search-laboratory/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. define feature and conjunction search by how the target is specified;
2. read a search function and state its slope in milliseconds per item;
3. say why the slope, not the mean, is the quantity of interest;
4. separate describing a search function from explaining it;
5. say what eight trials per point does to a slope estimate.

## Preparation

None. Students need two arrow keys or a pointer and about four minutes.

## Suggested use

**Everyone runs it at once**, then collect slopes rather than reaction times.
Asking "how fast were you?" gets the wrong answer to the wrong question; asking
"what did each extra item cost you?" is the whole lesson in one sentence.

**As the demonstration** in a lecture that introduces feature integration
theory, before any claim about parallel or serial processing is made.

**In a laboratory class** where students will design a search experiment. The
caution paragraph is a list of what their design would need to fix.

## Prediction question

Ask before starting, having shown one display of each type:

> In which of these two searches will having thirty-two items instead of eight
> slow you down more?

Nearly everyone gets the direction right. Then ask the harder version:

> By how much? Will the first one slow you down at all?

The answer for the single-feature search is "hardly at all", and students who
expect a proportional cost in both cases are the ones who learn most here.

## Activity sequence

1. **Six practice trials** with feedback, mixing both searches.
2. **The block runs straight on**, 48 trials, about three minutes. The line
   above the buttons says which target to look for on each trial.
3. **Read the two slopes** in the tiles before looking at the chart. They are
   the result; the chart is how you check it is not an artefact of one point.
4. **Look at the chart.** The flat line and the climbing line should be
   obvious. If a student's feature line is not flat, ask what that means: the
   answer is usually "not enough trials", which is the honest answer.
5. **Read the caution**, especially the sentence about absent trials.

## Debrief questions

- Why is the slope more informative than the average reaction time?
- What is it about the conjunction display that makes every extra item cost
  you something?
- A flat slope is often described as "parallel search". What exactly has been
  observed, and what has been inferred?
- Your feature slope came out slightly negative. What does that tell you?
- Real experiments include trials with no target at all. Why would those be
  slower, and why would the slope be steeper?

## Likely misconceptions

- **The conjunction search is harder because there are more colours.** It is
  harder because no single feature picks out the target. Point at the display:
  half the distractors share the colour, half share the orientation.
- **A flat slope proves everything was processed at once.** It is consistent
  with that and with other accounts. The takeaway says so deliberately, and
  this is the distinction between describing and explaining.
- **The slope measures how good someone's attention is.** It does not, and
  nothing here is a measure of any person.
- **Three points is enough to fit a line.** It is the minimum, and eight trials
  per point makes each of them wobbly. This is worth dwelling on with students
  who are about to design their own study.
- **Reaction time is the result.** The result is the relationship between
  reaction time and set size.

## Limitations and cautions

One person, eight trials per point, in a browser, with three points per line. A
slope estimated this way is rough and can come out negative by chance,
especially for the feature search where the true slope is near zero. Real
experiments include target-absent trials, whose slopes are steeper and usually
about twice the target-present ones; leaving those out is the largest
simplification here and it removes an important piece of evidence about search.
Browser timing adds noise of its own. Nothing here measures anyone's attention
or ability.

## Accessibility considerations

Arrow keys or two labelled buttons, and no trial is ever timed out. The
defining feature in the single-feature condition is orientation rather than
colour, so that condition does not depend on colour vision at all; the
conjunction condition needs a second dimension by definition and uses gold
against blue, which survives the common forms of colour blindness. The display
description names the target in words on every trial and says which half it is
in. The two search functions differ by marker shape and line dash as well as
colour and are named in a key with a sample of each. The chart description
gives every mean and both slopes, and the table repeats them. Nothing animates.

## Optional extension

Ask students how they would design a display in which the conjunction search
became flat. Guided search predicts it becomes shallower as the distractors
become more distinguishable, and the discussion is a good route into why the
original strict dichotomy did not survive.

## Evidence and citation notes

Feature integration theory is Treisman and Gelade (1980), *A feature-
integration theory of attention*, **Cognitive Psychology**, 12(1), 97–136. For
why the flat-versus-steep dichotomy is better treated as a continuum, see Wolfe
(1998), *What can 1 million trials tell us about visual search?*,
**Psychological Science**, 9(1), 33–39, and Wolfe (2021), *Guided Search 6.0*,
**Psychonomic Bulletin and Review**, 28, 1060–1092.
