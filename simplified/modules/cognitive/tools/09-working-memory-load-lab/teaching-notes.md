# Working-Memory Load Laboratory — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Cognitive Psychology
**Duration:** 6–8 minutes
**Level:** Second year, on working memory
**Edition:** Simplified, and narrower than its parent. This keeps the performed
load task and the load-by-domain factorial from **Working-Memory Load
Laboratory**. The third load level, the fuller practice mode and the extended
treatment of separable components are in the longer version at
`modules/cognitive/tools/09-working-memory-load-lab/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. describe a concurrent-task design and say what the retention interval does;
2. say why the *domain* of the concurrent task matters, not just its presence;
3. read an interaction between load and domain off two lines;
4. say what a single pool of general effort would predict;
5. name unmatched difficulty and small cells as limits here.

## Preparation

None, but say two things out loud before students start. First, no writing
anything down: holding the letters is the whole task. Second, the in-between
judgements are paced and will move on without them, which is deliberate,
because a filler task you can rush through is not a load.

## Suggested use

**Individually**, then collect the four percentages on the board and draw the
two lines for the room. The interaction is much clearer in aggregate.

**In the lecture that introduces the working-memory model**, so that separable
components arrive as an inference from an interaction rather than as a diagram
to memorise.

## Prediction question

Before the block:

> In between the letters and the recall you will be kept busy for about six
> seconds either way. Does it matter what you are kept busy *with*?

The intuitive answer is no: busy is busy. If working memory were one pot of
effort, that answer would be right, and the fact that it is usually wrong is
what the model was built to explain.

## Activity sequence

1. **Two practice trials** with the answers shown.
2. **The block**, 20 trials, about five minutes.
3. **Read the two lines.** Both should fall with load. The one for letters
   in between should usually fall further.
4. **Ask what a single-pot account predicts**, then point at the gap.
5. **Read the caution**, particularly the unmatched-difficulty point, which is
   the honest weakness of any demonstration of this kind.

## Debrief questions

- Both in-between tasks kept you busy for the same length of time. Why did one
  cost more?
- If working memory were one general pool, what would the two lines look like?
- The grid grows with the load, so chance is 50 per cent either way. Why does
  that matter?
- One in-between task might simply be harder than the other. How would you
  rule that out?
- Your two lines came out on top of each other. What should you conclude?

## Likely misconceptions

- **Any distraction costs the same.** This is exactly what the design tests
  and usually refutes.
- **The verbal task is harder, so of course it costs more.** A real
  possibility, not a dismissal, and the caution raises it before a student
  does. The proper answer is a difficulty-matched pair, which is hard to build.
- **Separable components means separate places in the brain.** It means
  material of one kind interferes more with material of the same kind. That is
  a functional claim.
- **A crossed pair of lines disproves the model.** With five trials per cell it
  is what noise looks like.
- **Recall accuracy measures memory span.** Nothing here is a span measure and
  nothing is a norm.

## Limitations and cautions

One person, five trials per cell, so each point is rough and the two lines can
cross the wrong way by chance. The two in-between tasks are matched for pacing
and for number of judgements but not for difficulty, so part of any difference
could be that one is simply harder; this is the largest weakness and the page
says so. The recall grid scales with load so that chance stays at 50 per cent,
which removes a confound but does not make five trials into many. Nothing here
measures anyone's memory or ability.

## Accessibility considerations

Every response is a labelled button and the whole activity is keyboard
operable. The display description names the letters to hold, states the
in-between question in words, and at recall says how many letters to pick and
how many are in the grid. Recall picks carry aria-pressed as well as a colour
and a glyph, and can be undone. Only the retention judgements are paced, which
is stated in the setup note; nothing else is timed out. The chart distinguishes
the two lines by marker shape and dash as well as colour, names them in a key
with a sample of each, and carries a description giving all four points.

## Optional extension

Ask students to design the difficulty-matched pair of in-between tasks that
would answer the objection in the caution. It is much harder than it sounds,
and working out why is a better lesson in experimental design than most
worked examples.

## Evidence and citation notes

The multi-component model is Baddeley and Hitch (1974), *Working memory*, in
**The Psychology of Learning and Motivation**, volume 8, and Baddeley (2000),
*The episodic buffer*, **Trends in Cognitive Sciences**, 4(11), 417–423. The
selective-interference logic used here is from that tradition; for a critical
view of domain-specific storage see Cowan (2001), *The magical number 4 in
short-term memory*, **Behavioral and Brain Sciences**, 24(1), 87–114.
