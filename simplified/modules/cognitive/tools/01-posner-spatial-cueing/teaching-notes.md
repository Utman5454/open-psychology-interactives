# Posner Spatial Cueing — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Cognitive Psychology
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** First year, as attention is introduced
**Edition:** Simplified, and narrower than its parent. This keeps the performed
experiment and the neutral condition from **Posner Spatial Cueing**. The second
cue-to-target interval, the running table of every block, the worked example
and the transfer challenge are in the longer version at
`modules/cognitive/tools/01-posner-spatial-cueing/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. describe the cueing paradigm and what each trial type is for;
2. split a cueing effect into a benefit and a cost using a neutral baseline;
3. say why a valid-minus-invalid difference hides which half produced it;
4. distinguish covert orienting from moving the eyes;
5. name browser timing, anticipation cut-offs and small cell sizes as limits.

## Preparation

None. Students need two arrow keys or a pointer, and about four minutes.

## Suggested use

**Everyone runs it at once on their own screens**, then you collect a few
results verbally. Ask specifically for the three means rather than for "the
effect", because the whole point is that there are two numbers, not one.

**As the opening five minutes** of a lecture on selective attention. The
phenomenon is felt before it is named, which is worth more than a slide.

**Before a laboratory class** in which students design a cueing study of their
own; the caution paragraph is effectively a list of what their design would
have to fix.

## Prediction question

Ask before anyone starts:

> The cue is just a box brightening, and you are told to keep your eyes on the
> cross the whole time. Will it make any difference to how fast you respond?

Many students say no, on the grounds that you cannot look at it. That is
exactly the intuition the activity exists to break, and it is a much better
starting point than being told what covert orienting is.

## Activity sequence

1. **Six practice trials** with feedback, to establish the response mapping.
2. **The block runs straight on** from practice, 72 trials, about three
   minutes, with no feedback at all. Say why beforehand: seeing how you are
   doing changes how you answer the next trial.
3. **Read the result chart.** Both bars are drawn outward from the student's
   own neutral mean, so the benefit and the cost are visibly different lengths.
4. **Ask for the valid-minus-invalid number**, then ask which half of it came
   from the benefit. Most students cannot say until they look at the neutral
   bar, which is the point.
5. **Read the caution together**, particularly the sentence about fixation.

## Debrief questions

- Your eyes did not move. Why did the cue help at all?
- Which was larger for you, the benefit or the cost? Why might a cost be
  larger than a benefit?
- Why does the cue have to be right most of the time for this to work?
- Which of your three means is least trustworthy, and why?
- What would a study need in order to be sure you were not just looking at the
  cued box?

## Likely misconceptions

- **Attention is where the eyes are.** The whole activity is a counterexample,
  and the prediction question is designed to surface the belief first.
- **A cueing effect is one number.** It is two, and they usually differ. This
  is the reason the neutral condition is not optional.
- **The cue works because it is informative, so the effect is a strategy.**
  Partly, at this interval. A very short interval and an uninformative cue
  produce a similar effect, which is where the longer version goes.
- **A bigger effect means better attention.** Nothing here measures anyone's
  ability, and the caution says so plainly.
- **Holding fixation was controlled.** It was requested. That is not the same
  thing, and students designing their own study should be made to feel the
  difference.

## Limitations and cautions

One person, one short block, in a browser. Only twelve of the seventy-two
trials are invalid, because the cue has to be predictive for the effect to
exist, so that mean rests on the fewest trials and is the least stable figure
on the page. Browser timing adds noise of its own and cannot be relied on to
the millisecond. Nothing checks where the student was actually looking. A
result near zero, or the wrong way round, is a result about the block rather
than about the student, and no number here says anything about anyone's
attention or ability.

## Accessibility considerations

Both arrow keys and two labelled buttons work, and each button names the key it
duplicates. No trial is ever timed out. The display carries a description that
updates with the trial and names the side the target appeared on, so the task
can be followed without seeing the boxes. The response keys are disabled until
a target is showing, so an accidental press cannot be recorded. The result
chart carries a description giving all three means and both effects, and every
value appears again in a table. Nothing fades, moves or animates, so there is
nothing for a reduced-motion rule to switch off. Each stage is announced
politely.

## Optional extension

Ask students to work out how many invalid trials they would need for a mean
they would trust, given the spread they can see in their own data, and then how
long a block that would take. It is usually a sobering number, and it explains
why real cueing studies run for an hour.

## Evidence and citation notes

The paradigm is Posner (1980), *Orienting of attention*, **Quarterly Journal of
Experimental Psychology**, 32(1), 3–25. The benefit-and-cost logic, and the
role of the neutral condition, are set out there. On the cost of invalid cues
as a disengagement problem see Posner, Walker, Friedrich and Rafal (1984),
*Effects of parietal injury on covert orienting of attention*, **Journal of
Neuroscience**, 4(7), 1863–1874.
