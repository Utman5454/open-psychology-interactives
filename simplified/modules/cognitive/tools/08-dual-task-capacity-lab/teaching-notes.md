# Dual-Task Capacity Laboratory — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Cognitive Psychology
**Duration:** 6–8 minutes
**Level:** Second year, on divided attention
**Edition:** Simplified, and narrower than its parent. This keeps the performed
blocks and the enforced order from **Dual-Task and Limited Capacity
Laboratory**. The difficulty manipulation, the response-overlap manipulation
and the block-by-block record are in the longer version at
`modules/cognitive/tools/08-dual-task-capacity-lab/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say why a dual-task cost needs a single-task baseline;
2. recognise that two easy tasks still interfere;
3. recognise that the cost is usually shared unequally;
4. reframe "can you do two things at once" as "what does it cost";
5. name fixed block order and browser timing as limits here.

## Preparation

None. Students need four keys or a pointer and about four minutes. The three
blocks run in a fixed order automatically; there is nothing to configure.

## Suggested use

**Individually**, then collect the two costs on the board. The spread across
the room is large and the *asymmetry* is the interesting part: ask who paid
mostly on the letter task and who paid mostly on the side task.

**In a lecture on working memory or attention**, as the demonstration that
capacity limits are not about difficulty.

**Before any discussion of phones and driving**, so that the last sentence of
the caution can be read out with the data in front of everyone.

## Prediction question

Before block 3:

> You have now done both of these tasks on their own and neither is hard.
> When you do both at once, how much slower do you expect to be?

Students routinely say "a bit" or "not much". Costs of 200 to 400 ms on tasks
that take 400 ms alone are common, and the size of that is the surprise.

## Activity sequence

1. **Block 1, letters alone.** Sixteen trials.
2. **Block 2, sides alone.** Sixteen trials. It runs on automatically.
3. **Block 3, both at once.** Twenty trials. Both stimuli appear together and
   both need answering, in whichever order the student likes.
4. **Read the chart.** Pale bar is alone, solid bar is together, and the gap is
   the cost.
5. **Compare the two costs** and ask which task they were protecting. Most
   students can tell you, and it usually matches the smaller cost.
6. **Read the caution**, especially the fixed-order limitation and the last
   sentence.

## Debrief questions

- Neither task got harder. Why did both get slower?
- Which task did you protect, and did you decide to?
- Why does the page insist on measuring each block on its own first?
- The blocks always run in the same order. What does that do to the cost, and
  in which direction?
- Does any of this tell you whether someone can safely use a phone while
  driving?

## Likely misconceptions

- **The tasks became harder.** They are identical in both blocks. Only the
  context changed.
- **The cost is shared evenly.** It rarely is, and the asymmetry is the more
  interesting half of the finding.
- **A dual-task cost proves a single central bottleneck.** It is consistent
  with several accounts; separating them needs the response-overlap
  manipulation, which is in the longer version.
- **Being good at multitasking would remove the cost.** Nothing here measures
  anyone, and the caution refuses that reading explicitly.
- **This generalises to driving.** Those tasks are far less trivial and run for
  far longer. Say so.

## Limitations and cautions

One person, a short block of each, in a browser. The three blocks always run in
the same order, so any practice effect over the session is mixed into the
dual-task cost and would make it look *smaller* than it is; a proper design
counterbalances the order across people. Browser timing adds noise. A cost near
zero or a negative one is a result about the block. Nothing here says anything
about driving, phones or anyone's ability to multitask.

## Accessibility considerations

Four arrow keys or four labelled buttons, each naming the key it duplicates,
and no trial is timed out. Only the keypads a block needs are shown, and keys
are disabled until a stimulus is present. On a dual trial, answering one task
disables that keypad and leaves the other live, so the state of the trial is
visible rather than remembered. The display description names what is showing.
The chart distinguishes alone from together by fill against outline as well as
by position and carries a description giving all four means and both costs.
Where cleaning leaves too few usable trials the chart and table are hidden
rather than drawn empty.

## Optional extension

Ask students to run it again deliberately protecting the *other* task, and
compare. The total cost tends to stay similar while its distribution moves,
which is a much more convincing demonstration of a shared limit than any
single run.

## Evidence and citation notes

For the general framework see Kahneman (1973), *Attention and Effort*, and for
the response bottleneck account see Pashler (1994), *Dual-task interference in
simple tasks: Data and theory*, **Psychological Bulletin**, 116(2), 220–244. On
why performance costs do not license claims about individual multitasking
ability, see Watson and Strayer (2010), *Supertaskers: Profiles in
extraordinary multitasking ability*, **Psychonomic Bulletin and Review**,
17(4), 479–485.
