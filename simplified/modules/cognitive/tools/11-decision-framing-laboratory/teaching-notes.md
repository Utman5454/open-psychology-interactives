# Decision Framing Laboratory — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Cognitive Psychology
**Duration:** 4–6 minutes
**Level:** First year, on judgement and decision making
**Edition:** Simplified, and narrower than its parent. This keeps the four
withheld decisions and the paired reveal from **Decision Framing Laboratory**.
The certainty manipulation, the reference-point shift and the further
scenarios are in the longer version at
`modules/cognitive/tools/11-decision-framing-laboratory/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say that identical outcomes described two ways can produce opposite
   choices;
2. identify the reference point a description sets up;
3. state the usual direction: cautious about gains, willing to gamble on
   losses;
4. say why the equivalence has to be withheld;
5. say why consistency is not superior reasoning.

## Preparation

None, and say nothing about framing beforehand. If the room knows what is
coming, the second version of each problem is answered to match the first and
there is nothing to see.

## Suggested use

**Individually and in silence**, then a show of hands: who chose the certain
option in decision 1, and who chose the gamble in decision 3. Those are the
same numbers. The split in the room is the result.

**As the opening of a lecture on prospect theory**, so the value function
arrives as an explanation of something already experienced.

**In a research-methods session on wording**, where the last sentence of the
caution is the real lesson.

## Prediction question

There is no useful prediction to ask beforehand, because asking one would give
the game away. Ask afterwards instead:

> If I had told you at the start that two of these were the same decision
> twice, would you have answered differently?

Almost everyone says yes, which is precisely why the equivalence is withheld,
and it makes the design decision intelligible rather than arbitrary.

## Activity sequence

1. **Four decisions, one at a time.** Nothing is explained and nothing is
   scored. Tell students to answer as they actually would.
2. **The reveal**, pairing the two versions of each problem side by side with
   both full texts visible.
3. **Read the numbers aloud** from one pair: 200 saved of 600 against 400 lost
   of 600.
4. **Read the takeaway**, then the caution.

## Debrief questions

- In the pair you switched on, what actually differed between the two
  versions?
- Where did each description invite you to start counting from?
- Is switching irrational? What rule would it be breaking?
- You did not switch at all. Does that mean you reasoned better?
- A hospital leaflet says a procedure has a 90 per cent survival rate. What
  else could it have said, and would that be dishonest?

## Likely misconceptions

- **The two versions differ somewhere I missed.** They do not, and the reveal
  puts both texts side by side so this can be checked rather than asserted.
- **Switching is an error.** There is no rule of rationality requiring two
  descriptions of the same outcome to feel the same. The caution says so.
- **Consistency shows better reasoning.** It shows this particular wording did
  not move you. Nothing more.
- **This means people are irrational.** The useful conclusion is about
  descriptions, not about people, and it applies to whoever writes them.
- **The effect is about maths ability.** It appears in people who can do the
  arithmetic perfectly well.

## Limitations and cautions

Four decisions from one person is a demonstration and not a measurement: the
effect is a tendency across groups and any individual can be perfectly
consistent. The scenarios are invented and describe no real event. Nothing here
is scored and nothing measures anyone. The most useful takeaway is not about
the student at all: it is that anyone writing a description of risk, in a
policy brief, a consent form or a news report, is making a choice whether they
intend to or not.

## Accessibility considerations

Every option is a labelled button, keyboard operable, and nothing is timed.
There is no motion. The reveal is built from real elements rather than markup
strings, and each card distinguishes a switched pair from an unswitched one by
a hidden note as well as by colour. Both framings appear in full text in the
reveal, so the comparison does not depend on remembering what was asked
earlier.

## Optional extension

Give students a real public-health message and ask them to rewrite it in the
opposite frame without changing a single number. Then ask which version they
would send, and why, and whether that answer is a communication decision or an
ethical one.

## Evidence and citation notes

The paradigm is Tversky and Kahneman (1981), *The framing of decisions and the
psychology of choice*, **Science**, 211(4481), 453–458; the scenarios here are
invented rather than reproduced. The underlying account is Kahneman and Tversky
(1979), *Prospect theory: An analysis of decision under risk*,
**Econometrica**, 47(2), 263–291. For how large the effect actually is across
studies see Kühberger (1998), *The influence of framing on risky decisions*,
**Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes**, 75(1), 23–55, which
reports a considerably more modest average than the original demonstration
suggests.
