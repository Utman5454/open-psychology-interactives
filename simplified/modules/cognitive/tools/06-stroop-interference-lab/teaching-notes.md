# Stroop Interference Laboratory — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Cognitive Psychology
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes, of which about two are the trials themselves
**Level:** First year upwards
**Edition:** Simplified. The longer version adds a second colour set, a second
trial mix so the effect can be moved by changing the context, a table of every
block run, a transfer challenge and a fully documented simulated route. It is
at `modules/cognitive/tools/06-stroop-interference-lab/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. describe colour-word interference from having felt it, not from a summary;
2. explain why a neutral condition is needed, and what is lost without it;
3. distinguish interference from facilitation, and say which baseline both are
   measured from;
4. read a difference score as the distance between two condition means;
5. say why a block like this cannot measure anything about a person.

## Preparation

None. It runs in a browser with no account, no network and no setup.

**Before you set it, read the access note below.** The task requires telling
four ink colours apart. In the simplified edition there is no two-colour
version and no simulated route; both are in the longer version, and a student
who cannot do this task should be sent there instead.

## Suggested use

**Projected, run by you, once.** Do the practice trials on the projector,
then run the block yourself while the room watches the words. Reveal your
result and let them predict the direction before you scroll.

**As preparation**, set individually before a session on automaticity or
cognitive control. Ask students to bring their two numbers, and put the range
of the room's interference scores on the board. The spread is the lesson: it
is wide, and it is mostly noise.

**As a five-minute opener**, then straight into the question the activity
deliberately does not answer: why does the word get in at all, when nobody
asked for it to be read?

## Prediction question

Before students open it, ask:

> You will name the ink colour of coloured words. Some words will be colour
> names that match the ink, some will conflict with it, and some will be
> ordinary nouns. Where will you be slowest, and where will you be fastest?

Almost everyone predicts the cost. Very few predict a benefit, and that is
the point of the neutral condition: with it there are two numbers, not one.
There is no prediction panel in the activity itself, so this question is
yours to ask.

## Activity sequence

1. **Six untimed practice trials**, each with feedback. Nothing is scored.
2. **45 scored trials**, 15 per condition, in a random order. No feedback of
   any kind until the block ends. Any trial can be taken as slowly as needed;
   nothing is ever timed out.
3. **The result**, withheld until now: three condition means on one scale,
   with the neutral mean marked and both effects drawn from it in opposite
   directions.

## Debrief questions

- Which was your larger effect, and by how much?
- Suppose the activity had only congruent and incongruent trials. What single
  number would you have, and what could you no longer say?
- Facilitation is usually the smaller of the two, and often absent. Why might
  a benefit be harder to produce than a cost?
- You were told to ignore the word. Why did that not work?
- Your neighbour has a larger interference score than you. What does that
  tell you about the two of you?

## The moment worth stopping on

The last question. It should get an uncomfortable pause, and the honest
answer is *almost nothing*: 15 trials per condition, a browser clock, one
session, one person, no practice effect controlled, no counterbalancing. A
difference score is the distance between two means in one block, not a
property of a person, and treating it as a trait is the single most common
misreading of this task.

## Likely misconceptions

**"The Stroop effect is one number."** It is at least two, and they come from
different places. Reporting only incongruent minus congruent adds them
together and hides both.

**"Automaticity explains it."** Saying reading is automatic re-describes the
result. What would count as evidence for automaticity beyond the effect it is
being used to explain? Worth asking; the simplified version does not answer it.

**"A bigger effect means worse attention."** No. It is a difference between
two of your own means on one occasion, and a large part of it is noise.

**"I could beat it if I concentrated."** Let a confident student try. The
effect is robust to intention, which is the interesting part.

**"Facilitation should be as big as interference."** It usually is not, and in
a block this short it often comes out at zero or the wrong way round. Say so
before students compare scores, or a null result will be read as a personal
failure.

## Limitations and cautions

- One person, one short block, in a browser. Fifteen scored trials per
  condition is enough to feel the effect and far too few to estimate anyone's
  reaction time.
- Browser timing is not laboratory timing. Display and input latency vary by
  machine and add noise that is not in the participant.
- The neutral words are common concrete nouns roughly matched in length to
  the colour words. That is approximate. A properly matched neutral set would
  control frequency and orthographic neighbourhood too, and the difference
  between "roughly matched" and "matched" is exactly the kind of thing that
  can produce or remove a small effect.
- Responses under 200 ms or over 3000 ms are excluded from the means and
  reported on the page. Errors are excluded from the means and reported as
  accuracy. Both decisions are defensible and both are choices; a student who
  asks what happens under different bounds is asking a good question.
- Nothing here measures attention, control, or any characteristic of the
  person doing it.

## Accessibility

Every response is a full-size key naming its colour in text, with the number
key printed on it; keys 1 to 4 do the same job for anyone working from the
keyboard. No trial is ever timed out, so a learner may take as long as they
need on any word without losing the trial. Nothing flashes, fades, moves or
animates, so there is nothing for a reduced-motion setting to switch off. The
task panel is dark and constant, and every ink clears 7:1 against it. In the
result, each mean is printed beside its bar, both effects are named with their
values, and the figure carries a description stating all three means and both
effects; the table gives every value including accuracy and excluded trials.

**The task does require discriminating four ink colours.** That is inherent to
the phenomenon and cannot be removed without removing it. The simplified
edition has no alternative route: send a student who needs one to the longer
version, which offers a two-colour set differing in brightness as well as hue,
and a fully documented simulated block that reaches every conclusion without
responding to a single colour.

## Extension

Ask students what they would need to add to decide whether the word is read
*before* the colour is named or *while* it is being named. Most proposals turn
out to need something the reaction time alone cannot give, which is the honest
place to leave it.

## Evidence and reading

Stroop's original 1935 report, and the large subsequent literature on
interference, facilitation and cognitive control. Point students at reviews of
that literature rather than at this activity: it is a way of experiencing the
effect and seeing it decomposed, not a summary of what is known about it.
