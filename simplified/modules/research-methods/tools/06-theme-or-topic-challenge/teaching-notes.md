# Theme or Topic? — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Research Methods · **Duration:** 4–5 minutes · **Level:** Second year upwards
**Edition:** Simplified. The longer version has three clusters, twelve
candidates and a rewrite challenge. It is at
`modules/research-methods/tools/06-theme-or-topic-challenge/`.

## Learning objectives

Students should be able to distinguish a topic summary from a developed theme;
recognise a true descriptive sentence as a staging post; identify the
organising concept that makes a theme analytic; and name which feature of the
design an overreaching statement exceeds.

## Preparation

None. It uses the same invented dataset as the coding laboratory and the
reflexivity builder, so the three can be run in sequence.

## Suggested use

**Immediately before students write up a qualitative project.** The staging
post is the commonest thing handed in as a theme.

**Projected, taking a vote on each candidate.** Candidate 1 is where the room
divides.

## Prediction question

> "Students feel anxious about asking questions in seminars." Is that a theme?

Most say yes. It is true, supported, and explains nothing.

## Activity sequence

Four candidates, one at a time, sorted into topic summary, staging post,
developed theme, or beyond what the data carry. The categories appear in a
fixed order, so the position of the answer varies rather than being learnable,
and each candidate is judged before the next is seen, so none can be solved by
elimination.

## Debrief questions

- What does a reader learn from "Asking for help"?
- Candidate 1 is true. What can you do with it that you could not do before?
- What single concept is doing the work in the developed theme?
- Candidate 3 fails twice. What are the two failures?
- Lena was not anxious. Which candidates survive that, and which do not?

## The moment worth stopping on

Lena. The developed theme says nothing about anxiety, which is why it survives
a participant who was not anxious. The staging post does not. That is the
clearest available demonstration that an organising concept is doing something
a summary of mood cannot.

## Likely misconceptions

**"A theme is what the extracts are about."** That is a topic.

**"If it is true and supported it is a theme."** That is a staging post, and a
great deal of published work stops there.

**"Themes emerge from the data."** They are constructed by an analyst using a
concept, which is why two analysts produce different ones.

**"Overreach means being wrong."** Candidate 3 might even be true. The problem
is that this design cannot establish it.

## Limitations and cautions

Four categories is a teaching simplification: real candidates sit on a
continuum and several could be argued into a neighbouring category by a good
supervisor. The developed theme offered is defensible rather than uniquely
correct. The extracts are invented and no participant, interview or study is
represented.

## Accessibility

Keyboard operable throughout. Answered options stay in the tab order and can
be re-read, and each state carries a glyph and a hidden sentence as well as a
colour. The cluster stays on screen throughout. No timed content and no motion.

## Extension

Ask students to rewrite candidate 1 as a theme by supplying an organising
concept, then to check it against Lena. That is the rewrite challenge from the
longer version and it works well on paper.

## Evidence and reading

Braun and Clarke on topic summaries versus themes, which is where this
distinction is argued most directly.
