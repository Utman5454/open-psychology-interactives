# The Same Mark, Two Different Distributions — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Research Methods
**Duration:** 4–6 minutes
**Level:** First year, or any point where z-scores are being introduced
**Edition:** Simplified, and deliberately narrower than its parent. This is the
first experiment of **The Normal Curve and z-Scores**. The second experiment
placing one mark on two distributions at once, the extended one-tailed against
two-tailed treatment, and the read-the-curve challenge set are in the longer
version at `modules/research-methods/tools/13-normal-curve-z-scores/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say what the mean and the standard deviation each do to a normal curve;
2. compute a z-score and read off the percentile and tail areas;
3. explain why the same raw mark can be ordinary in one cohort and remarkable
   in another;
4. say why standardising relabels the axis rather than changing anything;
5. explain why 68, 95 and 99.7 are facts about a model, not about data.

## Preparation

None, and no seed to quote: the curve is a function, not a sample, so every
student sees exactly the same figure at the same settings.

## Suggested use

**Projected, in the first five minutes of the lecture that introduces z.** Set
the mark to 62 on a mean of 50 with a spread of 10, then drag the spread down
to 4 without touching anything else. The mark has not moved. The cohort has not
got worse. The same 62 is now at the very top. That is the entire concept and
it takes forty seconds.

**As preparation** before a session on standardised assessment or test norms.

**In a statistics workshop**, using the three built-in challenges, which set
the controls and name what to watch rather than asking for an answer.

## Prediction question

Ask before anything is dragged:

> A student scores 62. Is that good?

Let the room argue. Someone will ask what the average was. Someone else will
ask how spread out the marks were. Those two questions are the z-score, and it
is much better for students to have asked them than to be handed the formula.

## Activity sequence

1. **Read the figure at the default settings.** Locate the mark, the mean, the
   shaded area, and the two axes. Make sure everyone sees that the lower axis
   is the *same* axis, relabelled.
2. **Hold the mark and drag the spread** from 10 down to 4. Watch the
   percentile climb from 88.5 to 99.9. Note also that the curve got taller,
   which is worth a sentence: the area has to stay at one.
3. **Reset, then drag the mean** from 50 up to 74. The same 62 is now below
   average. Nothing about the student changed.
4. **Check the landmarks.** Put the mark one standard deviation above the mean
   and read 84.1; two above and read 97.7. The 68 and 95 figures are visible in
   the *as extreme either way* tile at those settings.
5. **Release the explanation** and read the caution about skew and ceilings.

## Debrief questions

- Two universities both report that a student is at the 90th percentile. Do
  they mean the same thing?
- Why does widening a distribution make the curve shorter as well as wider?
- A department wants to convert marks to z-scores so that modules are
  comparable. What does that fix, and what does it not fix?
- Coursework marks are usually bunched near the top with a hard ceiling at 100.
  Which of the numbers on this page would then be wrong, and in which
  direction?

## Likely misconceptions

- **A high raw score is a good score.** The activity exists to break this.
- **The z-score depends only on the mean.** Step 2 changes only the spread and
  moves z further than step 3 does.
- **Density is probability.** The height of the curve is not a probability;
  the area is. Widening the curve lowers the height precisely because of this,
  which is why the density axis here is fixed rather than rescaled.
- **The 68-95-99.7 rule is a fact about data.** It is a fact about the normal
  model. Real marks with a ceiling do not obey it.
- **Standardising normalises a skewed distribution.** It does not. It
  subtracts and divides; it does not change the shape at all.

## Limitations and cautions

Every percentile and tail area here is read off a normal curve, so all of them
are properties of that model rather than of any real set of marks. Where a
distribution is skewed or has a ceiling, which coursework marks reliably do,
these numbers are wrong and so are the familiar landmarks. Say so. A z-score
also says only where a score sits, not whether the measure was any good: a z of
+2 on an unreliable test is still a score on an unreliable test. Nothing here
is a norm, a grade boundary or a cut-off.

## Accessibility considerations

All three controls are native range inputs reporting their value in text. The
figure description gives the mean, the spread, the mark, its distance from the
mean in standard deviations and both tail areas, so a student using a screen
reader has every quantity a sighted reader has. The mark and the mean are
distinguished by position, weight and an explicit on-figure label, not by
colour alone, and both labels carry a halo so they remain legible over the
curve. On a narrow screen the figure scrolls sideways rather than shrinking its
labels below legibility. Changes are announced politely. No timed content and
no motion.

## Optional extension

Ask each student to bring the mean and standard deviation of one of their own
module marks, enter them, and place their own mark. Doing this on the projector
is a bad idea; doing it privately on their own screens is a good one, and the
page stores and transmits nothing, which is worth telling them explicitly.

## Evidence and citation notes

The material is the standard normal model and the z transformation, covered in
any introductory statistics text. On the limits of applying normal-curve
percentiles to real assessment data, see Bond and Fox (2015), *Applying the
Rasch Model*, chapter 1, or any treatment of ceiling effects in educational
measurement.
