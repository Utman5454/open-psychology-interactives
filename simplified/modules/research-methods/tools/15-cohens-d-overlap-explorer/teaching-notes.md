# How Much Do Two Groups Overlap? — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Research Methods
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** Second year and above, once means and standard deviations are secure
**Edition:** Simplified, and narrower than its parent. This keeps the
prediction hook and the live two-curve figure from **Cohen's d and
Distributional Overlap**. The sample-size control that separates an effect size
from a test statistic, the four-index matching challenge and the
published-effect presets are in the longer version at
`modules/research-methods/tools/15-cohens-d-overlap-explorer/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. compute Cohen's d and say why it has no units;
2. translate an effect size into overlap, superiority and U3;
3. say how much two groups still overlap at a "large" effect;
4. explain why two unrelated studies can produce the identical d;
5. say what a group difference licenses about an individual, which is very
   little.

## Preparation

None, and no seed: the curves are functions, so every student sees the same
figure at the same settings.

## Suggested use

**Projected, and the prediction matters.** Do not skip it. Ask the room to
commit to a number before anything is shown. Most classes cluster on 2 or 8.
The answer is 21, and the surprise is the point: a "large" effect leaves the
two distributions sharing about 69 per cent of their area.

**In a session on reporting standards**, where students are being told to
report effect sizes alongside p-values. This explains why.

**Immediately before students read a paper** that describes an effect as large.

## Prediction question

The activity asks it directly:

> Two groups differ by what psychologists call a large effect: Cohen's d of
> 0.8. Out of a hundred people in the lower group, how many score above the
> average of the higher group?

Take a show of hands on each option before anyone clicks. The public commitment
is what makes the reveal land.

## Activity sequence

1. **Guess, then lock it in.** The figure is withheld until this is done.
2. **Read the four tiles.** Establish that all four describe the same picture.
3. **Drag the difference to zero.** Overlap goes to 100 per cent, superiority
   to 50 per cent: two identical distributions. Then drag it to the maximum and
   note that even at d = 2.0 nearly a third of the area is still shared.
4. **Press "Show me the same d twice".** Three studies with nothing in common
   produce not a similar figure but the identical one. This is what "no units"
   means, and it is why d can be pooled across studies in a meta-analysis.
5. **Release the explanation** and read the caution about Cohen's labels.

## Debrief questions

- If a large effect still leaves 69 per cent overlap, what would an effect
  have to look like for you to make a confident prediction about one person?
- A paper reports d = 0.3 and calls it small. Under what circumstances would
  you disagree?
- Why does an effect size not get bigger when you test more people, while a
  t-statistic does?
- Two studies report d = 0.5. One measured reaction time in milliseconds, the
  other wellbeing on a 7-point scale. Is comparing them legitimate?

## Likely misconceptions

- **A large effect means the groups barely overlap.** The central
  misconception, and the reason the prediction comes first.
- **An effect size tells you whether the effect is real.** It does not. It
  describes a difference; whether that difference is more than noise is a
  separate question, and one this activity deliberately does not answer.
- **Cohen's small, medium and large are benchmarks.** Cohen offered them as a
  fallback for researchers with nothing better and said so. The tiles here
  label them "Cohen's rough" for exactly this reason.
- **A group difference predicts an individual.** At d = 0.8 you would be wrong
  nearly three times in ten guessing which of two people scored higher.
- **d depends on the measurement scale.** It does not, which is both its great
  advantage and the reason a d divorced from its context is uninterpretable.

## Limitations and cautions

Every figure here assumes both groups are normal with the same standard
deviation, which is what makes the four indices agree with one another. Where
the spreads differ, d depends on which standard deviation you divide by, and
the overlap no longer follows from d alone; that is why the activity offers one
spread control rather than two. Cohen's labels are conventions, not thresholds,
and what counts as a consequential effect depends on the outcome and the cost
of acting on it: a d of 0.1 on mortality is enormous and a d of 0.8 on a
laboratory task may mean nothing outside the laboratory. Nothing here is a
published effect or a norm.

## Accessibility considerations

The prediction options carry a hidden state note as well as a colour and a
glyph, so a marked answer never depends on colour. The two curves differ by
dash pattern, and each label sits on its own curve's outer flank so the two
never collide even when the means coincide. The figure description gives the
difference, the spread and all four indices. On a narrow screen the figure
scrolls sideways rather than shrinking its labels below legibility. No timed
content and no motion.

## Optional extension

Give students a d from a paper on their reading list and ask them to set the
sliders to reproduce it, then to write one sentence describing the finding in
terms of overlap rather than in terms of d. The sentences are usually much more
cautious than the paper's own.

## Evidence and citation notes

Cohen (1988), *Statistical Power Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences*, 2nd
edition, is the source both of d and of the small, medium and large
conventions, and of his own warning about using them. For the overlap and
superiority translations see McGraw and Wong (1992), *A common language effect
size statistic*, **Psychological Bulletin**, 111(2), 361–365. On why the
conventions are a poor guide in practice see Funder and Ozer (2019),
*Evaluating effect size in psychological research*, **Advances in Methods and
Practices in Psychological Science**, 2(2), 156–168.
