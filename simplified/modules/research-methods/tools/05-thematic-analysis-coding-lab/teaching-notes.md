# Thematic Analysis Coding Laboratory — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Research Methods · **Duration:** 4–5 minutes · **Level:** Second year upwards
**Edition:** Simplified. The longer version has six extracts, a bank of
candidate codes with a note on what each commits you to, and a reflexive
prompt after every extract. It is at
`modules/research-methods/tools/05-thematic-analysis-coding-lab/`.

## Learning objectives

Students should be able to describe coding as an analytic act; explain why
competent analysts differ; distinguish semantic, latent and critical readings;
say what a reading brings forward and what it costs; and explain why
inter-rater agreement is not the criterion here.

## Preparation

None. Nothing typed is stored or transmitted.

## Suggested use

**Individually, before a seminar on coding.** Ask students to bring their own
code and read a few aloud without comment. The variety is the lesson.

**Projected, with the room writing a code on paper first.** Do not collect
them. Show the three passes and ask which their code was closest to.

## Prediction question

> Two experienced qualitative researchers code the same interview extract. How
> similar should their codes be?

Students trained on quantitative reliability usually answer "very".

## Activity sequence

1. The extract, and a box to write a code in.
2. The code is echoed back, unevaluated, with a one-line reflexive prompt.
3. Three passes over the same words, each with its codes, what it brings
   forward and what it pushes out of view.

## Debrief questions

- Did your code name something about Ama, or something about the course?
- The semantic pass is the one anybody could check. What does that buy, and
  what does it cost?
- The critical pass turns the analysis toward the course. What has it lost?
- If your supervisor's code differs from yours, what would resolve it?

## The moment worth stopping on

The last question. The answer is not a reliability statistic. It is an
argument about which reading answers the research question, and it is settled
by justification rather than by agreement. Say this explicitly, because
students arriving from quantitative methods will otherwise reach for kappa.

## Likely misconceptions

**"There is a correct code and I have to find it."** There is not.

**"Different codes mean somebody is wrong."** Different codes mean different
analytic positions, which is the expected case.

**"Latent coding is deeper, so better."** It finds meaning, and rarely fails
to find any. That is a risk, not a virtue.

**"Reflexivity means confessing your biases."** It means treating your
position as part of the analysis, and showing what it did.

## Limitations and cautions

Three passes is a teaching device: semantic, latent and critical are three
positions on a continuum chosen to make the range visible, and a narrative,
psychosocial or feminist reading would produce others again. Nothing here says
which to use, because that follows from the research question and the
analyst's commitments. One extract is not a dataset, and coding one extract is
not thematic analysis: themes are developed across a corpus.

## Accessibility

Keyboard operable throughout, with a text area sized for a few words. Nothing
written is marked, scored or compared. The three passes are text blocks with
their codes as lists and their trade-offs as labelled boxes, so nothing
depends on colour or position. No timed content and no motion.

## Extension

Ask students to write a second code for the same extract from a position they
do not hold, then to say what would have to be true of the research question
for that code to be the better one.

## Evidence and reading

Braun and Clarke on reflexive thematic analysis, and specifically their
argument about why coding reliability is not the criterion. That paper is the
right next step and is short.
