# What Each Profile Can Decide — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Neuropsychology
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** Final year and postgraduate
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the judgement grid from **Memory Systems
Detective**. A third claim, further profiles and the explicit single-to-double
dissociation step are in the longer version at
`modules/neuropsychology/tools/06-memory-systems-amnesia-detective/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. judge whether a case supports, counts against, or cannot decide a claim;
2. say why a profile with everything impaired is uninformative;
3. say what a single dissociation leaves open and a double one closes;
4. treat an undecidable pairing as a finding;
5. separate evidence about systems from evidence about difficulty.

## Preparation

None. Say before starting that "cannot decide" is a real answer and not a way
of avoiding one, or students will avoid it.

## Suggested use

**Projected, cell by cell**, taking a vote on each before revealing. The
Profile C cells are where the room splits.

**In a session on dissociation logic**, immediately after double dissociations
have been defined, so the definition gets exercised rather than recited.

## Prediction question

Before Profile C is judged:

> Profile C is the most severely impaired person here. Which of the two claims
> does that profile tell us most about?

The expected answer is "the most impaired case must be the most informative".
It is the least informative, and that is the lesson.

## Activity sequence

1. **Read the three profiles.** Make sure both abilities are noted for each.
2. **Claim 1, Profiles A and B.** Both count against a single shared system.
   Point out that together they are a double dissociation.
3. **Claim 1, Profile C.** The key cell. Take the vote before revealing.
4. **Claim 2, all three.** Only A can decide it.
5. **Read the summary**, which scores the cannot-decide cells separately.

## Debrief questions

- Why does a preserved-and-lost pattern count against one shared system?
- Why does Profile C not support the claim, given how severe it is?
- What does a single dissociation leave open? What does adding the second one
  close?
- Could Profile B be a person who simply found the drawing task harder?
- If a case cannot decide a question, has the case told you nothing at all?

## Likely misconceptions

- **The most impaired case is the most informative.** The reverse, for this
  purpose.
- **Cannot decide means not enough data.** It means the evidence is compatible
  with both answers, which is a different and stronger statement.
- **A single dissociation proves separability.** It leaves the difficulty
  explanation open, which is why the second case matters.
- **Preserved means normal.** It means not detectably impaired on this
  measure, and the caution says so.
- **Supports and counts against exhaust the options.** They do not, which is
  the whole design.

## Limitations and cautions

Three fictional profiles described in a sentence each. Real cases are never
this clean: impairments are partial, tasks are hard to equate for difficulty,
and someone who looks intact on one measure may be impaired on a more
sensitive one. Absence of evidence in one session is weak evidence of absence.
No syndrome is named and no real patient is described.

## Accessibility considerations

Every judgement is a labelled button marked with a glyph and a hidden note as
well as a colour. Each profile card states both abilities in words, and every
judgement row repeats that summary beside the options, so the evidence never
has to be held in mind or scrolled back to. A reason appears for every cell
whether right or wrong, and progress is announced politely.

## Optional extension

Ask students to write a fourth profile that would decide claim 2, then check it
against the logic: it has to show skill improvement with recall intact, or the
reverse, in a way that separates the two.

## Evidence and citation notes

The classic evidence is Milner, Corkin and Teuber (1968), *Further analysis of
the hippocampal amnesic syndrome*, **Neuropsychologia**, 6(3), 215–234, and for
the systems framework Squire (2004), *Memory systems of the brain*,
**Neurobiology of Learning and Memory**, 82(3), 171–177. On the logic of
dissociations and its limits see Shallice (1988), *From Neuropsychology to
Mental Structure*, chapters 10 and 11, and Dunn and Kirsner (2003), *What can
we infer from double dissociations?*, **Cortex**, 39(1), 1–7.
