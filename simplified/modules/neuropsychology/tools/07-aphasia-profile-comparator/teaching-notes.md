# Which Measure Separates Them? — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Neuropsychology
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** Second year and above
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the pairing and the prediction from
**Aphasia Profile Comparator**. Two further profiles, the remaining pairings
and the running record are in the longer version at
`modules/neuropsychology/tools/07-aphasia-profile-comparator/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say why a measure is informative about a contrast, not in general;
2. identify the separating measure as the largest gap, not the lowest score;
3. say why two profiles both poor on a measure cannot be told apart by it;
4. say what makes a score interesting: being out of line, not being low;
5. describe a battery as chosen for the comparisons that need making.

## Preparation

None, but run the three pairings in the order the page offers them. The third
is the one that teaches; the first two exist to set up the wrong intuition.

## Suggested use

**Projected, with a vote on each prediction** before revealing. On the third
pairing the room will usually vote for repetition, because both profiles are
so obviously bad at it, and that is exactly the error.

**In a session on test selection or battery design**, where the general
principle transfers well beyond aphasia.

## Prediction question

The activity asks it directly, three times. The one worth dwelling on is the
third:

> Both of these people repeat very badly. Will repetition tell them apart?

The answer is no, and the reason is that a measure separates only when the two
candidates differ on it.

## Activity sequence

1. **Profile P against Profile S.** Fluency, by a mile. Easy.
2. **Profile Q against Profile R.** Comprehension. Also easy.
3. **Profile R against Profile S.** Both repeat badly. Naming separates them
   by 34 points and repetition by 2.
4. **Read the verdict**, which names the principle.
5. **Read the caution**, particularly on the contested categories.

## Debrief questions

- What makes a measure useful, in one sentence?
- Both R and S score terribly on repetition. Why is that not informative?
- Which score on a profile is the interesting one, and why is it not the
  lowest?
- If you had time for only two tests, how would you choose them?
- Does knowing the separating measure tell you what is wrong with either
  person?

## Likely misconceptions

- **The lowest score is the diagnostic one.** The third pairing refutes this.
- **A good test is good generally.** It is good for a contrast.
- **A big gap means a big impairment.** It means a difference between two
  people, which is a different thing.
- **These categories are settled.** They are contested, many patients fit none
  of them, and the labels describe performance rather than explain it. The
  caution says so.
- **Four scores describe a person's language.** They do not, and the caution
  says that too.

## Limitations and cautions

Four fictional profiles with four invented numbers each. Real assessment is not
four scores: measures correlate, fatigue and effort move all of them together,
and a single number for comprehension hides more than it shows. These profiles
are drawn far more sharply than real ones, and the classical categories they
gesture at are contested. Nothing here is an assessment instrument.

## Accessibility considerations

A profile already chosen for one slot is disabled in the other and says so in
its own label rather than only appearing greyed. The chart description gives
both scores and the gap for every measure and names the largest and smallest.
The two profiles are distinguished by vertical position and by an explicit key,
and every gap is drawn as a marked span with the largest labelled. Changing
either profile clears the prediction rather than leaving a stale answer.

## Optional extension

Give students a fifth profile and ask which existing profile it would be
hardest to distinguish from, and what test they would add. That is battery
design in one question.

## Evidence and citation notes

For the classical syndromes and their limits see Goodglass and Kaplan's
framework as discussed critically in Caramazza (1984), *The logic of
neuropsychological research and the problem of patient classification in
aphasia*, **Brain and Language**, 21(1), 9–20. On why group categories fit
individual patients poorly see Schwartz (1984), *What the classical aphasia
categories can't do for us, and why*, **Brain and Language**, 21(1), 3–8.
