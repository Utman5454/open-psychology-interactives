# Building a Split-Brain Trial — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Neuropsychology
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** First or second year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the trial builder from **Split-Brain
Laboratory**. Cross-cueing, further response channels, chimeric stimuli and
the switched-off-conditions record are in the longer version at
`modules/neuropsychology/tools/09-hemispheric-lateralisation-split-brain/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. trace a lateralised stimulus to the receiving hemisphere;
2. say why the response channel decides the outcome;
3. say why a brief presentation is a necessary condition;
4. say why these patients seem unremarkable in daily life;
5. separate what the procedure shows from the two-minds claim.

## Preparation

None. Watch for the standard confusion: this is about sides of the *view*, not
sides of the eye or of the head. The option labels say so.

## Suggested use

**Projected, built once by the room.** Ask for each setting in turn and predict
the outcome before revealing.

**In a lecture on lateralisation**, immediately before or after any mention of
"left-brained" and "right-brained" popular claims, which the caution addresses.

## Prediction question

With the classic trial built and the person unable to answer:

> They have just told you they saw nothing. Is that true?

It is not, and asking them to point with the left hand demonstrates it in one
click. That contrast is the whole lesson.

## Activity sequence

1. **Intact callosum, any combination.** Everything works. Establish the
   baseline.
2. **Press "Show me the classic trial".** Word left, speak, cut, brief. Cannot
   answer.
3. **Change the response to the left hand.** Same word, same person, correct
   answer.
4. **Press "Now break the condition it depends on".** Only the exposure
   changes, and the finding disappears.
5. **Read the caution**, which is the part most often skipped and most often
   needed.

## Debrief questions

- Why does asking for a different response change the answer?
- The person said they saw nothing. What did they mean?
- Which single change abolishes the whole effect, and why?
- Why are these patients unremarkable at home?
- What does this show about hemispheres, and what does it not show?

## Likely misconceptions

- **Split-brain patients have two personalities.** The caution refuses this
  directly.
- **The right hemisphere cannot understand words.** It understood well enough
  to point at the spoon; it could not *speak*.
- **The brief presentation is a technical detail.** It is a necessary
  condition, and the second button demonstrates it.
- **This is about the left eye and the right eye.** It is about sides of the
  visual field.
- **The findings generalise to everyday behaviour.** They largely do not, which
  is why the procedure is so contrived.

## Limitations and cautions

A handful of patients, operated on for epilepsy severe enough to warrant it,
whose brains were already unusual. Language was not always lateralised in the
standard way, surgery was not always complete, and outside the laboratory
behaviour was largely unremarkable. The popular reading goes far beyond what
these procedures show, and recent work has questioned even the perceptual
split. Nothing here describes a real patient.

## Accessibility considerations

All four controls are labelled radio groups and each option states its own
consequence, so the mapping from side to hemisphere and response to hemisphere
is available without the diagram. The guided buttons move the radios as well as
the drawing. A hemisphere holding the word is shaded and says so in words; a
cut callosum is drawn broken with a cross and labelled. The description names
the side, the exposure, the callosum, who has the word, who must answer, and
whether they can.

## Optional extension

Ask students how the patient might get the answer out anyway, given a cut
callosum. Cross-cueing, where one hemisphere signals the other through the
body or the environment, is a real phenomenon and a good lesson in how hard
these experiments are to run cleanly.

## Evidence and citation notes

The classic work is Sperry (1968), *Hemisphere deconnection and unity in
conscious awareness*, **American Psychologist**, 23(10), 723–733, and Gazzaniga
(2005), *Forty-five years of split-brain research*, **Nature Reviews
Neuroscience**, 6(8), 653–659. For the challenge to the standard interpretation
see Pinto and colleagues (2017), *Split brain: Divided perception but undivided
consciousness*, **Brain**, 140(5), 1231–1237.
