# Network Disconnection Mapper — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Neuropsychology
**Duration:** 4–6 minutes
**Level:** Second year and above
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the live network model from **Network
Disconnection Mapper**. A fourth region and task, pathways serving more than
one pairing, and the backwards inference exercise are in the longer version at
`modules/neuropsychology/tools/03-network-disconnection-mapper/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. explain how a deficit can arise from a disconnection;
2. read the pattern of failures as evidence about where the break is;
3. say why a selective deficit with normal grey matter points at white matter;
4. say why "which region does this job" can be the wrong question;
5. say what a three-region cartoon licenses and what it does not.

## Preparation

None. Six checkboxes and a diagram.

## Suggested use

**Projected, with the room predicting each time.** Ask what will fail *before*
each box is ticked. The region cases are predicted easily; the pathway case
almost never is.

**In the lecture that introduces disconnection syndromes**, so that the idea
arrives as something the room has just produced rather than as a definition.

## Prediction question

Before cutting anything:

> I am going to break something without damaging any region at all. What do
> you expect to happen to the three tasks?

Many students expect nothing, because nothing is damaged. That is exactly the
intuition the activity is for.

## Activity sequence

1. **Damage speech planning.** Repetition and naming both fail. Ask why those
   two and not the third: they are the tasks that share the region.
2. **Reset, then cut sound-to-planning.** Repetition alone fails. Point at the
   two circles: both intact.
3. **Ask what a scan would show.** Nothing, if it looked only at grey matter.
4. **Press "Show me the case that matters"** and read the note, which makes
   the comparison explicit.
5. **Try each of the other cuts** and confirm that each takes out exactly one
   task, and each region exactly two.
6. **Read the caution**, which is where the honesty about the cartoon lives.

## Debrief questions

- Why does damaging a region take out two tasks and cutting a pathway only
  one?
- Given only the list of what fails, could you say where the break is?
- What would you ask for if a patient had a very selective deficit and a clean
  structural scan?
- What is wrong with the question "where is repetition in the brain"?
- What would change if a pathway carried traffic for two different tasks?

## Likely misconceptions

- **No damage means no deficit.** The pathway case is a direct counterexample.
- **A deficit localises to the region that does the job.** It localises to
  something necessary, which may be a connection.
- **Disconnection is rare or exotic.** White matter is most of the volume, and
  strokes damage it routinely.
- **This is what the language network looks like.** It is not, and the caution
  says so first rather than last.
- **A clean scan means the complaint is not neurological.** This is the
  clinically important misconception and the reason the activity exists.

## Limitations and cautions

A cartoon. Real networks have many more regions, pathways that carry traffic in
both directions and serve more than one pairing, and enough redundancy that a
single cut rarely produces the clean single-task failure shown here. The task
names are simplified. What holds is the inference: the pattern of what fails
constrains where the break is, and a deficit is not evidence that any
particular region was damaged. Nothing here describes a real patient.

## Accessibility considerations

The network is manipulated by six labelled checkboxes rather than by clicking
the diagram, so nothing depends on pointing at a small target. A damaged region
is drawn with a broken outline and a cross as well as a colour; a cut pathway
is drawn as two stubs with a visible gap and a cross. The diagram description
lists every damaged region, every cut pathway and every failing task, so the
state is fully available as text, and each task tile says works or fails in
words with its reason.

## Optional extension

Ask students to design a fourth task that would let them tell apart two
different pathway cuts that currently look identical. Working out that they
need a task using a different pairing is the logic of a dissociation arriving
on its own.

## Evidence and citation notes

The disconnection framework is Geschwind (1965), *Disconnexion syndromes in
animals and man*, **Brain**, 88(2), 237–294 and 585–644, revisited by
Catani and ffytche (2005), *The rises and falls of disconnection syndromes*,
**Brain**, 128(10), 2224–2239. On white-matter damage in lesion-deficit
inference see Forkel and colleagues (2014), *Anatomical predictors of aphasia
recovery*, **Brain**, 137(7), 2027–2039.
