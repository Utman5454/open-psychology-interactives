# Where Recognition Breaks — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Neuropsychology
**Duration:** 4–6 minutes
**Level:** Second year and above
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the forward simulation from **Face
Recognition Detective**. A sixth component, covert recognition, and the
backwards inference are in the longer version at
`modules/neuropsychology/tools/10-face-recognition-prosopagnosia-detective/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. describe recognition as an ordered sequence with a branch;
2. predict which questions survive a break at a given step;
3. distinguish failing to recognise a face from failing to name a known
   person;
4. say why expression can survive an impairment that destroys identification;
5. say what a box-and-arrow model is and is not evidence for.

## Preparation

None.

## Suggested use

**Projected, stepping through all five breaks in order.** Read the profile
aloud each time. By the third the room can predict the fourth, which is the
model doing its job.

**In a lecture on cognitive models**, as a worked example of why the value of a
model is the profiles it predicts rather than the boxes it draws.

## Prediction question

Before selecting anything:

> Someone tells you they cannot recognise faces. What else would you expect
> them to be unable to do?

Students usually assume one syndrome. There are at least five different
profiles here, and only one of them is what the word usually means.

## Activity sequence

1. **Intact.** Everything works.
2. **Face perception.** Everything fails, including expression. Ask why.
3. **Familiarity.** Matching strangers survives; nobody feels familiar.
4. **Identity.** Familiar but unplaceable. Ask how that differs from the
   previous one.
5. **Name.** The mildest, and universal.
6. **Expression.** Identification intact, feelings unreadable.
7. **Press the guided button** and read the note, then the caution.

## Debrief questions

- Why does a break at perception take out expression too?
- What is the difference between "your face is not familiar" and "I know your
  face but not who you are"?
- Which of these five is the mildest, and who has experienced it?
- What survives that this model does not represent at all?
- What would make you doubt the model?

## Likely misconceptions

- **Prosopagnosia is one thing.** Five profiles here, and the word usually
  refers to one of them.
- **It is a vision problem.** Matching strangers survives in most profiles.
- **Not being able to name someone is a recognition failure.** It is a break at
  the last step only.
- **The boxes are places in the brain.** They are not, and the caution says so.
- **Real cases are all-or-nothing.** They are usually partial.

## Limitations and cautions

A box-and-arrow model summarises which abilities come apart; it does not map
anything in the brain, and drawing it as a neat sequence overstates how orderly
the process is. There is feedback between stages, familiarity and identity are
not cleanly separable in practice, and real cases are usually partial.
Developmental prosopagnosia in particular fits a single-step story poorly. The
model earns its place by predicting which profiles should exist. Nothing here
describes a real person.

## Accessibility considerations

The impairment is chosen by a labelled radio group, not by clicking the
diagram, and the guided button moves the radio as well as the drawing. An
impaired step is drawn with a broken outline and a cross as well as a colour.
The description names the sequence, the branch, the impaired step and every
failing question. Each question tile says can or cannot in words and lists the
steps it needs.

## Optional extension

Ask students what a person with the familiarity break would use to get through
a day. Voices, gait, clothing and context are all outside this model, and
noticing that is a better lesson about model scope than any caveat.

## Evidence and citation notes

The model is a simplified form of Bruce and Young (1986), *Understanding face
recognition*, **British Journal of Psychology**, 77(3), 305–327. On the
separability of expression and identity see Calder and Young (2005),
*Understanding the recognition of facial identity and facial expression*,
**Nature Reviews Neuroscience**, 6(8), 641–651, which also questions how clean
the separation is. On developmental prosopagnosia see Susilo and Duchaine
(2013), *Advances in developmental prosopagnosia research*, **Current Opinion
in Neurobiology**, 23(3), 423–429.
