# What Is the Score Actually Made Of? — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Neuropsychology
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** Second year and above
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the decomposition and the untrained-task
comparison from **Recovery and Plasticity Simulator**. Five simulated people,
the staged experiments and the plateau treatment are in the longer version at
`modules/neuropsychology/tools/12-recovery-plasticity-simulator/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. separate restitution, relearning, compensation and practice;
2. say why only restitution shows on an untrained task;
3. say why an untrained task is worth more than another trained session;
4. recognise that a large steady rise can generalise almost not at all;
5. say why compensation is a real solution nonetheless.

## Preparation

None. Four sliders and one chart.

## Suggested use

**Projected, one slider at a time.** Set everything to zero, then raise
relearning alone: the solid line climbs and the dashed one does not move. That
single observation is the whole activity.

**In a session on rehabilitation outcomes**, where the practical question is
what to measure.

**When discussing plasticity claims**, since "the brain rewired itself" is
often asserted from data that looks exactly like the guided case.

## Prediction question

Before pressing the guided button:

> Here is a patient whose score climbed steadily from 20 to 80 over two years
> with no plateau. Has the underlying problem improved?

It looks like the best possible recovery graph. Seven per cent of it
generalised.

## Activity sequence

1. **Zero everything.** Neither line moves.
2. **Raise relearning alone.** Solid climbs, dashed does not. Ask why.
3. **Repeat for compensation and practice.** Same result.
4. **Raise restitution alone.** Both climb together, and the page draws one
   label because the lines coincide.
5. **Press the guided button** and read the note.
6. **Read the caution**, which is where the additive-bands idealisation is
   admitted.

## Debrief questions

- Why can relearning not lift the untrained task?
- Which single measurement would tell you most about this patient at two
  years?
- A family asks whether the improvement will continue. What do you need to
  know first?
- Is compensation a worse outcome than restitution?
- What is wrong with drawing these four as separate bands that add up?

## Likely misconceptions

- **Improvement means recovery.** Only the restitution part does.
- **A steady rise with no plateau means real recovery.** The guided case is a
  counterexample.
- **Compensation is second best.** A person managing their week with a
  notebook has solved their problem; what changes is the prediction, not the
  value.
- **Practice effects are small enough to ignore.** They are not, over repeated
  testing, and they are the easiest of the four to mistake for recovery.
- **The four contributions are separable in reality.** They are not, and the
  caution says so.

## Limitations and cautions

The four contributions are drawn as separate additive bands and they are not
separable like that: relearning and restitution interact, compensation can
itself drive neural change, and practice effects are not constant across two
years. The time courses are illustrative shapes rather than measured ones, and
real recovery is noisier, less monotonic and far more variable between people.
The untrained task is an idealisation too: one sharing the same underlying
function is hard to find, and any second task acquires a practice effect once
given twice. Nothing here describes a real patient or predicts any recovery.

## Accessibility considerations

Each contribution is a native range input reporting its value in text. The
chart description gives the starting score, all four contributions at two
years, both final scores and the gap. The two lines differ by dash pattern as
well as colour and are labelled at their right-hand ends; where they coincide a
single combined label is drawn rather than two on top of each other. The band
key runs along the bottom so it cannot collide with the line labels at any
setting.

## Optional extension

Ask students to design the measurement schedule they would want for a patient
in rehabilitation, given everything on this page. The answer usually includes
an untrained transfer task and a much longer gap between testings, which is
exactly the argument.

## Evidence and citation notes

For the restitution and substitution distinction see Kolb and Gibb (2014),
*Searching for the principles of brain plasticity and behavior*, **Cortex**,
58, 251–260. On practice effects in serial neuropsychological testing see
Calamia, Markon and Tranel (2012), *Scoring higher the second time around*,
**The Clinical Neuropsychologist**, 26(4), 543–570. On generalisation as the
criterion for rehabilitation outcomes see Cicerone and colleagues (2019),
*Evidence-based cognitive rehabilitation: Systematic review of the literature
from 2009 through 2014*, **Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation**,
100(8), 1515–1533.
