# What Your Battery Cannot Say — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Neuropsychology
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** Final year and postgraduate
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the constrained selection and the two-part
verdict from **Assessment Battery Builder**. Two further referrals, seven more
task types and the battery comparison are in the longer version at
`modules/neuropsychology/tools/11-neuropsych-assessment-battery-builder/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. treat a battery as choices about what not to find out;
2. identify which questions their selection left open;
3. say why a conclusions-only report misleads;
4. name mood, sleep and illness as the coverage usually squeezed out;
5. say why an engagement check matters for a reluctant attender.

## Preparation

None. Read the referral aloud, including the sentences about sleep, the chest
infection and the daughter: every one of them is a question the battery has to
answer or declare unanswered.

## Suggested use

**In small groups, each building a battery**, then comparing what each group
left open. Groups reliably converge on the cognitive tasks and drop mood,
sleep and effort, which is the finding.

**In a session on report writing**, where the second panel is the point.

## Prediction question

Before anyone selects anything:

> Eighty minutes. Can you answer all eight questions?

You cannot: full coverage needs ninety, and the best possible is seven of
eight. Nobody discovers that until they try, which is why the budget is
enforced rather than advisory.

## Activity sequence

1. **Read the referral** and the eight questions.
2. **Build a battery.** The page refuses anything over budget and says why.
3. **Write the report** and read both panels.
4. **Compare across the room.** Who covered mood? Who covered sleep? Who
   checked engagement?
5. **Read the caution**, particularly the sentence about diagnosis.

## Debrief questions

- Which questions did you drop, and was that a decision or an accident?
- What would a reader assume about the questions you did not mention?
- Mr K sleeps badly and has just had a chest infection. What follows if you
  did not ask?
- He came because his daughter insisted. Why does that make an engagement
  check more important, not less?
- Could any battery on this list settle the referral question?

## Likely misconceptions

- **A good battery covers everything.** It cannot, and the arithmetic proves
  it here.
- **Time pressure is a practical nuisance rather than a scientific one.** It
  determines what the report can claim.
- **Cognitive tests are the assessment.** Mood, sleep, medication and effort
  are part of it, and they are what gets dropped.
- **Not mentioning something is neutral.** It reads as normal.
- **The battery answers the referral question.** No combination here could
  diagnose or exclude a dementia, and the caution says so.

## Limitations and cautions

Durations are approximate and coverage is simplified: a real task speaks to
more questions than one or two, more weakly, and how much it tells you depends
on the person. Nothing here is a real test, protocol or referral, and no
selection would be sufficient to diagnose or exclude a dementia, which is a
clinical judgement made over time with information this exercise does not
include. The exercise is about the structure of the problem.

## Accessibility considerations

Every task is a labelled checkbox stating its duration and what it speaks to,
so the trade-off is legible without any chart. A task that would exceed the
budget is refused with a spoken reason rather than silently ignored, and the
checkbox returns to its previous state. Time used and questions covered are
live counters with remainders stated in words. The verdict is two lists in two
cards of equal weight, so what the battery cannot say is not visually
subordinate to what it can.

## Optional extension

Ask students to write the two sentences of the report that state the
limitations. Most find it much harder than choosing the tests, which is the
point worth making.

## Evidence and citation notes

On the incremental value of tests and the cost of adding them see Meehl and
Rosen (1955), *Antecedent probability and the efficiency of psychometric
signs*, **Psychological Bulletin**, 52(3), 194–216. On performance validity as
a routine rather than optional component see Sweet and colleagues (2021),
*American Academy of Clinical Neuropsychology consensus conference statement on
validity assessment*, **The Clinical Neuropsychologist**, 35(6), 1053–1106. On
reversible contributors in an older adult presenting with cognitive complaints,
any clinical text on the assessment of suspected dementia covers the ground.
