# Where the Cut Is — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Neuropsychology
**Duration:** 4–6 minutes
**Level:** First or second year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the updating pathway schematic and the
field diagrams from **Visual Field Defect Mapper**. The backwards inference,
macular sparing and further sites are in the longer version at
`modules/neuropsychology/tools/05-visual-field-defect-mapper/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. predict the defect from a site on the pathway;
2. say why damage before the chiasm affects one eye and after it both;
3. say why a chiasm lesion takes the outer half of each eye's view;
4. say why damage further back can take a quarter;
5. say that a pattern narrows the site without pinning it down.

## Preparation

None, but be ready for the orientation question, which is where students get
lost: each circle is drawn as the *person* sees the world, so the left of the
circle is the left of their view, not the left of their head as you face them.
The figure labels this, but say it out loud.

## Suggested use

**Projected, in pathway order**, from the optic nerve backwards. Ask for a
prediction before each site is selected.

**As a five-minute opener** to a lecture on the visual system, so the anatomy
arrives with a consequence attached.

## Prediction question

Before selecting the chiasm:

> Damage right in the middle, where the fibres cross. Which half of the world
> disappears?

Almost everyone says one side or the other. Nobody says "the outside of both",
and working out why that is the answer is the single most useful minute here.

## Activity sequence

1. **Intact.** Establish the orientation convention.
2. **Left optic nerve.** One eye. Note the rule: any defect confined to one eye
   is in front of the chiasm.
3. **Chiasm.** The surprise. Read the note from the guided button.
4. **Left optic tract.** The same side in both. Define homonymous here.
5. **Meyer's loop.** A quarter, and say why.
6. **Primary visual cortex.** The same picture as the tract. Ask what that
   means for a clinician holding only a field chart.

## Debrief questions

- Why does a nerve lesion affect one eye and a tract lesion both?
- Why is the chiasm the only site here that takes a different side from each
  eye?
- Why can damage further back take a quarter rather than a half?
- Two sites gave you the same picture. What would you need to tell them apart?
- Would this person necessarily notice?

## Likely misconceptions

- **Left brain damage means the left eye.** It means the right half of the
  world, in both eyes.
- **The chiasm takes out one side.** It takes the outside of both.
- **Field loss equals blindness in one eye.** Only before the chiasm.
- **The pattern names the lesion.** It narrows it; the tract and V1 are
  indistinguishable here.
- **A patient will always report it.** Many do not, and the caution says so.

## Limitations and cautions

A schematic. Real defects are rarely sharp-edged, are often incomplete, and
seldom respect tidy boundaries. Many other things cause field loss, from the
retina forwards, and the pattern narrows possibilities rather than naming a
diagnosis. Some people are unaware of substantial field loss, so no complaint
is not evidence of an intact field. Nothing here replaces perimetry or
describes a real patient.

## Accessibility considerations

The site is chosen by a labelled radio group, not by clicking the diagram, and
the guided button moves the radio as well as the drawing so the two never
disagree. A lost quadrant is a solid dark wedge against a pale intact one, and
the break carries a cross and a label as well as a colour. Each circle is
labelled with whose eye it is and with left and right of the person's view. The
description names the site, states in words which quadrants each eye has lost,
and names the defect.

## Optional extension

Give students a field chart and ask where the damage could be. With the tract
and V1 producing the same picture, the honest answer is a list, which is
exactly the backwards exercise the longer version builds out.

## Evidence and citation notes

Standard neuroanatomy; any clinical neurology text covers it. For the classic
account of macular sparing and its interpretation see Horton and Hoyt (1991),
*The representation of the visual field in human striate cortex*, **Archives of
Ophthalmology**, 109(6), 816–824. On unawareness of field loss see Celesia
(2010), *Visual perception and awareness*, **Journal of Clinical
Neurophysiology**, 27(6), 414–421.
