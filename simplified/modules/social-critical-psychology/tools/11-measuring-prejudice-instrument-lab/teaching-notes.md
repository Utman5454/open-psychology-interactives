# What the Instrument Actually Sees — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Social & Critical Psychology
**Duration:** 6–8 minutes
**Level:** Final year or postgraduate
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the instruments, the judgement about what
each directly observes and the analysis that follows, from **Measuring
Prejudice: Instrument Laboratory**. The informant-report instrument, the
prediction round and the closing claims exercise are in the longer version at
`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/11-measuring-prejudice-instrument-lab/`.

## Before you teach it

Nothing on the page measures anybody, and nobody takes any instrument. Vantry
and its regions are invented, no group stands for any real group, and no
stereotype content appears anywhere: the activity is about what an instrument
records and would run identically for any pair of invented categories. That is
deliberate, and it is worth saying at the start so that the discussion stays on
measurement.

It is also not an argument that measuring prejudice is impossible or that the
instruments are worthless. Each observes something real, the alternatives to
expressed attitudes were developed for good reasons, and the criticism is of
the inferential step rather than of the measurement.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say what each instrument directly observes;
2. distinguish that from the inference reported with it;
3. recognise measures at different levels as being about different objects;
4. explain why individual-level evidence cannot settle an institutional claim;
5. say where an instrument becomes the construct.

## Preparation

Students should have met at least one implicit measure by name.

## Suggested use

**When prejudice measurement is taught**, and particularly before any debate
about whether implicit measures predict behaviour, since that debate assumes
the two are measures of one thing.

**In a research methods session on construct validity**, where the four
instruments are a worked set of examples.

**Projected, doing the first instrument together and letting students do the
rest.**

## Prediction question

> Four instruments, all described as measuring prejudice. What mainly differs
> between them?

What each directly observes. "How easy each is to fake" is marked partial: it
is a real difference between two of them and the reason the second was
developed, and it does not reach the fourth at all.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Open the self-report instrument.** Ask what it records before reading the
   options. The answer is what people will write on a scale.
3. **Read the ten-year narrowing.** Ask whether that is a change in feeling.
4. **Open the latency instrument.** Note that individual scores overlap heavily
   with zero.
5. **Open the behavioural choice.** One occasion, in a study, where nobody
   loses a job.
6. **Open the institutional outcomes.** Read the last line twice.
7. **Open the comparison.** Read column two and column four together.

## Debrief questions

- What does each instrument record, in a sentence, without using the word
  prejudice?
- The self-report gap narrowed by nine points. What are the candidate
  explanations?
- The latency distribution overlaps heavily with zero. What does that do to a
  claim about an individual?
- The institutional pattern has not moved in twelve years. Whose attitudes
  would you need to measure to explain it?
- If a large study of individual attitudes found no bias, would the
  institutional finding be refuted?
- Which of these would you use, and to answer what question?

## Likely misconceptions

- **They are four routes to one thing.** Three are about individuals and one is
  about a distribution. That is a difference of object, not of accuracy.
- **The implicit measure gets behind the self-report.** It records a difference
  in sorting speed. Everything else is inference.
- **The institutional finding proves discrimination.** It is one candidate
  explanation among several, and the activity lists others.
- **Individual studies can settle the institutional claim.** They cannot, in
  either direction, because it is a claim about a different object.
- **This means the instruments are worthless.** Each observes something real.
  The criticism is of the inferential step.

## Limitations and cautions

Four instruments is a simplification and a fifth level is missing. The lists of
alternative explanations are illustrative rather than exhaustive, and a good
argument for a further one should be credited. Every figure was invented for
teaching and none is evidence about anywhere.

## Accessibility considerations

The instrument is a group of native radio buttons in a labelled fieldset. The
judgement options carry three non-colour state signals plus a visually hidden
sentence, and once answered are locked with `aria-disabled` rather than the
`disabled` attribute so they stay reachable. The comparison is a real table
whose every cell is a sentence.

## Optional extension

Ask students to write the sentence a press release would use for each finding,
and then the sentence the instrument actually licenses. The gap between the two
is the same size every time, which is the transferable observation.

## Evidence and citation notes

On what implicit measures record and what follows see De Houwer, Teige-Mocigemba,
Spruyt and Moors (2009), *Implicit measures: A normative analysis and review*,
**Psychological Bulletin**, 135(3), 347–368, and on their relation to behaviour
Oswald, Mitchell, Blanton, Jaccard and Tetlock (2013), *Predicting ethnic and

different object see Doise (1986), **Levels of Explanation in Social
Psychology**, Cambridge University Press. On outcome patterns sustained without
anybody's attitude see Reskin (2003), *Including mechanisms in our models of
ascriptive inequality*, **American Sociological Review**, 68(1), 1–21.
