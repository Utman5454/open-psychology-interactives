# Nine Things She Said — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Social & Critical Psychology
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** Second year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the nine fixed statements and the framework
switch from **The Self Through Different Lenses**, cut from seven frameworks to
five. The account-matching task and the side-by-side comparison are in the
longer version at
`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/05-self-through-different-lenses/`.

## Before you teach it

There is no correct framework here and no hidden fact about Nadia to find. When
a framework is said to background something, that never means its users deny
it: the claim is about what its concepts can represent. If a student concludes
that a framework with more coverage would be better, the answer is that it
would almost certainly be vaguer.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say which question each framework asks;
2. explain why what a framework makes central is inseparable from what it
   cannot represent;
3. distinguish having no concept for something from denying it;
4. identify the statement no framework here claims;
5. explain why a framework that represented everything would predict nothing.

## Preparation

Students should have met at least two of the five frameworks by name. The
activity gives each one as a question rather than as a definition.

## Suggested use

**After several frameworks for the self have been taught separately**, as the
thing to do with them together.

**Before an essay that asks students to compare theoretical approaches**, since
the coverage table is the comparison such essays usually fail to make.

**Projected, working through all five from the front.**

## Prediction question

> Five frameworks are applied to the same nine statements. What will mainly
> differ between them?

What each has no concept for. "Not much" is marked partial: two of the five
really are close to being different vocabularies for related questions, and one
of them disagrees about what a self is rather than which part to study.

## Activity sequence

1. **Read the nine statements aloud.** Do not comment.
2. **Take the vote.**
3. **Work through all five frameworks.** For each, read its question first,
   then look at which two statements it claims.
4. **Watch the coverage line at the bottom.** After each framework it says how
   many of the nine anybody has claimed.
5. **Open the summary table.** Read down the final column.
6. **Stop at statement 8.** The fees went up and the hours were cut. No
   framework here treats it as the kind of thing it explains.

## Debrief questions

- Each framework claims exactly two statements. Is that a limitation?
- Which framework has no concept for statement 8, and why not?
- The one framework that can reach it does so by turning it into a belief about
  her future. What has that done to the fee increase?
- Statement 9 is the tutor's remark. Which frameworks treat it as information
  about Nadia, and which as information about the course?
- If you were commissioning research on students like Nadia, which framework
  would you fund, and what would go unstudied?
- Would a framework that covered all nine be better?

## Likely misconceptions

- **One of them is right.** They ask different questions. A question can only
  be answered with evidence that counts as evidence for it.
- **A blind spot is an oversight.** It is what having concepts consists of.
- **Backgrounding means denying.** Researchers in every one of these traditions
  would agree that fees matter. Their frameworks have no term for it.
- **More coverage is better.** It would be vaguer, and it would predict less.
- **This is a criticism of psychology.** It is a description of what theories
  are. The same exercise on any set of theories would produce a similar table.

## Limitations and cautions

The codings are authored judgements made for teaching and a good argument for a
different coding should be credited. Five frameworks is not a map of the
literature: narrative, dialogical, psychodynamic, cultural and neurocognitive
accounts are absent, as are two further frameworks in the longer version, one
of which does slightly better with the September statement.

## Accessibility considerations

The framework is a group of native radio buttons in a labelled fieldset. Every
coding is given in words in a real table with headers, and rows carry a
non-colour state as reinforcement rather than as the signal. The framework's
question appears as text, and both the coverage tracker and the summary verdict
are stated in words.

## Optional extension

Ask students to name a framework, from anywhere in psychology or outside it,
that would treat statement 8 as central, and then to say what that framework
would have no concept for. There is always something.

## Evidence and citation notes

On self-schemas see Markus (1977), *Self-schemata and processing information
about the self*, **Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 35(2),
63–78. On social comparison, Festinger (1954), *A theory of social comparison
processes*, **Human Relations**, 7(2), 117–140. On self-discrepancy, Higgins
(1987), *Self-discrepancy: A theory relating self and affect*, **Psychological
Review**, 94(3), 319–340. On social identity, Tajfel and Turner (1979), *An
integrative theory of intergroup conflict*, in Austin and Worchel's **The
Social Psychology of Intergroup Relations**. On relational and contextual
selves, Gergen (2009), **Relational Being**, Oxford University Press. On the
general point that a theory's coverage and its content are the same decision,
see Popper (1959), **The Logic of Scientific Discovery**, Hutchinson.
