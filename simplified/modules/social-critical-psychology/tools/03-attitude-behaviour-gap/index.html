Four People, One Score

Everyone in this activity holds the same view. Almost nothing else about them is the same.

What the model is and is not

What the model is and is not. The likelihoods come from a small logistic model whose coefficients are printed below and in the teaching notes. They are chosen to make a teaching pattern visible. They are not estimates of anything, they come from no dataset, and they should never be quoted as effect sizes. This page also takes no position on whether voicing disagreement is a good idea: it estimates a frequency, not a virtue, and the costs of speaking up fall very unevenly across a workforce.

First, a prediction

Aisha scores 0.86 out of 1 on an attitude scale about speaking up when she disagrees in a team meeting. It is a well-made scale, matched to the behaviour, and she answered it honestly. Out of the next thirty meetings, how many will she speak up in?

Key terms
Perceived norm
What this person takes the people around them to expect and to do.
Perceived control
How far they believe the act is available to them, given who they are in this room.
Opportunity
Whether the occasion arises at all. In the model below it multiplies rather than adds, so when it is near zero nothing else matters.
Correspondence
How closely the measure matches the act. The scale here is a behaviour-level attitude scale, which is a favourable case.

      What this shows

      The gap is not a fact about attitudes

      Key idea: The four colleagues hold the same view and speak up at completely different rates, and the reason is in columns a study of attitudes would not have collected. Marek is the case worth keeping: every internal condition he has is as strong as Rowan's, and the occasion simply does not arise in his team, so a course aimed at changing his attitude would change nothing at all. Then the three hundred make a further point. The attitude-behaviour correlation is often reported as though it were a property of attitudes, or of people, or of the concept of an attitude. Here the same attitude scores produce a correlation twice as large when everybody is in the same kind of room, because when the conditions are held constant the only thing left varying is the attitude. A low attitude-behaviour correlation is partly a measurement of how unequal the conditions are.

      A simulation cannot test a theory. The coefficients are invented, the correlations that follow from them are not findings, and no number here estimates anything. Six factors is a deliberate simplification of a literature with many more, and the model has no feedback in it at all: in reality speaking up changes the norm, the norm changes the opportunity, and being the person who spoke up last time changes what is available to you next time. Two further cautions. Holding conditions constant to raise a correlation is a description of what happens in a tidy workplace, not a research recommendation, and a study that removes variation in conditions has removed the thing that most needed explaining. And the model estimates a frequency and not a virtue: the costs of speaking up fall very unevenly, and Devi's four months on a fixed-term contract are a reason rather than a deficit.
