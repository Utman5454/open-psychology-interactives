# Four People, One Score — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Social & Critical Psychology
**Duration:** 6–8 minutes
**Level:** First or second year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the logistic model with its coefficients
printed, the four colleagues who share a score, and the demonstration that the
correlation depends on how much conditions vary. The correspondence control,
the per-person sliders and the guided prediction round are in the longer
version at
`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/03-attitude-behaviour-gap/`.

## Before you teach it

The page estimates a frequency, not a virtue. It takes no position on whether
speaking up is a good idea, and the costs of doing so fall very unevenly across
a workforce. Devi's four months on a fixed-term contract are a reason, not a
deficit, and it is worth saying so before anyone describes her as lacking
assertiveness.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. explain why a strong attitude does not on its own predict behaviour;
2. distinguish factors that shift the odds from opportunity, which multiplies;
3. explain why an attitude-focused intervention can change nothing;
4. explain why an average is not a prediction about an individual;
5. explain why a low correlation is partly a fact about the conditions.

## Preparation

Students should have met the idea of a correlation. They do not need to know
the reasoned-action literature; the model is given.

## Suggested use

**When attitudes are introduced**, before any claim that attitudes predict
behaviour is either asserted or debunked.

**Before teaching about behaviour-change interventions**, because Marek is the
case that decides where an intervention should be aimed.

**Projected, with the vote first.** "Somewhere around half" is the popular
answer and is marked partial for an instructive reason.

## Prediction question

> Aisha scores 0.86 on a well-matched attitude scale. In how many of the next
> thirty meetings will she speak up?

You cannot say. The four colleagues who share her score answer 25, 8, 4 and 14.
Note that "around half" is close to their average of 13 and is a prediction
about nobody.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Read the table across, then read the first column down.** It is 0.86 four
   times.
3. **Read the four descriptions.** Ask which columns a study of attitudes
   would have collected.
4. **Press "Give Marek the room Rowan is in".** One number changes and he goes
   from 4 to 24. Ask what a training course aimed at Marek would have achieved.
5. **Go to the three hundred and press the workplace button.** Ask the room to
   watch the markers. They move up and down and never sideways.
6. **Read the two correlations.** 0.26 and 0.61, on identical attitude scores.

## Debrief questions

- What differs between Rowan and Devi?
- Marek has Rowan's confidence and almost her sense of the norm. Why is his
  rate the lowest of the four?
- What does it mean for opportunity to multiply rather than add?
- Where should an intervention aimed at Marek be aimed?
- The correlation doubled and nobody's attitude changed. What is a low
  attitude-behaviour correlation telling you?
- Would you recommend making everybody's conditions identical in order to raise
  it?

## Likely misconceptions

- **Attitudes do not predict behaviour.** They do, when the room lets them.
  Rowan speaks up in 25 meetings out of 30 with the same score as Marek.
- **The gap is a measurement problem.** The scale here is well matched and
  honestly answered, which is why it is fixed at the favourable weight.
- **A better model would close the gap.** The other factors are in the model.
  The gap is between an attitude and a behaviour, not between a model and the
  data.
- **The average tells you what a person will do.** The average of these four is
  13, and not one of them is near it.
- **Raising the correlation would be an improvement.** It would mean equalising
  the conditions, and a study that removes variation in conditions has removed
  what most needed explaining.

## Limitations and cautions

A simulation cannot test a theory. The coefficients are invented, the
correlations are not findings, and no number estimates anything. Six factors is
a deliberate reduction, and the model has no feedback in it at all: in reality
speaking up changes the norm, the norm changes the opportunity, and having been
the person who spoke up last time changes what is available next time. That
omission is the largest one.

## Accessibility considerations

Every factor for every colleague is in a real table with headers, and each
person's situation is also given in prose. The workplace control is a button
carrying `aria-pressed` with a visible word saying which version is in use, and
both correlations are readouts as well as being visible in the scatter.

## Optional extension

Ask students to design the intervention they would actually run in Marek's
organisation, and then to say which column of the table it moves. Almost every
proposal that starts with the word training moves the wrong one.

## Evidence and citation notes

On the tradition the model borrows from see Ajzen (1991), *The theory of
planned behavior*, **Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes**,
50(2), 179–211. On correspondence between measure and act see Ajzen and
Fishbein (1977), *Attitude-behavior relations*, **Psychological Bulletin**,
84(5), 888–918. On the older argument that the gap is a question about
situations see Wicker (1969), *Attitudes versus actions*, **Journal of Social
Issues**, 25(4), 41–78. On why removing variation in conditions raises a
correlation without improving anything, the general treatment is in the
Simplified Edition activity **The Same Genes, A Different Heritability**, which
is the same arithmetic in a different literature.
