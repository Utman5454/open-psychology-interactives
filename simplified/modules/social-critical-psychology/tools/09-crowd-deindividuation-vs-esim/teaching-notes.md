# How Many, and At What — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Social & Critical Psychology
**Duration:** 6–8 minutes
**Level:** Final year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the participation model, the target
weighting, the concentration measure and the disinhibition index from the full
**Crowds** activity, with the five independent conditions reduced to four
scenarios and one live control. Setting each condition separately and the
falsification exercise are in the longer version at
`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/09-crowd-deindividuation-vs-esim/`.

## Before you teach it

The numbers come from a teaching model, not from any real crowd. Neither
account is refuted here and both have serious critics. The older account is not
a straw man: it was built to explain real observations, it is still defended in
modified forms, and in two of the four afternoons here its prediction about
direction is the one that comes true.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say how the accounts differ about patterning;
2. say where each locates the cause;
3. explain why a count of participants cannot distinguish two afternoons;
4. explain why anonymity sharpens action where a shared understanding exists;
5. recognise that each account wins in some conditions.

## Preparation

Students should have met both accounts by name.

## Suggested use

**When crowd psychology is taught**, and specifically to replace the framing in
which one account simply replaced the other.

**In teaching on how theories are tested**, because the two accounts here make
opposite directional predictions about the same manipulation.

**Projected.** The order that works is: read the two accounts, take the vote,
run the common-category afternoon, toggle anonymity, then press the comparison.

## Prediction question

> The action was almost entirely on one target and three things a few metres
> away were untouched. Which account fits better?

The social identity account. "Neither, without knowing how many joined in" is
marked partial: the count matters and is reported, and it is the measure on
which the two accounts agree most.

## Activity sequence

1. **Read the two-account table aloud.** Note the last column: they tell you to
   measure different things.
2. **Take the vote.**
3. **Run the common-category afternoon.** Six sections of six take part, and
   70 per cent of the action is on one target.
4. **Toggle anonymity.** Concentration rises to 0.76. Ask which account
   predicted the direction.
5. **Go to the fragmented afternoon and toggle anonymity there.**
   Concentration falls. Ask the same question.
6. **Press the comparison.** Two afternoons, identical index, 0.12 against
   0.76.

## Debrief questions

- What does the disinhibition index measure, and what does it miss?
- Anonymity moved concentration in opposite directions in two afternoons. Why?
- The restraint afternoon has one section acting and five not. Which account
  explains the five?
- Which account would you use to advise on how to police an event, and what
  would it tell you to do?
- What would each account have to observe in order to be wrong?
- Two of the four afternoons went the classic account's way. Does that matter?

## Likely misconceptions

- **The classic account has been refuted.** It has not, and here it predicts
  the direction correctly in half the cases.
- **The disagreement is about how violent crowds get.** It is about whether
  what happens has a shape.
- **Anonymity always increases disorder.** Where a shared understanding is in
  play it concentrates the action instead.
- **A common category is something a crowd arrives with.** In this model it is
  produced by how the crowd is treated during the afternoon.
- **A shared norm means more action.** The restraint afternoon has a shared
  norm and one section taking part.

## Limitations and cautions

Invented numbers throughout, and the concentration measure is a device written
for this page rather than a published statistic. Six sections with fixed
readiness scores is a considerable simplification of an afternoon in which
people talk to each other, change their minds, arrive and leave. The successor
account has critics of its own, chiefly that identifying a shared understanding
after the fact is easier than predicting it beforehand.

## Accessibility considerations

The scenario is a group of native radio buttons in a labelled fieldset, and
identifiability is a button carrying `aria-pressed` with a visible word for its
state. Every target's share is labelled in words on its bar and repeated in the
chart description. Participation is a real table whose verdict column reads yes
or no. The matched pair is a table rather than a figure.

## Optional extension

Ask students what a police commander who believed each account would do
differently on the day. The two answers are genuinely different, and the second
one is the reason the successor account was developed.

## Evidence and citation notes

On the classic account see Zimbardo (1969), *The human choice*, in the
**Nebraska Symposium on Motivation**, volume 17. On the elaborated social
identity model see Reicher (1996), *The Battle of Westminster*, **European
Journal of Social Psychology**, 26(1), 115–134, and Drury and Reicher (2000),
*Collective action and psychological change*, **British Journal of Social
Psychology**, 39(4), 579–604. On anonymity sharpening rather than dissolving
norms see Postmes and Spears (1998), *Deindividuation and antinormative
behavior: A meta-analysis*, **Psychological Bulletin**, 123(3), 238–259. On
the policing implications see Reicher, Stott, Cronin and Adang (2004), *An
integrated approach to crowd psychology and public order policing*, **Policing**,
27(4), 558–572.
