# Where the Prevalence Comes From — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Social & Critical Psychology
**Duration:** 6–8 minutes
**Level:** Second or final year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the threshold and the instrument from
**Constructing a Category**, which are the two stages where a description most
visibly becomes a thing. The naming stage, the comparison set, the separate
duration and interference rules and the institutional-hardening stage are in
the longer version at
`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/02-constructing-a-category/`.

## Before you teach it

The five people are describing something. Nothing here suggests they are
inventing it, and nothing here is a criticism of anyone who finds a category
useful for naming their own experience. The category, its features, its
instrument and its prevalence are all invented; no real diagnosis, construct,
criteria set or published estimate is described. Nothing is administered to
anybody.

The failure mode to watch for is a student leaving with "so it is all made up".
The page argues against that in three places, and so should you.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. explain why a prevalence depends on the threshold;
2. explain why socially constructed does not mean made up;
3. explain how an instrument decides who counts;
4. recognise that the features which sound most like a condition may
   distinguish least;
5. say what stops being asked once the category has hardened.

## Preparation

None.

## Suggested use

**When social construction is introduced**, as the alternative to defining the
term and moving on.

**In teaching on classification, diagnosis or screening**, where the threshold
step is the one usually skipped.

**Projected, with the vote first.** Most of a room picks "how common the
difficulty is", which is the belief the activity is about.

## Prediction question

> A paper reports that eighteen per cent of working adults meet the criteria.
> What does that figure most depend on?

Where the line was drawn. Sampling and item wording are both marked partial:
they are real and neither moves the figure as far as the threshold does.

## Activity sequence

1. **Read the five accounts aloud.** Do not comment on them yet.
2. **Take the vote.**
3. **At the opening line of five features, note the prevalence:** 18.3 per
   cent, which is the figure in the vote.
4. **Move the line to two.** 82.3 per cent. Ask what changed about the four
   hundred people. The bars do not move; only the shading does.
5. **Go to the questionnaire and press the guided button.** The three features
   that sound most like the condition find three of the five.
6. **Go back to the top of the page and read what Person 3 and Person 5 said.**
   Both describe difficulty that comes from their hours. The instrument has
   just decided that whatever they have, it is not this.
7. **Try to find a set of three that finds all five.** There is not one.

## Debrief questions

- Which of the two numbers on this page was discovered?
- The line moved and the prevalence changed tenfold. What is a prevalence a
  fact about?
- Why do the features that sound most like the condition find the fewest
  people?
- Person 3 and Person 5 are not found. What follows for them institutionally?
- If no three items find all five, what should the instrument do?
- Does any of this mean the five people are not struggling?

## Likely misconceptions

- **So the condition is not real.** The page says three times that it is not
  making that argument. Classification is how any science starts.
- **The threshold is arbitrary, so any threshold is as good as any other.** A
  threshold has to be somewhere if anybody is to be offered anything. What
  follows is that it should be argued for, not that it should not exist.
- **A better-worded questionnaire would find everyone.** No set of three items
  finds all five, and that is a fact about choosing three from six rather than
  about wording.
- **The commonest features are the most diagnostic.** They are the least: they
  are common because their intercepts are high and their relation to the
  underlying dimension is weak.
- **This applies to every category.** The population here has one latent
  dimension, which is what makes the line look plainly arbitrary. Whether a
  given real category has natural joints is an empirical question.

## Limitations and cautions

The four hundred are simulated from a single latent dimension. That is a strong
assumption and it is doing a lot of the work: a category with genuine natural
joints would not behave this way. The scoring rule, two endorsements out of
three, is a crude stand-in. The naming stage and the institutional stage, both
of which add object status, are not here.

## Accessibility considerations

The threshold is a native range input reporting its value in text. An item that
will not fit the three-item questionnaire is dimmed and described as not
fitting rather than disabled, so it stays in the tab order, and a fourth
selection is refused with a spoken reason. The accounts and the detection
results are real tables with headers and verdicts in words. The distribution's
bars carry their counts as text.

## Optional extension

Ask students to write the sixth account: somebody the instrument would find who
is not struggling at all. It takes about a minute, and it is the false-positive
half of the argument that the activity does not otherwise reach.

## Evidence and citation notes

On categories acquiring the appearance of natural objects see Hacking (1999),
**The Social Construction of What?**, Harvard University Press, and Hacking
(1995), *The looping effects of human kinds*, in Sperber, Premack and Premack's
**Causal Cognition**. On thresholds manufacturing prevalence see Kessler et al.
(2003), *Mild disorders should not be eliminated from the DSM-V*, **Archives of
General Psychiatry**, 60(11), 1117–1122, and the reply literature around it. On
instruments narrowing what a construct can include see Michell (2000),
*Normal science, pathological science and psychometrics*, **Theory and
Psychology**, 10(5), 639–667.
