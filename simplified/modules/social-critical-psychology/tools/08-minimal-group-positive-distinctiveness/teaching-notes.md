# Ahead, or Better Off — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Social & Critical Psychology
**Duration:** 6–8 minutes
**Level:** First or second year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the allocation task performed and the full
four-option structure from **Minimal Group and Positive Distinctiveness**, cut
from six allocations to four, and keeps the three starting positions, which are
the original tool's own addition. The sorting task and the claims exercise are
in the longer version at
`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/08-minimal-group-positive-distinctiveness/`.

## Before you teach it

The categories are invented and empty on purpose: that is what lets the
paradigm ask whether categorisation alone is enough. It is also why the result
cannot simply be lifted out of the laboratory, and the third starting position
exists to make the absence of history visible rather than to fix it. Nothing is
measured about anybody and there is no score.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. explain why maximum difference rather than maximum ingroup profit is the
   finding;
2. explain why self-interest cannot reach it;
3. say what an allocation pattern cannot establish about motives;
4. recognise the same allocation as creating or widening an inequality;
5. state what the design removes on purpose.

## Preparation

None.

## Suggested use

**When social identity theory is introduced**, before the theory, so that the
result to be explained is in the room first.

**In teaching on intergroup conflict**, specifically to establish how little
the paradigm requires and how much it therefore leaves out.

**Projected, with the room voting on each allocation before you click.**

## Prediction question

> Nobody can allocate to themselves and the points buy nothing. What do most
> people do?

Put their own category as far ahead as possible, even though a different option
would give it more. Both "split evenly" and "give their own category most" are
marked partial: the first is a real minority strategy, the second is the wrong
kind of favouritism.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Read the constraints aloud before any allocation.** You cannot allocate to
   yourself, you will never meet either person, the points buy nothing.
3. **Do allocation 1 from the front.** Ask the room which option gives a
   Kestrel the most. It is not the one that puts Kestrels furthest ahead.
4. **Let students do the remaining three.**
5. **Read the running line.** If they chose the largest lead throughout, it has
   cost Kestrels 23 points.
6. **Press the scoring button.** Same points, three starting positions.
7. **Read the last column across the three rows.** Created, widened, narrowed.

## Debrief questions

- Which option would a person who wanted the best outcome for their category
  have chosen?
- What did choosing the largest lead cost, and what did it buy?
- Nobody could allocate to themselves. What does that rule out as an
  explanation?
- Your choices were identical in all three rows of the last table. Were they
  the same act?
- What has this design removed, and what would you need to add before saying
  anything about a real conflict?
- Does the pattern tell you why people chose as they did?

## Likely misconceptions

- **It is just selfishness.** Nobody can allocate to themselves and the points
  buy nothing.
- **It is ordinary favouritism.** Ordinary favouritism would take the
  most-to-a-Kestrel option, which is available every time and is not what most
  people take.
- **The paradigm explains real intergroup conflict.** It shows categorisation
  is sufficient here. Real conflicts have the histories and resources this
  design removes on purpose.
- **Positive distinctiveness is established by the result.** Several accounts
  fit a pattern of choices between four options, and this cannot choose
  between them.
- **Choosing fairly means you are unbiased.** It is one of the strategies the
  paradigm reliably finds, and one run of four allocations shows very little.

## Limitations and cautions

A demonstration of a paradigm rather than a replication, and nothing reproduces
the matrices, procedure or data of any published study. Four allocations is a
short run. The paradigm cannot adjudicate between positive distinctiveness,
demand characteristics, a norm of playing the game as it appears to be set up,
and simple category salience.

## Accessibility considerations

Every option is written out in full with both point values in its label, so no
allocation has to be read from a grid. Once made, an allocation's options are
locked with `aria-disabled` rather than the `disabled` attribute so they stay
reachable, and each explains in words what kind of option it was and what the
maximum-difference option cost. The three starting positions are a real table
whose final column is a sentence.

## Optional extension

Ask students to design a fifth option that a person maximising their category's
welfare would take, and then to say why the paradigm does not include it. It
does: it is the most-to-a-Kestrel option, and noticing that it was there all
along is the point.

## Evidence and citation notes

The paradigm is Tajfel, Billig, Bundy and Flament (1971), *Social
categorization and intergroup behaviour*, **European Journal of Social
Psychology**, 1(2), 149–178. On positive distinctiveness see Tajfel and Turner
(1979), *An integrative theory of intergroup conflict*, in Austin and Worchel's
**The Social Psychology of Intergroup Relations**. On alternative readings of
the effect see Berkowitz (1994), *Guns and youth*, for the demand-characteristic
line, and more directly Gaertner and Insko (2000), *Intergroup discrimination
in the minimal group paradigm*, **Journal of Personality and Social
Psychology**, 79(1), 77–94. On what the design removes see Reicher (2004),
*The context of social identity*, **Political Psychology**, 25(6), 921–945.
