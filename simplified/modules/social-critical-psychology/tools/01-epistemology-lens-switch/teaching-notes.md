# The Method Comes Last — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Social & Critical Psychology
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** Second or final year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the cascade from **Epistemology Lens
Switch**: declare a starting point, then make decisions whose options are never
labelled with the position they belong to. Three of the five decisions and the
second pass from another starting point are in the longer version at
`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/01-epistemology-lens-switch/`.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. say what each of three epistemologies takes its object to be;
2. explain why the method follows from the object;
3. say what each completed study could and could not claim;
4. treat "in tension" as a design question rather than an error;
5. name the fourth position that locates the problem inside individuals.

## Preparation

Students should have met the words positivist, critical realist and social
constructionist at least once. They do not need to be able to define them; the
activity gives each position as a creed rather than a label.

## Suggested use

**At the start of a research methods or critical psychology unit**, before any
method is taught, so that the question "which method should I use?" is already
known to be downstream of something else.

**Before a dissertation planning session.** The habit it builds, being able to
say what you took the thing to be, is the one most often missing from a
proposal.

**Projected, built once from the front, then once more by students.**

## Prediction question

> Three researchers produce three different studies from the same file. What
> most decides which method each uses?

What they take the thing to be. Note that two of the wrong answers are marked
partial: the client's question and the researcher's training both matter, and
neither settles it.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Read the case aloud, including the January policy change.** That one
   sentence is what makes the governors' question unanswerable from the log.
3. **Declare a starting point.** Ask the room to predict which of the four
   options in the next decision will follow from it, before reading them.
4. **Make both decisions.** Read the annotation on each choice.
5. **Deliberately make one choice in tension**, and read what it says. It does
   not say you are wrong.
6. **Finish the study and read the table across.** Only the first row can
   answer the question that was actually asked, and only by treating the log
   as a measure at exactly the moment the rule changed.
7. **Read the fourth position.** Ask who would fund it.

## Debrief questions

- Which of the three studies answers the governors' question?
- What does it have to assume to do so?
- The other two refuse the question. Are they being awkward?
- Which decision in a real study is this one, and where is it written down?
- The fourth position is coherent and fundable. What is wrong with it here?
- Could you combine two of these positions? What would you have to say?

## Likely misconceptions

- **One of these is the scientific one.** The page does not rank them, and each
  row of the table names something it cannot say.
- **In tension means wrong.** Good mixed designs do it on purpose. What they
  also do is say how they hold the two together.
- **Coherent means good.** Coherence is the minimum to clear. A perfectly
  coherent study can be trivial, unethical or badly executed.
- **Constructionism says nothing really happened.** The third row studies how a
  category is assembled, which is not a claim that the lessons were fictional.
- **Critical realism is just qualitative work with a longer name.** It commits
  you to unobservable mechanisms, and its discipline is the negative case.
- **The fourth position is a trap.** It is a real research programme and the
  one most likely to be funded, which is exactly why it is worth naming.

## Limitations and cautions

Three positions is a simplification of a contested landscape, and the page does
not rank them. Two decisions is a short cascade; the longer version adds what
counts as evidence and what claim the study could support, and both add
constraints of their own. The comparison in step three is written rather than
derived, so it is one defensible account of what each position could produce.

## Accessibility considerations

The starting point is a group of native radio buttons in a labelled fieldset,
and each decision is a group of buttons with three non-colour state signals
plus a visually hidden sentence naming the state. A chosen option says in words
whether it follows from the declaration or sits in tension with it. The three
studies are compared in a real table with headers.

## Optional extension

Ask students to write the sentence a real methods section would contain that
declares its research object, and then to find that sentence in a published
paper. It is usually not there, which is the point worth arriving at.

## Evidence and citation notes

On the three positions see Bhaskar (1975), **A Realist Theory of Science**,
Leeds Books, for critical realism; Gergen (1985), *The social constructionist
movement in modern psychology*, **American Psychologist**, 40(3), 266–275; and,
for the argument that method choice is downstream of ontology, Danermark,
Ekström and Karlsson (2019), **Explaining Society**, 2nd edition, Routledge. On
the general point that measurement instruments encode a definition before data
collection begins see Hacking (1999), **The Social Construction of What?**,
Harvard University Press.
