# Two Reasons to Agree — Simplified Edition

**Module:** Social & Critical Psychology
**Duration:** 5–7 minutes
**Level:** First or second year
**Edition:** Simplified. This keeps the two-route model with both routes shown
separately, and the conditions that move each, from **Conformity Under
Context**. The continuous sliders, the participant task and the hundred
simulated individuals are in the longer version at
`modules/social-critical-psychology/tools/04-conformity-under-context/`.

## Before you teach it

Nothing here reproduces the stimuli, procedure or data of any published study.
The parameters are printed on the page so they can be argued with, and the
figures are chosen to put the classic conditions in roughly the right order of
magnitude. Conformity is not being measured as a defect: in the ambiguous
condition the agreement may well be correct, and the page says so.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student should be able to:

1. distinguish the two routes by what moves each;
2. explain why privacy separates them;
3. explain why one dissenter changes more than the arithmetic suggests;
4. explain why a rate is not a finding without a context;
5. name identification as a term the classic account lacks.

## Preparation

None.

## Suggested use

**When the classic conformity studies are taught**, in place of reciting the
headline percentage.

**Before teaching about interventions in groups**, because the diagnostic
comparison is exactly the question an intervention designer faces: will making
this anonymous help?

**Projected, running conditions live.** The order that works is classic, then
private, then ambiguous, then ambiguous private.

## Prediction question

> Five others give the same obviously wrong answer aloud. On what share of
> rounds does the participant agree?

About a third. Note that "it depends on the room" is marked partial: it is the
conclusion the activity reaches, and arriving before running anything makes it
a slogan rather than a finding.

## Activity sequence

1. **Take the vote.**
2. **Run the classic condition.** 35 per cent. Say aloud that two rounds in
   three are answered correctly in front of five people who just said
   otherwise.
3. **Run the private condition.** Ask the room to watch the informational
   column. It does not move.
4. **Run the ambiguous condition.** The rate is the highest on the page.
5. **Run the ambiguous private condition.** Ask them to predict the fall first.
   It is half what it was on the easy display.
6. **Run the ally condition and the outgroup condition.** Both collapse the
   rate through the normative route alone.

## Debrief questions

- Which conditions moved the informational route, and which did not?
- Why does making a response private separate the two routes?
- You are asked to reduce agreement in a real meeting. What do you need to know
  first?
- Four of the five still agree in the ally condition. Why does the rate fall so
  far?
- The outgroup condition falls a long way. What term in the model does that?
- In the ambiguous condition, is agreeing a mistake?

## Likely misconceptions

- **People conform about a third of the time.** That figure belongs to one
  room. The six conditions here span from 7 to 66 per cent on the same task.
- **Conformity is a personality trait being measured.** Nothing about the
  participant changes across the six conditions.
- **Anonymity fixes conformity.** It fixes one route. On the ambiguous display
  it removes about half as much.
- **Group size is what matters.** Unanimity is a step, not a headcount. One
  dissenter out of five changes far more than removing a confederate would.
- **Agreeing is always an error.** In the ambiguous condition other people are
  evidence, and the agreement may be correct.
- **These are timeless rates.** They are of their time and place, and rates
  vary across decades and societies.

## Limitations and cautions

A teaching model rather than a replication. The parameters are invented and
printed. Six conditions is a small slice of a large literature, and the model
has no individual differences in it at all, so the group rate here conceals the
fact that a rate of a third is compatible with most participants never agreeing
once and a minority agreeing almost always. The longer version simulates that
distribution.

## Accessibility considerations

Conditions are ordinary buttons, and one already run is marked with
`aria-disabled` rather than the `disabled` attribute so it stays reachable.
Every figure is in a real table with headers as well as in the chart, and each
bar is labelled with its value and with the name of its route in words. The
chart description lists every condition run with both routes and its total.

## Optional extension

Ask students to design a meeting procedure that reduces normative influence
without reducing informational influence, and then to say when they would not
want to. Committees that suppress both end up worse informed, which is the
trade the diagnostic comparison sets up.

## Evidence and citation notes

On the two routes see Deutsch and Gerard (1955), *A study of normative and
informational social influences upon individual judgment*, **Journal of
Abnormal and Social Psychology**, 51(3), 629–636. On the ally effect and
unanimity as a step see Asch (1955), *Opinions and social pressure*,
**Scientific American**, 193(5), 31–35. On variation across time and place see
Bond and Smith (1996), *Culture and conformity*, **Psychological Bulletin**,
119(1), 111–137. On identification as the missing term see Turner (1991),
**Social Influence**, Open University Press.
