Who Gets to Define the Story?

The same situation can support several accounts. But those accounts do not always have equal power.

Step 1

Aisha stops attending a weekly seminar

Move through three accounts of the same situation. Notice not only what each account says, but what it could do.

Aisha has missed four seminars. She continues to submit work and regularly uses the online materials.

Aisha

“I’m working late shifts most weeks. I keep up with the materials, but sometimes I genuinely cannot get there.”

Peers

“She’s hardly ever there now. It feels like she isn’t really part of the group anymore.”

Attendance record

“Persistent non-attendance: student engagement concern.”

Compare

Different accounts, unequal authority

All three descriptions refer to the same attendance pattern, but they construct Aisha differently, invite different responses, and do not have equal institutional weight.

Key idea: Critical approaches to identity ask how people are positioned through language, whose account is treated as authoritative, and how those descriptions can shape responses, opportunities and constraints that may then reinforce the original identity.
